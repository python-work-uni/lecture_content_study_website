Issues and Open Questions
- AI is bound to replace routine human mental labour but will it replace human ingenuity?
- Where is the labour market headed in the age of AI?
- Is AI growth going to deplete natural resources (energy and water) and will the gains arising 
from AI be worth the environmental costs of mass AI?
- Are the AI pessimists wrongly assuming that energy efficiency will not improve in the 
future?
- As AI gets smarter and is used more routinely, are humans destined to become “dumber”?

- If AI is used in critical, even life-and-death, scenarios, who is responsible if something goes 
wrong?
- How does society protect vulnerable people from AI outputs generated for nefarious 
purposes?
- How do we draw the line between productive use of AI and unethical or illegal use of AI?
- Is it ever right to claim AI outputs as our own work? What if AI produces a first cut and 
we carefully review and revise it?

The University of Sydney
Good Practices When Using AI
- Be ready to think and challenge. To do this, you need to have an opinion and a goal.
- Plan on having a conversation, not a one-off search query.
- Verify information provided by AI – first ask for sources, then read them.
- AI hallucination continues to be a thing.
- Give prompts that contain detail, e.g. role playing (“You are a patient advisor and I am 
seeking your help on study habits”), your own understanding of the problem (“I think 
that the variable t in signals and systems always refers to continuous time. Am I right?”), 
precise descriptions (“I need a Python function that replaces all instances of positive 
values in the attached Excel with +”).
- The more context you can give AI, the better its response
- In a long conversation, remember that AI’s context window is limited. Ask it to summarize 
the earlier conversation so that the context is refreshed.

The University of Sydney
Definition of artificial intelligence (?)
Intelligence = ability to take actions that lead to desirable outcomes with an adequately high 
probability in an environment that frequently results in a state of nature not previously 
encountered
This means the following systems are examples of artificial intelligence:
- Automatic cruise control in a car – able to regulate speed on any road under any conditions
- Noise cancelling headphones – works to some acceptable degree with any noise and any audio 
signal
- Power system – provides required power to an uncertain number of consumers at any time
- Google Maps – able to find a route from any place to any place at any time and estimate travel 
time
- Social media recommendations – able to suggest people/places/etc. to check out based on 
individual data
- ChatGPT – able to respond with near-human (or super-human) intelligence to any question on any 
topic

The University of Sydney
What Does AI Mean Today?
- At its most basic, an AI is nothing more than the implementation of a mapping from a set of inputs 
to one or more outputs
- Human intelligence is also the same – in response to our sensory inputs, we produce 
thoughts, words, muscle activations, etc.
- AI, therefore, encompasses many of the “old” or “traditional” engineered systems, which can be 
construed to be making an automated decision, by whatever means.
- But this definition is a bit too broad and AI currently refers only to computational methods for 
arriving at these decisions.
- Even this narrower definition is quite broad, since it includes all programmed systems such as 
if/then/elseif rules-based systems.
- A lot of what we mean by AI these days is really Machine Learning (ML)
- ML refers to a computational machine that ingests data and learns a model that links input 
data (features) to output data (labels). This is what we focus on in ENGG2112.

The University of Sydney
Keys to the Rapid Development of ML
1. Acceptance That Explanation is Not Always Necessary
Engineers and scientists traditionally sought to understand model functionality.
ML shows that sometimes, we only need to know it works reliably, even without full explanations.
Example: In smartphones, users care only that facial recognition works accurately, not how it does.
2. Advances in Algorithms and Computational Power
In the 1990s, neural networks and deep learning were possible but computationally impractical.
Today, GPUs and TPUs enable massive parallel computing, making complex models trainable in 
hours. For example, image recognition that once needed supercomputers can now be done on a 
standard laptop.
3. Explosion of Data Availability
ML models need large datasets to train effectively.
In the 90s, data collection was limited, but today, IoT, smartphones, and online platforms generate 
vast amounts of data. Example: Self-driving cars—modern ML models continuously improve as they 
process millions of miles of real-world driving data.

SLIDE 26

The University of Sydney
Traditional Methods Still Matter - Shouldn’t Discard Them
1. If a Traditional Model Works, Keep It
Many engineering problems have established analytical models that are efficient and reliable.
ML should not replace these methods unless it offers a clear advantage. For example, in structural 
engineering, finite element analysis (FEA) is understood and reliable, so ML may not provide 
significant value.
2. Engineers Must Still Learn Traditional Methods
Many ML models are black boxes, meaning they lack the interpretability of traditional models.
Understanding fundamental engineering principles is crucial for verifying ML outputs.
Example: ML can predict turbulence patterns in fluid mechanics, but engineers still need to 
understand Navier-Stokes equations to validate results.
3. Hybrid Approaches Are the Future
The best engineering solutions often combine traditional models with ML.
Example: Predictive maintenance in aviation—traditional physics-based failure models combined with 
ML anomaly detection create more accurate and reliable predictions.
