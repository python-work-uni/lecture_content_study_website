Multi-Disciplinary Engineering: Your First Steps into AI

This week sets the stage for the whole unit: who teaches it, how lectures, tutorials and teams work, exactly how you are assessed, and why the course now focuses on the socio-technical side of AI. It also gives you a working definition of AI and machine learning and the three-phase pipeline behind almost every AI system you will ever meet.

Read this first: Hold onto one mental model — AI is a toolbox, and machine learning is one of its power tools. A machine learning system does just three things: it defines a model, trains it on data to find good parameters, then deploys it to handle inputs it has never seen. Everything else this week — the assessment rules, the team project, the ethics debates, the good-practice habits — is about learning to pick and use that tool wisely.

The Big Picture

One unit, one big idea: engineers need to combine human judgement with AI, and this course is where you start learning how.

ENGG2112 Multi-Disciplinary Engineering is a core unit for every stream of the Bachelor of Engineering — chemical, biomedical, civil, electrical, software and more. That mix is deliberate. In your career you will work with people who think in completely different ways, so the unit puts you in teams that span more than one engineering discipline.

The unit has changed its focus this semester. Older versions taught the technical side of AI/ML first, then had you apply it in a project. Attendance Rules

Attendance is a hurdle: clear it or fail, no matter how good your marks are.

A hurdle requirement is a condition you must satisfy to pass the unit at all, independent of your marks. Attendance is one.

The hurdle
compulsory attendance ≥ 80% of tutorials
  • You must attend at least 80% of tutorials.
  • Missing up to two tutorials needs no Special Consideration (SC) — no questions asked.
  • Missing three or more tutorials without SC gives an Absent Fail (AF), regardless of the marks you accumulated over the semester.
  • Attendance is taken twice — at the start and somewhere in the middle-to-end of the tutorial — so leaving after the first roll-call does not count.
  • Lecture attendance is not compulsory but strongly encouraged; if you miss a lecture in person, at least watch the recording.
⚠️
Common trap: two misses are forgiven; the third without SC is fatal. Treat 80% as a hard line, not a number to negotiate.

AI as a Toolbox

AI is not a replacement for your engineering fundamentals — it is one more tool, and a good engineer knows when to reach for it.

The unit frames AI and ML as a toolbox that humanity has created. You need to learn to use those tools both for learning and for engineering — solving problems that are valuable to society.

  • New this semester: teaching you to use generative AI in productive and safe ways.
  • Both AI for learning and AI for engineering.
  • The central question of the age: how should humans interact with AI?
  • You learn by using AI on new topics — both seen and unseen in lectures — then using it in your project.
  • The team project needs your uniquely human creativity and sense of purpose, connecting technical solutions to real problems.
ℹ️
Using AI does not mean abandoning the fundamentals. Use AI where it is appropriate, and use knowledge that has been around since the Renaissance where that is appropriate. You need both, and you must choose the right tool for the job.

The technical underpinnings of AI are covered from Week 3. Powerful parallel computing hardware enabled computational learning methods based on theory or heuristics. The question the unit keeps asking is: how do we make use of them intelligently?

Good Practices When Using AI

AI speeds up learning, but it does not make learning easy — and passive use teaches you almost nothing.

Before AI, a big part of the difficulty of learning was simply finding the right material. AI removes that searching step, but you still have to absorb and understand what it hands you. That is the key nuance: AI can accelerate learning, but it does not decrease the difficulty of learning.

The habits that work

  • Be ready to think and challenge. Enter the conversation with an opinion and a goal — know what you want out of it.
  • Plan on having a conversation, not a one-off search query. Direct the thinking in two directions rather than letting the AI lead.
  • Verify the information. First ask for sources, then actually read them. AI hallucination — confidently stating something false — continues to be a real thing.
  • Give detailed prompts. The more context you supply, the better the response.
  • In a long conversation, remember the context window is limited. Ask the AI to summarise the earlier conversation so the context is refreshed.

Three kinds of detail to put in a prompt

role play

"You are a patient advisor and I am seeking your help on study habits."

your understanding

"I think that the variable t in signals and systems always refers to continuous time. Am I right?"

precise spec

"I need a Python function that replaces all instances of positive values in the attached Excel with +."

⚠️
Common trap: if the AI asks "would you like me to do X?" and you simply answer "yes" again and again, the AI is directing your thinking, not you. That passive pattern is how people use AI for hours and learn nothing.
ℹ️
Live demo: the lecturer demonstrated a Claude conversation about transformer models, deliberately challenging the AI's claims and verifying specific mathematical details rather than accepting the output passively.

Issues and Open Questions

The lecture leaves these deliberately open. They are the debates you will revisit all semester — and some of them are project-worthy.

These are not settled facts to memorise; they are the questions that make AI a socio-technical subject. Notice how many of them are about responsibility, fairness and human agency rather than code.

  • AI is bound to replace routine human mental labour (data entry and the like), but will it replace human ingenuity?
  • Where is the labour market headed in the age of AI?
  • Will AI growth deplete natural resources (energy and water), and will the gains be worth the environmental costs of mass AI?
  • Are the AI pessimists wrongly assuming that energy efficiency will not improve in future?
  • As AI gets smarter and more routine, are humans destined to become "dumber" — and can we arrest that trend?
  • If AI is used in critical, even life-and-death, scenarios, who is responsible if something goes wrong?
  • How does society protect vulnerable people from AI outputs generated for nefarious purposes — such as a convincing fake of a real person's likeness?
  • How do we draw the line between productive use of AI and unethical or illegal use?
  • Is it ever right to claim AI outputs as our own work? What if AI produces a first cut and we carefully review and revise it?
💡
Why it matters: how humans interact with AI matters a lot. We humans have the agency to ensure that AI interactions benefit us.

What Is AI?

Start with intelligence itself, then notice that a surprising amount of ordinary engineering already counts as AI.

Defining intelligence

The lecture's working definition is deliberately broad and probabilistic:

Intelligence
Intelligence = ability to take actions that lead to desirable outcomes with an adequately high probability, in an environment that frequently results in a state of nature not previously encountered

Read that slowly. The hard part is the last clause: the system keeps meeting situations it has never seen before. If it can still act well, we call it intelligent. That is why these ordinary systems are examples of artificial intelligence:

🚗 Automatic cruise control

Regulates speed on any road under any conditions.

🎧 Noise-cancelling headphones

Work to some acceptable degree with any noise and any audio signal.

🔌 Power system

Provides the required power to an uncertain number of consumers at any time.

🗺️ Google Maps

Finds a route from any place to any place at any time and estimates travel time.

📱 Social media recommendations

Suggests people and places to check out based on individual data.

💬 ChatGPT

Responds with near-human (or super-human) intelligence to any question on any topic.

What AI means today

At its most basic, an AI is nothing more than the implementation of a mapping from a set of inputs to one or more outputs. Human intelligence is the same shape: in response to our sensory inputs we produce thoughts, words, muscle activations and so on.

That definition is too broad, though — it would include every old engineered system that makes an automated decision, and even all if/then/elseif rules-based programs. So AI today means computational methods for arriving at those decisions. Even that is broad, so in practice a lot of what we mean by AI these days is really Machine Learning (ML).

Machine Learning (ML) — a computational machine that ingests data and learns a model linking input data (features) to output data (labels). This is what ENGG2112 focuses on.

Today's AI has the same input and output; the conceptual difference is that the "black box" in the middle is nonlinear and has a huge number of weights, not just a few like W0, W1, W2.

The ML Pipeline: Model and Training

Almost all machine learning runs in three phases: define a model, train it, deploy it. Here are the first two.

Define model→ Train (find θ)→ Deploy

Phase 1 — the model

  • First, define a model that links inputs to output(s).
  • The model has a set of parameters, denoted theta (θ) — the W0, W1, W2 of the simple linear case.
  • The critical task of ML is to find the best values for θ using the available data.
  • This is accomplished in the training phase.
The model, in symbols
y = f_θ(x)

Here x is the input, y is the output, and θ are the parameters that training must discover. The lecture's examples of this mapping:

  • C3PO: optical signals into its eyes → a recognised object (R2D2, Luke).
  • Self-driving car: sensors around the car → steering, brake and accelerator signals.
  • Google Home: audio signals → a recognised command.
  • Robot: sensors on the robot → signals to each leg to control movement.

Phase 2 — training / learning

Training is where the "learning" happens. If you know both the inputs and the outputs for many examples — say 10,000 input–output pairs — can you discover the best value of θ? The answer depends on what kind of learning you are doing, which is the next section.

🤔
Pause & predict: In the equation y = f_θ(x), which symbol is the thing the machine learns, and which two are given to it? (Answer: θ is learned; x and y are given during training.)

Three Ways Machines Learn

The difference comes down to one question: do we know the correct outputs for our training data?

🏷️ Supervised learning

We use known inputs and known outputs to find θ. The data is labelled, so the model is told the right answer.

🧩 Unsupervised learning

The outputs are unknown, so θ cannot be found directly. Instead the objective is to discover patterns in the data, such as clusters.

🎯 Reinforcement learning

Relies on feedback from the environment in response to an action — for example a scoring function. It takes random steps at first, then converges on good versus bad strategies.

TypeInputsOutputsGoal
SupervisedKnownKnownFind θ from labelled examples
UnsupervisedKnownUnknownDiscover patterns / clusters
ReinforcementActionsReward feedbackLearn a good strategy

Reinforcement learning is important but a little more complex. You define a reward: when the machine takes an action, you must be able to measure a reward value. A high reward means the action was good; a low reward means it was bad. Over training, the machine converges on the strategy that earns the best rewards.

S · U · R
"Known in, known out / find the pattern / learn from reward"
Supervised (both known) · Unsupervised (outputs unknown, find patterns) · Reinforcement (reward feedback).

Deployment and Generalisation

Training is only useful if the model then works on inputs it has never seen. That is the whole point of AI.

After training you have the "best" setting for θ, and you are ready to deploy the model — put it into production. This stage is also called generalisation: the trained model must handle new inputs it did not see during training.

inputs (new)→ Model y = f_θ(x)→ outputs

🚗 Self-driving car

A human driver trains it. After training it should be ready to drive itself.

🤖 C3PO

Shown many pictures of R2D2 and Luke for training. After training he can tell them apart.

🔊 Google Home

Trained on many example phrases spoken by different people. After training it recognises speech from anyone.

Worked example: face unlock on your phone

  1. Model: maps an image of your face to "authorised" or "not authorised" — a function y = f_θ(x).
  2. Train: when you set up the phone you take many photos. These known input–output pairs find good values of θ.
  3. Deploy: next time you unlock it, the phone sees a picture it has never seen in training and still gets the right answer — that is generalisation.
⚠️
Common trap: a model that cannot handle unseen data is useless. For data it has seen, we already know the output — so there would be no point using the model at all. Generalisation is the entire reason we build these systems.

Why ML Took Off — and Its Limits

Three shifts made modern AI possible; but traditional engineering methods still matter, and the best answers are often hybrids.

Three keys to ML's rapid development

  1. Acceptance that explanation is not always necessary. Engineers and scientists traditionally sought to understand how a model works. ML shows that sometimes we only need to know it works reliably, even without a full explanation. Example: with smartphone facial recognition, users care only that it is accurate, not how it works.
  2. Advances in algorithms and computational power. In the 1990s neural networks and deep learning were possible but computationally impractical. Today GPUs and TPUs enable massive parallel computing, making complex models trainable in hours. Example: image recognition that once needed supercomputers can now run on a standard laptop.
  3. Explosion of data availability. ML models need large datasets. In the 90s data collection was limited; today the IoT, smartphones and online platforms generate vast amounts. Example: self-driving cars keep improving as they process millions of miles of real-world driving data.
A · C · E
"Acceptance, Computing, Explosion"
Acceptance that explanation is not always necessary · Computational power and better algorithms · Explosion of data.

Traditional methods still matter — don't discard them

  1. If a traditional model works, keep it. Many engineering problems have established analytical models that are efficient and reliable; ML should not replace them unless it offers a clear advantage. Example: in structural engineering, finite element analysis (FEA) is understood and reliable, so ML may add little value.
  2. Engineers must still learn traditional methods. Many ML models are black boxes — they lack the interpretability of traditional models. Understanding fundamental principles is crucial for verifying ML outputs. Example: ML can predict turbulence patterns in fluid mechanics, but engineers still need the Navier–Stokes equations to validate the results.
  3. Hybrid approaches are the future. The best solutions often combine traditional models with ML. Example: predictive maintenance in aviation — physics-based failure models combined with ML anomaly detection give more accurate, reliable predictions.
ℹ️
Scale check: when the lecturer did a PhD, systems had roughly 10–20 parameters and simulated overnight on MATLAB. Today we train models with hundreds of billions of parameters. That leap in scale is the main difference between old and new AI.

Project Examples and Pitfalls

Good projects pair a technical question with the wider human questions — and a "negative example" is one that ignores why the work exists.

Three worked themes

ThemeTechnical questionWider questions
Drones for surveillance and tracking How can multiple drones improve performance over a single drone? What are the cost–benefit trade-offs? What are the consequences of identification errors? Are they ethically acceptable? Are there legal implications?
Cybersecurity Can AI operating on network parameters detect the spread of malware and viruses so it can be stopped early? Who operates and owns the AI? How would the spread be stopped? What are the costs of stopping it?
Detecting driver fatigue Using onboard cameras and other sensors, can AI detect the early onset of driver fatigue? Will it increase cost substantially? Can we quantify lives saved? What level of false alarm and missed detections is acceptable?

Negative examples — when NOT to use AI/ML

  • Predicting a stock market index from the past, with no discussion of how the prediction will be used or what inaccurate predictions cost.
  • Classifying water as potable or not from chemical readings — do we even need ML or AI for this?
  • Predicting a bushfire at a location from current weather and known fires — why would AI/ML work better than conventional methods?
  • Diagnosing diabetes from non-invasive vital signs — the accuracy is low relative to a simple blood test.
⚠️
Common trap: a technically clever project that ignores why it exists, how it will be used, and what errors cost is a negative example. Couple your method to a real problem, a use case and an error cost.

Questions from the floor

💻 Will the final exam involve Python?

Yes — some programming, because you will program ML. Python is expected and you have already learned it.

🔄 Can I attend a different tutorial?

No — you must be with your group. A rare once-or-twice swap may be tolerated, but do not make it a habit.

🤖 Could AI replace the lecturer?

No. AI learns from data; humans learn from experience. AI is "very cold and lifeless"; we bring quirks, personality and individuality. (Prof. Maher recommended the book What Are People for? on this question.)

⚖️ How long until legal regulation catches up?

Anyone's guess. AI is advancing faster than legal regulation can catch up, and it depends on the country.

🆚 How are AIs different, e.g. Gemini vs Claude?

Much is commercial-in-confidence, but they share the same transformer algorithm with an attention mechanism and the same pre-training approach. They differ in training data, company, safety and security levels, and pricing. They also use the same supervised/unsupervised/reinforcement principles — different data, different safety.

📈 Is AI creating or taking opportunities?

It depends on how you act. The opportunities exist for you, but you must demonstrate your value as a human. If you only do what AI can do, there is no value — the company would just use AI.

✍️ Can I use AI in the exam?

No — it is a written exam. The university has a two-lane policy: you must demonstrate what you know without AI for a certain percentage of your grade, even when the subject is AI itself.

🎨 What about AI built on human work taken without permission?

Regulation and law have not caught up, so AI companies are not doing anything illegal — though many consider it unethical. The fair use principle is often cited. (Prof. Maher noted the Prime Minister recently visited the University of Sydney to discuss AI taking artists' work without compensation.) This course uses ML for engineering projects for social good, not artists' work; raise any ethical concern with the teaching team.

🫧 When does the AI "bubble" pop?

Prefer the term scaling curve, not "bubble". The debate is whether more data keeps making models smarter without limit, or whether we hit an asymptote and need a new paradigm. The transformer algorithm and attention mechanism will work to a point we have not reached yet.

🧰 Technical skills: old vs new ENGG2112?

In the old version, one or two people in a group had the skills and the rest sat around. Now everyone picks up some technical skill because you are asked about it individually. You are not expected to be an ML expert programmer — but you should be able to specify requirements for AI to write a proper program.

👨‍🏫 How has AI changed the lecturers' jobs?

Prof. Lim only began using AI seriously in the last few months to a year. He uses it to learn about all kinds of things, always reading the original source where possible.

👥 Must we be in the same tutorial to be a group?

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Intelligence
The ability to take actions leading to desirable outcomes with adequately high probability in an environment that frequently presents a state of nature not previously encountered. It is about coping with the unseen.
Artificial intelligence (AI)
At its most basic, the implementation of a mapping from a set of inputs to one or more outputs. Today it means the computational methods used to arrive at those decisions.
Machine learning (ML)
A computational machine that ingests data and learns a model linking input data (features) to output data (labels). This is the focus of ENGG2112.
Features
The input data fed into a machine-learning model — the x in y = f_θ(x).
Labels
The output data a machine-learning model predicts — the y in y = f_θ(x).
Model
A mathematical mapping y = f_θ(x) that links inputs to outputs; its adjustable values are the parameters θ.
Parameters (θ)
The adjustable values a model learns during training. In simple linear models these are weights like W0, W1, W2; modern models have hundreds of billions.
Generalisation
A trained model's ability to perform correctly on new inputs it did not see during training. Without it, the model is useless.
Generative AI
AI that produces new content or responses, such as ChatGPT or Claude. The unit's new focus is on using it productively and safely.
Socio-technical
An approach that treats technology and its social, ethical and human context as inseparable. It is the lens of the new ENGG2112.
AI for Good
The project theme of using AI to benefit society, humanity and the economy — pairing a technical solution with its wider impact.
Black box
A model whose internal workings lack interpretability, making its outputs hard to verify. A key reason engineers still need traditional fundamentals.
Transformer
The algorithm with an attention mechanism underlying models such as Gemini and Claude. They share it, but differ in data, safety and pricing.
Hallucination
When an AI confidently states information that is wrong. It is why you should ask for sources and read them.
Context window
The limited amount of prior conversation an AI can hold at once. In a long chat, ask it to summarise earlier turns.
Hurdle requirement
A condition, such as 80% tutorial attendance, that must be met to pass regardless of the marks you accumulate.
Absent Fail (AF)
The result of missing three or more tutorials without Special Consideration — it overrides any marks earned.
Supervised learning
Learning where both the inputs and the outputs of the training data are known, allowing the model to find θ from labelled examples.
Unsupervised learning
Learning where the outputs are unknown; instead the goal is to discover patterns, such as clusters, in the data.
Reinforcement learning
Learning from feedback or reward in response to actions taken in an environment; the machine explores randomly at first, then converges on a good strategy.
Training
The phase where known data is used to find the best parameter values θ. It is one of the three pipeline phases.
System identification
The traditional engineering practice of fitting a linear model with unknown parameters to observed data — an early form of the input–output thinking behind AI.
False alarm
An incorrect positive detection — the system says something happened when it did not. A key trade-off in projects like driver-fatigue detection.
Missed detection
A failure to detect something that did happen. Together with false alarms, it defines what error rate a real application can accept.

No terms in this category.

Q1

In your own words, define intelligence as the lecture does, then explain why automatic cruise control qualifies as an example of AI.

Answer: Intelligence is the ability to take actions leading to desirable outcomes with adequately high probability in an environment that frequently presents a state of nature not previously encountered. Cruise control qualifies because it can regulate speed on a road it has never travelled, under conditions it has not seen — it acts well in the face of the unseen.
Q2

Why is the broad definition "an AI maps inputs to outputs" narrowed today, and what does AI usually mean in practice?

Answer: That definition would include every old engineered system that makes an automated decision and even all rules-based if/then/elseif programs. So AI today means computational methods for arriving at those decisions. Even that is broad, and much of what we mean by AI is really Machine Learning — a machine that ingests data and learns a model linking features to labels.
Q3

Write the machine-learning model in symbols and say what each symbol means and which part is learned.

Answer: y = f_θ(x). x is the input (features), y is the output (label), and θ are the parameters. The critical task of ML is to find the best θ from available data, which happens in the training phase.
Q4

Contrast supervised and unsupervised learning. Give one situation where each fits.

Answer: Supervised learning uses known inputs and known outputs to find θ (e.g. training a self-driving car from a human driver's steering and braking). Unsupervised learning has unknown outputs, so θ cannot be found directly; instead it discovers patterns such as clusters (e.g. grouping unlabelled customers).
Q5

How does reinforcement learning find its parameters, and what is the role of the reward?

Answer: It relies on feedback from the environment in response to actions. A scoring or reward function measures how good each action was. The machine takes random steps at first to discover what is good and bad, then converges on a good strategy as training proceeds.
Q6

What is deployment, and why is generalisation the entire point of building a model?

Answer: Deployment is putting the trained model into production after training has found the best θ. It is also called generalisation: handling new inputs not seen during training. If a model could not handle unseen data, it would be useless, because for data it has already seen we already know the output.
Q7

List the three keys to the rapid development of ML and give the lecture's example for each.

Answer: (1) Acceptance that explanation is not always necessary — users care that facial recognition works, not how. (2) Advances in algorithms and computational power — GPUs and TPUs train complex models in hours; image recognition moved from supercomputers to laptops. (3) Explosion of data — IoT, smartphones and online platforms supply huge datasets; self-driving cars learn from millions of miles.
Q8

Why do traditional engineering methods still matter? Support your answer with the finite element analysis and Navier–Stokes examples.

Answer: If a traditional model works, keep it — ML should only replace it with a clear advantage, and FEA in structural engineering is already efficient and reliable. Engineers must also learn fundamentals because ML models are black boxes, so you need principles to verify outputs — for example Navier–Stokes equations to validate ML turbulence predictions. The best solutions are hybrids, such as physics-based failure models combined with ML anomaly detection in aviation predictive maintenance.
Q9

Explain the attendance hurdle precisely: how many tutorials can you miss without Special Consideration, and what happens on the next miss?

Answer: You must attend at least 80% of tutorials. You may miss up to two without Special Consideration. If you miss three or more without SC, you receive an Absent Fail regardless of the marks accumulated over the semester. Attendance is taken twice per tutorial, so leaving early does not count.
Q10

Break down the assessment weightings: which items are open, which are secure, and what are the two totals?

Answer: Open items: transcript #1 (5%), transcript #2 (5%), proposal (10%), final report (15%) = 35%. Secure items: explain learnt topic (5%), Q&A/oral exam (20%), final exam (40%) = 65%. The three individual tasks total 15% (formative). Tutorial attendance is a hurdle.
Q11

A student proposes predicting a stock-market index from past prices "because AI is powerful". Using the lecture's negative examples, explain what is wrong with this framing.

Answer: It is a negative example because it ignores why the prediction exists, how it would be used, and what inaccurate predictions cost. The lecture's test is to couple a method to a real problem, a use case and an error cost. The same failure appears in classifying potable water (do we even need ML?), predicting bushfire from weather (why beat conventional methods?), and non-invasive diabetes diagnosis (less accurate than a blood test).
Q12

Give three habits that make AI use productive, and explain the trap of answering "yes" to every follow-up the AI offers.

Answer: Habits include: come with an opinion and a goal and be ready to challenge; treat it as a conversation, not a one-off search; verify information by asking for sources and reading them; give detailed, context-rich prompts; and ask it to summarise a long conversation because the context window is limited. The trap is that if you just answer "yes" to each offer, the AI directs your thinking instead of you directing the conversation — you learn little. Note too that AI accelerates learning but does not reduce its difficulty.
