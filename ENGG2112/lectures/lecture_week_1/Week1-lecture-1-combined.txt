The University of Sydney
Good Practices When Using AI
- Be ready to think and challenge. To do this, you need to have an opinion and a goal.
- Plan on having a conversation, not a one-off search query.
- Verify information provided by AI - first ask for sources, then read them.
- AI hallucination continues to be a thing.
- Give prompts that contain detail, e.g. role playing ("You are a patient advisor and I am seeking your help on study habits"), your own understanding of the problem ("I think that the variable t in signals and systems always refers to continuous time. Am I right?"), precise descriptions ("I need a Python function that replaces all instances of positive values in the attached Excel with +").
- The more context you can give AI, the better its response.
- In a long conversation, remember that AI's context window is limited. Ask it to summarize the earlier conversation so that the context is refreshed.

[TRANSCRIPT]
SPEAKER 0: Now let's dive into the good practices when using AI. We were just looking at an AI transcript from some students and it was very interesting what happened. The transcript basically showed: a student was asking about something, AI answered, tagged on a question at the end - "Would you like me to do whatever?" - and then the student says "yes" and it carries on. And it just carries on and carries on. But what is happening there is that you are letting the AI direct your thinking rather than helping to direct the thinking in a two-way conversation.

So the first thing is you have to be ready to think and to challenge. You have to enter into this conversation with some kind of idea of what you want to get out of it. You cannot just say "tell me about signals" and then it says something and says "would you like me to show you whatever?" and you say yes. You are not going to learn anything that way.

It is a conversation, not a one-off search query. You need to sometimes verify information provided by AI because sometimes it can be wrong. Give it prompts that contain detail - for example, roleplay: "You are a teacher and I am a first-year undergraduate student, I know nothing about whatever but I do know something about whatever, teach me about something." Be prepared to ask a detailed question with a lot of information that will help AI tailor its reply to you.

It is also a good habit to give it your own understanding. After spending 15 minutes asking about some complicated topic, summarise the information you have got and tell it: "This is what I understand now. Is this correct?" And the more context you give to AI, the better its response. When you have a very long conversation, its context window can be limited - ask it to summarise the earlier conversation so that it does not forget.

The University of Sydney
What Does AI Mean Today?
- At its most basic, an AI is nothing more than the implementation of a mapping from a set of inputs to one or more outputs
- Human intelligence is also the same - in response to our sensory inputs, we produce thoughts, words, muscle activations, etc.
- AI, therefore, encompasses many of the "old" or "traditional" engineered systems, which can be construed to be making an automated decision, by whatever means.
- But this definition is a bit too broad and AI currently refers only to computational methods for arriving at these decisions.
- Even this narrower definition is quite broad, since it includes all programmed systems such as if/then/elseif rules-based systems.
- A lot of what we mean by AI these days is really Machine Learning (ML)
- ML refers to a computational machine that ingests data and learns a model that links input data (features) to output data (labels). This is what we focus on in ENGG2112.

[TRANSCRIPT]
SPEAKER 0: At its most basic, an AI is nothing more than the implementation of a mapping from a set of inputs to one or more outputs. Let us come back to the phone example - the input is your face, and the output is: yes it is an authorised user, or no it is not. And then you go to ChatGPT, which is an LLM - the inputs would be the entire conversation up to now and the output would be the next word.

Engineers have been interested in input-output systems since the beginning. Those of you in electrical engineering, mechanical, or aerospace might have done signals and systems. That is the traditional way of modelling a system. You model it using a linear system with unknown parameters. This is known in the old days as system identification. This can be considered an early form of AI.

What is the difference between that and today's incredibly complex AI? There is still an input, there is still an output. It is just that the black box in the middle is not just a few weights W0, W1, W2. There are a very large number of weights, and the system is nonlinear. That is conceptually the only difference between the systems we have already played with for a very long time and AI systems today.

Abstract view of Machine Learning: Model
- First, define a model linking inputs to output(s)
- This model has a set of parameters denoted theta
- The critical task of ML is to find the best values for the parameters theta using available data
- This is accomplished in the training phase

inputs -> Model [y = f_theta(x)] -> outputs

[TRANSCRIPT]

The job of ML or AI is, through training: if I know both the inputs and the outputs for many examples, let us say 10,000 input-output pairs, can I discover the best value of theta? The University of Sydney
Abstract view of machine learning: Training/learning
- In the training phase, we use known inputs and outputs (supervised learning) to find theta.
- In unsupervised learning, the outputs are not known and therefore theta cannot be found. Instead, objective is to discover patterns in the data.
- There is a third class of learning called reinforcement learning which relies on feedback from the environment in response to an action taken by the machine to discover the model parameters. E.g. a scoring function

Supervised Learning: inputs (known) -> Model -> outputs (known)
Unsupervised Learning: inputs (known) -> Model -> outputs (unknown)

[TRANSCRIPT]
SPEAKER 0: Of course, there are different kinds of machine learning. In supervised learning, you know both the input and the output for your training samples. In unsupervised learning, all you are trying to do is figure out the clusters of data points. You do not know exactly what these clusters represent, but you take this data and try to cluster them and draw boundaries between them. You are learning a pattern, trying to encapsulate that pattern in this function F_theta of X.

And there is reinforcement learning, which is very important but a bit complex. You define a reward. If this machine takes an action, you must be able to measure some reward value. If the reward is high, the action is good; if the reward is bad, the action is bad. It takes random steps at first to figure out what is good and what is bad, and then as it goes along in training, it eventually converges and figures out what is a good strategy versus a bad strategy.

The University of Sydney
Keys to the Rapid Development of ML
1. Acceptance That Explanation is Not Always Necessary
   Engineers and scientists traditionally sought to understand model functionality.
   ML shows that sometimes, we only need to know it works reliably, even without full explanations.
   Example: In smartphones, users care only that facial recognition works accurately, not how it does.

2. Advances in Algorithms and Computational Power
   In the 1990s, neural networks and deep learning were possible but computationally impractical.
   Today, GPUs and TPUs enable massive parallel computing, making complex models trainable in hours.
   Example: Image recognition that once needed supercomputers can now be done on a standard laptop.

3. Explosion of Data Availability
   ML models need large datasets to train effectively.
   In the 90s, data collection was limited, but today, IoT, smartphones, and online platforms generate vast amounts of data.
   Example: Self-driving cars - modern ML models continuously improve as they process millions of miles of real-world driving data.

The University of Sydney
Traditional Methods Still Matter - Shouldn't Discard Them
1. If a Traditional Model Works, Keep It
   Many engineering problems have established analytical models that are efficient and reliable.
   ML should not replace these methods unless it offers a clear advantage.
   Example: In structural engineering, finite element analysis (FEA) is understood and reliable, so ML may not provide significant value.

2. Engineers Must Still Learn Traditional Methods
   Many ML models are black boxes, meaning they lack the interpretability of traditional models.
   Understanding fundamental engineering principles is crucial for verifying ML outputs.
   Example: ML can predict turbulence patterns in fluid mechanics, but engineers still need to understand Navier-Stokes equations to validate results.

3. Hybrid Approaches Are the Future
   The best engineering solutions often combine traditional models with ML.
   Example: Predictive maintenance in aviation - traditional physics-based failure models combined with ML anomaly detection create more accurate and reliable predictions.

Right now we have 20 billion weights and we are able to train them. That is the main difference.
