SECTION 9: Good Practices When Using AI
------------------------------------------------
AI speeds up learning but does **not** reduce its difficulty. Before AI, a big part of the difficulty was *finding* material; AI removes that part, but you still must absorb and understand it.

- **Be ready to think and challenge** — come with an opinion and a goal.
- Plan a **conversation**, not a one-off search query.
- **Verify** AI's information: ask for sources, then read them.
- Remember **hallucination** is still a thing.
- **Give detailed prompts** — for example role play ("You are a patient advisor helping me with study habits"), your own understanding ("I think t always means continuous time in signals and systems — right?"), or precise specs ("I need a Python function that replaces positive values in this Excel with +").
- **More context gives a better response.**
- In a long chat the **context window is limited**; ask the AI to **summarise earlier conversation** so it is refreshed.

⚠️ **Common Trap:** If the AI asks "would you like me to do X?" and you just answer "yes", the **AI directs your thinking** instead of you directing the conversation — passive use teaches you little.

ℹ️ **Note:** In the live demo the lecturer used Claude on transformer models, **challenging claims and verifying mathematical details** rather than accepting output passively.

SECTION 10: What Is AI and ML
------------------------------------------------
### Defining intelligence
**Intelligence** is the ability to take actions leading to desirable outcomes with adequately high probability in an environment that frequently presents a **state of nature not previously encountered**.

So these ordinary systems are examples of AI:
- **Cruise control** — regulates speed on any road, in any conditions.
- **Noise-cancelling headphones** — work acceptably with any noise and audio.
- **Power systems** — supply an uncertain number of consumers at any time.
- **Google Maps** — routes from any place to any place and estimates travel time.
- **Social media recommendations** — suggest people/places from individual data.
- **ChatGPT** — responds with near-human (or super-human) intelligence to any question.

### What AI means today
At its most basic, **AI is the implementation of a mapping from inputs to one or more outputs**; human intelligence is the same (sensory inputs produce thoughts, words, muscle activations). This broad definition would include old engineered systems and rules-based `if/then/elseif` programs, so today AI means **computational methods** for those decisions — and much of it is really **Machine Learning (ML)**.

**Machine Learning (ML):** a computational machine that **ingests data and learns a model** linking **input data (features)** to **output data (labels)**.

| System | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Phone face unlock | Image of your face | Authorised / not authorised |
| ChatGPT (an LLM) | The whole conversation so far | The next word |
| C3PO | Optical signals into its eyes | Recognised object (R2D2, Luke) |

💡 **Why It Matters:** Engineers have built **input–output systems** for centuries (for example "system identification": fitting a linear model with unknown parameters). Today's AI has the same input and output — the only conceptual difference is that the "black box" has a **huge number of weights and is nonlinear**, not just a few weights like $W_0, W_1, W_2$.

SECTION 11: The ML Pipeline
------------------------------------------------
ML runs in three phases: **define a model, train it, deploy it.**

### 1. The model
- Define a **model** linking inputs to output(s).
- The model has **parameters**, denoted **theta** ($\theta$).
- The critical task of ML is to find the **best theta** using available data.

```
inputs → Model [y = f_theta(x)] → outputs
```

Lecture mappings: **C3PO** (optical signals → recognised object), **self-driving car** (sensors → steering/brake/accelerator), **Google Home** (audio → recognised command), **robot** (sensors → leg control signals).

### 2. Training / learning
- **Supervised:** known inputs and known outputs are used to find theta.
- **Unsupervised:** outputs are unknown, so instead the goal is to **discover patterns** (such as clusters).
- **Reinforcement:** learns from **feedback/reward** in response to actions; it starts with random steps and converges on good versus bad strategies.

| Type | Inputs | Outputs | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supervised | Known | Known | Find theta from labelled examples |
| Unsupervised | Known | Unknown | Discover patterns / clusters |
| Reinforcement | Actions | Reward feedback | Learn a good strategy |

### 3. Deployment (generalisation)
- After training we have the "best" theta and **deploy** the model.
- This is **generalisation**: handling **new inputs not seen in training**.
- Phone face unlock: setup photos train it; later a new photo still unlocks it. A self-driving car is trained by a human, then drives itself. Google Home is trained on many phrases, then understands anyone.

```
Define model → Collect data → Train theta → Deploy (generalise)
```

⚠️ **Common Trap:** A model that cannot handle **unseen** data is useless — for data it *has* seen, we already know the answer. Generalisation is the whole point.

SECTION 12: Why ML Took Off and Its Limits
------------------------------------------------
### Three keys to ML's rapid rise
1. **Acceptance that explanation is not always necessary** — ML can work reliably without a full explanation (users care that face recognition works, not how).
2. **Advances in algorithms and computing power** — neural networks were possible but impractical in the 1990s; today **GPUs and TPUs** train complex models in hours (image recognition moved from supercomputers to laptops).
3. **Explosion of data** — IoT, smartphones and online platforms now supply the large datasets ML needs (self-driving cars learn from millions of miles).

🧠 **Memory Hook:** **ACE** — **A**cceptance, **C**omputational power/algorithms, **E**xplosion of data.

### Traditional methods still matter
1. **If a traditional model works, keep it** — ML should not replace reliable analytical models without a clear advantage (for example **finite element analysis** in structural engineering).
2. **Engineers must still learn fundamentals** — ML models are **black boxes**, so principles are needed to verify outputs (for example **Navier–Stokes** equations to check turbulence predictions).
3. **Hybrid approaches are the future** — combining physics-based models with ML gives better results (for example predictive maintenance in aviation).

ℹ️ **Note:** Where the lecturer's PhD trained roughly 10–20 parameters overnight, we can now train models with many billions of weights.

SECTION 14: Project Examples and Q&A
------------------------------------------------
| Theme | Technical question | Wider questions |
|---|---|---|
| Drones for surveillance | Can multiple drones beat one drone? What are the cost–benefit trade-offs? | Consequences of identification errors? Ethically acceptable? Legal issues? |
| Cybersecurity | Can AI on network parameters detect spreading malware/viruses? | Who owns/operates the AI? How is the spread stopped? At what cost? |
| Detecting driver fatigue | Can onboard cameras/sensors detect early fatigue? | Cost? Quantify lives saved? Acceptable false-alarm and miss rates? |

### Negative examples — when NOT to use AI/ML
- Predicting a stock-market index with **no discussion of use or error cost**.
- Classifying water as potable — **do we even need ML?**
- Predicting bushfire from weather — **why beat conventional methods?**
- Diagnosing diabetes non-invasively — **less accurate than a blood test**.

⚠️ **Common Trap:** A technically clever project that ignores **why it exists, how it is used, and what errors cost** is a negative example.

### Q&A highlights
- **Will the final exam involve Python?** Yes, some programming; Python is expected and you have learned it.
- **Can I attend a different tutorial?** No — you must work with your group; a rare swap might be tolerated.
- **Could AI replace the lecturer?** No: AI learns from data, humans learn from experience, and AI is "cold and lifeless" while we bring individuality.
- **How do AIs differ (Gemini vs Claude)?** Same **transformer algorithm with attention mechanism**, but different training data, owners, safety and pricing.
- **Is AI creating or removing opportunities?** It depends on you — demonstrate **human value**; if you only do what AI does, there is no value.
- **Can I use AI in the exam?** No — it is written; the university's **two-lane policy** requires proving knowledge without AI for part of your grade.
- **What about AI built on human work taken without permission?** Regulation lags and fair-use applies; this course uses ML for social good, not artists' work — raise any ethical concern with the team.

TERMS TO KNOW
================================================
Generative AI: AI that produces new content or responses, such as ChatGPT or Claude.
Socio-technical: An approach treating technology and its social, ethical and human context as inseparable.
Artificial intelligence (AI): A computational method that maps inputs to outputs to make automated decisions.
Intelligence: The ability to act for desirable outcomes with high probability in previously unseen situations.
Machine learning (ML): A machine that ingests data and learns a model linking input data to output data.
Features: The input data fed into a machine-learning model.
Labels: The output data a machine-learning model predicts.
Model: A mathematical mapping $y = f_\theta(x)$ linking inputs to outputs.
Parameters: The adjustable values, denoted $\theta$, that a model learns during training.
Training: The phase where known data is used to find the best parameter values.
Supervised learning: Learning where both the inputs and outputs of the training data are known.
Unsupervised learning: Learning where outputs are unknown and the goal is to find patterns.
Reinforcement learning: Learning from environment feedback or reward to discover parameters.
Generalisation: A trained model's ability to perform correctly on unseen data.
Hallucination: When an AI confidently states information that is wrong.
Context window: The limited amount of prior conversation an AI can hold at once.
AI for Good: The project theme of using AI to benefit society, humanity and the economy.
Open assessment: An assessment in which AI use is permitted.
Secure assessment: An assessment that must be done without AI.
Hurdle requirement: A requirement, such as 80% attendance, that must be met to pass regardless of marks.
Absent Fail (AF): The result of missing three or more tutorials without Special Consideration.
Black box: A model whose internal workings lack interpretability, making it hard to verify.
Transformer: The algorithm with an attention mechanism underlying models such as Gemini and Claude.

### Example 1: Face unlock as Model, Train, Deploy
1. **Model:** maps an image of a face to "authorised" or "not authorised", i.e. $y = f_\theta(x)$.

### Example 2: Choosing the learning type
| Scenario | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Car trained by a human driver | Supervised | Known inputs and correct steering/brake outputs |
| Grouping unlabelled customers | Unsupervised | Outputs unknown; find patterns |
| Robot learning from a reward score | Reinforcement | Learns from environment feedback |

### Example 3: Deciding whether a problem suits ML
The negative examples give a scoping test:
1. Predicting a stock index with no use or error cost → **fails**.

3. Predicting bushfire from weather → **fails**; no reason ML beats conventional methods.
4. Diagnosing diabetes non-invasively → **fails**; less accurate than a blood test.
Lesson: couple a method to a real problem, a use case and an error cost.
