Neural Networks: MLPs, Training & CNNs

How machines learn patterns straight from data — from a single artificial neuron, through how networks train, to the convolutional architecture that made image recognition work.

Read this first: A neural network is just many tiny "weighted votes" stacked in layers. Each neuron scales its inputs by weights, adds a bias, then passes the sum through a non-linear activation. Training measures how wrong the network is with a loss function, then nudges every weight downhill using gradient descent and backpropagation. For images, plain networks break — convolution and pooling fix them.

Why Neural Networks for Engineering?

Neural networks learn the rules from raw data, so you don't have to hand-write them.

Traditional engineering leans on equations you derive from physics. But many real signals — vibration traces, camera images, sensor logs — are too messy for a tidy formula. A neural network (NN) sidesteps this: it learns patterns directly from data by stacking many simple units called neurons. Show it enough examples and it finds the relationship itself, without a human specifying the rules.

Lecture by Professor T. J. Lim.

Four engineering applications

🌉 Structural Health Monitoring

Detecting cracks, fatigue and anomalies in bridges and aircraft from vibration and sensor signals.

🔎 Automated Defect Detection

Spotting defects on production lines from images — faster and more consistent than manual inspection.

🛠️ Predictive Maintenance

Forecasting equipment failure from sensor data before it happens.

🤖 Autonomous Control Systems

Learning control policies for robots, drones and self-driving vehicles directly from data.

💡
Why it matters: Manual inspection is slow and inconsistent, and physics is hard to write for messy signals. NNs learn the pattern from data instead — exactly what predictive maintenance and defect detection need.

Borrowing from Biology: The Artificial Neuron

One neuron does three things: scale, add, then decide.

The artificial neuron copies a biological neuron. In the brain, signals arrive at the dendrites, are combined in the cell body, and — if strong enough — fire down the axon. The artificial version does the same job with arithmetic: inputs are scaled by weights, summed, and passed through an activation function to produce an output.

The perceptron

The full computation performed by a single neuron — called a perceptron — is:

Perceptron (one neuron)
ŷ = σ(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ⋯ + w_K x_K + b) = σ(z)

Here σ(·) is the activation function. It is usually non-linear, which is why the perceptron performs a non-linear operation. The weighted sum is often written separately as z = w₁x₁ + ⋯ + w_K x_K + b.

learned Weights and bias

The weights w₁, …, w_K and the bias b are the parameters that must be learned during training.

given Inputs

The inputs x₁, …, x_K are the input features in the first layer, and the outputs of a prior layer of neurons in the hidden layers.

Output ŷ — labelled ŷ, but it is only the network output at the output layer. At every other layer the neuron output is just an input to the next layer of neurons.
🧠
Remember: A neuron is a tiny "weighted vote" — each input votes with a weight, the bias acts as the threshold/tie-breaker, and the activation function decides the answer.

Activation Functions

The activation function decides how the weighted sum is squashed into the neuron's output.

After a neuron adds up its weighted inputs, an activation function transforms that raw sum z into the neuron's output. The lecture names three common choices.

ActivationFormulaOutput rangeTypical use
Sigmoidσ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)(0, 1)Classic choice for output probabilities
Tanhtanh(z)(−1, 1)Zero-centred — common in hidden layers
ReLUmax(0, z)[0, ∞)Cheap and effective — the default for hidden layers today

Sigmoid squashes every value into the interval (0, 1), which makes it a natural fit for output probabilities. Tanh squashes to (−1, 1) and is zero-centred, so it is common in hidden layers. ReLU keeps positive values and zeroes out negatives; it is cheap, effective, and the default for hidden layers today.

S · T · R
“Sigmoid, Tanh, ReLU”
The three common activations — squashing to (0,1) for probabilities, (−1,1) and zero-centred for hidden layers, and [0,∞) as the modern default.

Non-Linearity and Stacking Neurons

Without a non-linear activation, a deep network is still just a straight line.

Why bother with activations at all? Because of non-linearity. Without an activation function, even a complex multi-layered system collapses into a linear model and cannot represent cognitive systems or other complex systems. Add non-linear activations, and increasing the complexity of the network lets us mimic cognitive functions such as image recognition.

ℹ️
Key idea: Stacking linear layers just gives another linear layer. Non-linearity between the layers is what lets depth buy real expressive power.

Stacking neurons: the multilayer perceptron

Stack neurons into layers and you get a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) — a feedforward network, because each layer feeds its outputs forward to the next. The lecture's example has:

Hidden layer 1 · 4 neurons→ Hidden layer 2 · 4 neurons→ Output layer · 2 classes

What is the network actually learning?

An MLP synthesises complex non-linear decision boundaries — the surfaces that separate one class from another. The more layers and neurons you add, the more flexible those boundaries can be. This is both a blessing and a curse:

  • If training data is sufficient, an MLP can solve very complex classification problems it has never seen before.
  • If training is insufficient, the model may overfit and generalise poorly.

We return to overfitting and how to control it later in this guide.

Loss Functions: Measuring How Wrong We Are

You can't improve what you can't measure — the loss turns "how wrong" into a single number.

In any supervised learning setting we want the model output ŷ to be as close as possible to the ground-truth label y. But "close" needs precision. In classification we might try to maximise P(y = ŷ); in regression we might minimise E[(y − ŷ)²]. Both need the probability distribution of the features and the label — conceptually we learn that distribution from training data, so the more data we have, the better.

Either way, we define a loss function to minimise over the model parameters θ. For regression, MSE (mean square error) is the natural choice. For classification, the probability of error is not suitable, so we use a proxy called cross entropy.

Regression → MSE

Predicts a continuous number. Penalises the average squared gap between the prediction and the true value.

Classification → Cross entropy

Predicts a class. Penalises confident wrong probabilities; measures the distance between two distributions.

Mean Squared Error (MSE)

In regression the model directly outputs an estimate of the continuous label, ŷ = f_θ(x). For a given input x, the prediction ŷ is a function of θ, so the squared error (y − ŷ)² is a function of θ too. We want the θ that minimises the average of (y − ŷ)² over the distribution of x and y. Since we only have training data (not the true distributions), we approximate that average over all training samples:

Mean squared error
L(θ) = (1/N) Σᵢ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² for i = 1 … N

This is generally not a simple function of θ, so we cannot minimise it with a closed-form expression — which is why we need iterative training.

Cross entropy

In classification, y has a sample space of L values and the model must output a probability distribution over those values — its confidence that y takes each value:

A valid distribution
p₁, …, p_L such that Σₗ p_l = 1 for l = 1 … L

Naturally we want max(p₁, …, p_L) = p_y — the probability the model assigns to the ground-truth label — to be as high as possible for every training sample. But that statement gives us no way to find the optimal θ. So we bring in the concept of entropy. The cross entropy of two distributions P and Q over the same discrete sample space 𝒮 is:

Cross entropy
H(P, Q) = − Σ_{x∈𝒮} P(x) log₂ Q(x)

Cross entropy is minimal when P = Q — when the two distributions are identical. In that sense it behaves like a distance between distributions.

The elegant one-hot result

Suppose the label sample space is 𝒮_y = {1, 2, 3, 4} and the true value is y = 2. The true distribution is one-hot: p₁ = p₃ = p₄ = 0 and p₂ = 1. The model estimates a different distribution p̂_l. Because the true distribution puts all its mass on the label y, only one term of the sum survives, and the cross entropy collapses to:

Cross entropy for a one-hot label
H = − log₂ p̂_y

This is an incredibly elegant result: p̂_y is the y-th output of the model, so its relationship to the parameter vector θ is explicit. Here yᵢ denotes the ground-truth label of the i-th data sample. Worked illustration: if p̂₂ = 0.7, the loss is −log₂ 0.7 ≈ 0.515 bits; if the model is very confident at p̂₂ = 0.99, the loss drops to −log₂ 0.99 ≈ 0.0145 bits.

⚠️
Common trap: Don't use MSE for a class label or cross-entropy for a continuous target. Match the loss to the type of y — that pairing is a favourite exam point.

Training by Gradient Descent

The gradient points uphill; we step the opposite way to walk downhill on the loss.

Both MSE and cross-entropy are differentiable with respect to θ. But we still cannot solve the stationarity condition algebraically:

We can't solve this directly
∇L(θ) = dL/dθ = 0

A simple numerical method is to "descend" toward the nearest minimum through a sequence of steps taken in the direction of steepest descent.

  • The direction of steepest ascent is given by the gradient ∇L(θ); therefore the direction of steepest descent is the negative of that.
  • Denoting the step size (or learning rate) by μ, the steepest-descent iteration is:
Steepest-descent update
θ_{t+1} = θ_t − μ ∇L(θ_t)

Iterations continue until a stopping criterion is met — for example, θ changes by less than a threshold from one iteration to the next, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

💡
Why it matters: Gradient descent is the engine of all neural-network training. The gradient says which way is uphill; you step the opposite way, scaled by the learning rate, to reduce the loss.
🤔
Pause & predict: If the gradient is zero, what has the iteration found — and why might that not be the global minimum?

Batch, Mini-Batch and Stochastic Gradient Descent

Using every sample per step is slow; using one is noisy. A mini-batch sits in between.

Our two loss functions are averages over the entire training set, which is normally very large (at least in the thousands). The computational complexity and time of gradient calculation both scale linearly with N, the training-set size. Using all samples — known as batch processing — therefore consumes a lot of resources and is very slow. Instead we approximate the gradient with a mini-batch:

Mini-batch loss
L_batch(θ) = (1/N_b) Σ_{i ∈ mini-batch} lᵢ(θ)

where N_b ≪ N is the mini-batch size and lᵢ(θ) is either the squared error or the cross entropy for the i-th training sample.

The three variants

The mini-batch size N_b can be anything from 1 to N. The generally accepted terminology is:

MethodMini-batch sizeMeaning
Batch GDN_b = NUse all training samples per iteration
Mini-Batch GD2 ≤ N_b < NUse more than 1 but fewer than all samples per iteration
Stochastic GDN_b = 1Use only one sample per iteration

Unfortunately, SGD is also often used loosely to refer to mini-batch GD. Putting terminology aside, the main idea is to use N_b as a trade-off variable: a smaller N_b gives noisier but faster and less complex updates. The default in scikit-learn is N_b = 200, and it can be reset using the batch_size input parameter.

Epochs and batches

As we proceed through the batches, we eventually pass all the training data through the SGD solver. Each complete pass through the training data is known as a training epoch. The number of epochs we use is arbitrary — it depends on how much time and computational resources we have.

Mini-batches→ Weights updated once per mini-batch using GD→ Repeat over several epochs
🧠
Remember (BMS): Batch = all N, Mini = more than 1 but fewer than N, Stochastic = exactly 1 sample per step.

Choice of Step Size (Learning Rate)

The learning rate is the single most important knob in training.

The learning rate μ controls how far each gradient-descent step moves. It has three regimes:

🐢 Too small

Crawls toward the minimum — training takes far longer than necessary.

✅ Just right

Converges quickly and smoothly toward the minimum.

💥 Too large

Overshoots the minimum — it can oscillate or even diverge.

Good news: you don't need to pick the step size manually. Optimizers like Adam adapt the step size automatically.

✅
Do this: If training loss bounces or explodes, your learning rate is too large. If it barely moves, it is too small. When in doubt, reach for an adaptive optimizer like Adam.

Overfitting and Regularization

A model that memorises its training data is useless on new data.

If we have more parameters than training data — for example, an MLP with 1000 weights and only 500 training samples — we can drive the loss to a value that matches every input feature perfectly with its class. This is an extreme case of the overfitting phenomenon, and its generalisation performance will be poor.

How to fight it

  • Ensure the number of training samples exceeds the number of model parameters — an obvious first line of defence.
  • But overfitting is not always due to such an easily detected scenario. It is usually because the underlying model is simpler than what we assumed — and we don't know the underlying model!
  • All we can do is try to drive the smallish, essentially redundant weights down to zero.
  • The usual way is to penalise the norm of the parameter vector — adding a term that drives the norm toward zero. This is regularization.
💡
Why it matters: A flexible model can fit noise as easily as signal. Enough data and a norm penalty keep that flexibility in check so the model generalises.
🤔
Pause & predict: Your model gets 100% on training data but 60% on the test set. Is it overfitting or underfitting — and what would you change first?

Backpropagation

Backpropagation is just the chain rule, applied layer by layer from the output backwards.

In SGD we need the gradient of the loss with respect to each and every weight in the network. To do this we use the chain rule of differentiation, starting from the output layer and progressing backwards — hence backpropagation.

To illustrate, consider one of the output-layer weights:

Output-layer neuron
ŷ = σ(w₁x₁ + ⋯) = σ(z)

Applying the chain rule:

Chain rule for a weight
dL/dw₁ = (dL/dŷ) · (dŷ/dw₁) = (dL/dŷ) · σ'(z) · x₁

All the terms on the right-hand side can be calculated. In the lecture, the derivative with respect to one of the hidden-layer weights is demonstrated the same way.

🧠
Remember (CBO): Chain rule, Backwards from Output. Forward pass to predict, backward pass to assign blame to each weight.

Why Images Are Problematic for MLPs

Images are spatially structured; a plain MLP throws that structure away and explodes in size.

An MLP assumes that features are just a flat list: if you swap two features in the order they are fed in, the result is unchanged. In other words, adjacency carries no information, because the ordering of features is arbitrary. That is fine for a table of independent measurements — but disastrous for an image, where nearby pixels are strongly related.

To feed an image into an MLP we would flatten the pixels, turning the 2D matrix of pixel values into a 1D vector. This creates two problems.

ProblemWhat goes wrong
1. Parameter explosionA 128 × 128 grayscale image becomes 16,384 features at the input layer. If the first hidden layer has only 128 neurons, we already need over 2 million weights in that first layer.
2. No shift invarianceAny detail (a face, a sign) shifted by even one pixel is not recognised as the same thing. The MLP has no built-in notion that something is the "same pattern but moved".
The arithmetic behind problem 1
128 × 128 = 16,384 inputs; 16,384 × 128 = 2,097,152 weights
💡
Why it matters: Flattening both destroys spatial structure and blows up the parameter count. CNNs are designed to keep the 2D structure and share weights instead.

Convolution: Filtering to Reveal Features

A small filter slides over the image and lights up wherever its pattern appears.

Convolution (or linear filtering) is nothing new. A filter for a 2D image can change the image so that certain colours are amplified or edges are sharpened — digital image processing has been doing this for years. The effect of filtering depends critically on the filter kernel w_{j,k}, where the total number of weights in the kernel is user-defined. The filter output is:

2D convolution
y_{m,n} = Σ_j Σ_k w_{j,k} · x_{m−j, n−k}

Depicted pictorially, this is much less obtuse than the equation looks: the kernel is a small window that slides across the image, and at each position it multiplies the overlapping pixels by its weights and sums them.

The vertical edge detector

The lecture's illustrative kernel is a vertical edge detector. If the image window contains a vertical edge — say a value of 10 in the first two columns and 0 in the third — the filter responds strongly, because the kernel contrasts neighbouring columns. Where there is no transition, the response is weak. Different kernels reveal different feature maps: the lecture shows the original image, its vertical edges, its horizontal edges, and a blur.

The CNN convolutional layer

A CNN starts with a convolutional layer where the kernels are not hand-designed but learned from the data. Instead of manually deciding what features to extract, we let the learning algorithm decide. If the task is deciding whether a picture contains a furry animal, "furriness" could be a more important feature than "vertical edge".

This automatic feature extraction is the key to solving the 2nd MLP problem — the inability to recognise a feature that has been shifted. It does not solve the complexity issue, which is where max pooling comes in.

ℹ️
Key idea: A learned kernel is just a set of weights. Because the same kernel slides everywhere, a feature is detected no matter where it appears — that is shift invariance for free.

Pooling and the LeNet-5 CNN

Pooling shrinks each feature map without losing the evidence that a feature was found.

An image carries redundancy — an edge carries over multiple pixels, and so does a single colour or a texture. This redundancy translates into the feature map too. It is therefore sufficient to collapse a small region of a feature map into just one number: within a 2 × 2 pixel square, retain only the largest value to represent the presence of that feature in the region.

This operation is called max pooling, and it solves the 1st MLP problem of parameter explosion. We can also use average pooling, which keeps the average of the feature map in each region.

Max pooling

Keeps the strongest activation in each region — the clearest indication that the feature was found. That is all the next layer needs for face recognition or anomaly detection.

Average pooling

Keeps the average of the region instead of the maximum. Smoother, but less selective about the strongest evidence.

Max pooling, 2 × 2
[[0.2, 0.9], [0.4, 0.1]] → 0.9 (strongest activation kept)

We just reduced the number of inputs to the next layer by a factor of 4 without losing (much) information — for example, a 128 × 128 feature map becomes 64 × 64 after 2 × 2 pooling.

LeNet-5: the pioneering CNN

The lecture highlights LeNet-5 as the pioneering convolutional neural network. Its pipeline is the template every modern CNN still follows:

Input image→ Convolutional layer (learned kernels)→ Max pooling→ … layers …→ Softmax output
🧠
Remember (CP): Convolution fixes Position (same pattern, any location); Pooling fixes Parameter count (shrink the map). Together they make image recognition practical.

Softmax: Turning Scores into Probabilities

The network produces raw scores; softmax normalises them into a valid probability distribution.

Recall that in a classification problem where the label space has L values, a model should produce probability values p₁, …, p_L, where p_l quantifies the model's confidence that the true label is l. Forward propagation through a network is designed to produce L values, but there is no way those values will sum to 1 without normalisation. This normalisation operation is softmax:

Softmax
softmax_i(z₁, …, z_L) = e^{z_i} / Σₗ e^{z_l} for l = 1 … L

There are many ways to transform L raw numbers into a probability distribution, but softmax makes the most sense because:

  • It allows the raw input to be any real value — negative or positive.
  • The exponentiation magnifies the gap between the largest and smallest values.
  • Most importantly, it has a simple derivative with respect to any z_i — which is exactly what backpropagation needs.

Worked examples: dog, cat or airplane

Example 1. The task is classifying an image as "dog", "cat" or "airplane". If the raw network outputs are (2.0, 1.0, 0.1), the softmax outputs are (0.659, 0.242, 0.099). Instead of a factor of two between the top two choices, the ratio is now 2.72 — the gap has been magnified. Check: e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e^0.1 = 1.105, sum = 11.212, and 7.389 / 11.212 ≈ 0.659.

Example 2. Same task. If the raw outputs are (1, 2, 8), the model is more confident in the most likely label than above. Softmax accentuates that certainty and outputs probabilities of (0.001, 0.002, 0.997).

💡
Why it matters: Networks output raw scores, not probabilities. Softmax turns them into a valid distribution that pairs naturally with cross-entropy loss.

Parameters vs Hyperparameters (and the Code)

Keep the two kinds of "knobs" separate — the model learns one, you choose the other.

It is easy to blur two ideas that both sound like "settings". Keep them apart:

ParametersHyperparameters
What they areWeights w and bias bSettings chosen before training
Who sets themLearned from data during trainingChosen by the human designer
Examplesw₁, …, w_K, bLearning rate μ, mini-batch size N_b, epochs, layers/neurons, CNN kernel size

Parameters are learned by minimising the loss with SGD and backpropagation. Hyperparameters shape training — how big each batch is, how large each step is, how long to train, and how big the network is. The number of epochs is described in the lecture as arbitrary, depending on available time and compute.

The hands-on code

The lecture closes with hands-on examples in scikit-learn and PyTorch. In scikit-learn the mini-batch size defaults to 200 and is reset via the batch_size input parameter; optimizers such as Adam remove the need to hand-pick the step size.

⚠️
Common trap: Most training mistakes come from hyperparameters — step size, batch size, epochs — not from the parameters the optimiser learns. Tune those first.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else, remember this.

1

Neural networks learn patterns from data — loosely inspired by the brain, used in engineering for structural health monitoring, automated defect detection, predictive maintenance and autonomous control.

2

A neuron scales, sums, then decides — inputs are scaled by weights, summed with a bias, and passed through an activation: ŷ = σ(Σ w_k x_k + b).

3

Weights and bias are the learned parameters — inputs are features in the first layer and hidden-layer outputs thereafter; only the output layer produces the network output.

4

Activations add non-linearity — sigmoid squashes to (0,1) for output probabilities, tanh to (−1,1) and is zero-centred for hidden layers, and ReLU (max(0,z)) is the cheap modern default.

5

Match the loss to the task — MSE, L(θ) = (1/N) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)², for regression; cross-entropy for classification, which reduces to −log₂ p̂_y for a one-hot label.

6

Gradient descent walks downhill — since ∇L(θ) = 0 has no closed form, iterate θ_{t+1} = θ_t − μ∇L(θ_t) until a stopping criterion is met.

7

Mini-batch SGD trades noise for speed — N_b = N is batch GD, 2 ≤ N_b < N is mini-batch GD, N_b = 1 is stochastic GD; smaller N_b is noisier but faster, and scikit-learn defaults to 200.

8

An epoch is one full pass through the data — its number is arbitrary, and the learning rate must be not too small, not too large, with optimizers like Adam adapting it automatically.

9

Overfitting happens when the model is too flexible — e.g. 1000 weights with 500 samples; fix it with more data and by penalising the parameter-vector norm (regularization).

10

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied backwards — from the output layer back, e.g. dL/dw₁ = (dL/dŷ) · σ'(z) · x₁.

11

MLPs fail on images, but CNNs fix it — parameter explosion (128 × 128 → 16,384 inputs → 2M+ weights) and no shift invariance; convolution learns kernels while pooling cuts inputs by 4×.

12

Softmax makes probabilities — it normalises L raw scores so they sum to 1, magnifying gaps and keeping a simple derivative.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Neural network (NN)
A machine-learning model, loosely inspired by the brain, that learns patterns directly from data by stacking simple units called neurons.
Perceptron
The full computation of a single neuron: ŷ = σ(Σ w_k x_k + b). The basic building block of a network.
Activation function
A usually non-linear function σ(·) applied to a neuron's weighted sum to produce its output; it is what gives the network non-linearity.
Sigmoid
An activation, σ(z) = 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ), that squashes values into (0,1) — classically used for output probabilities.
Tanh
An activation, tanh(z), that squashes to (−1,1). It is zero-centred, so it is common in hidden layers.
ReLU
An activation, max(0, z): cheap and effective, the default choice for hidden layers today.
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)
A feedforward neural network that stacks neurons into hidden layers and an output layer; the most straightforward NN.
Non-linearity
The property introduced by activation functions that lets a network model complex, curved relationships; without it, even a deep network is linear.
Decision boundary
The surface separating classes in the input space. More layers and neurons let an MLP synthesise more flexible non-linear boundaries.
Loss function
A differentiable measure of how far the model output is from the ground-truth label, minimised over the parameters during training.
Overfitting
Fitting the training data so closely (e.g. more parameters than samples) that generalisation to new data becomes poor.
Parameter
A value learned from data during training — specifically a weight w or bias b.
Hyperparameter
A setting chosen by the designer before training — such as learning rate, mini-batch size, epochs, or architecture.
Gradient descent
The method that repeatedly steps in the direction of steepest descent (the negative gradient) to minimise the loss.
Learning rate (μ)
The step size controlling how far each gradient-descent update moves; too small crawls, too large overshoots or diverges.
Epoch
One complete pass through the entire training dataset; the number of epochs used is arbitrary.
Stochastic / mini-batch GD
Gradient descent using a subset of samples per step: mini-batch uses 2 ≤ N_b < N, stochastic uses N_b = 1; "SGD" is often used loosely for mini-batch GD.
Backpropagation
Computing every weight's gradient via the chain rule, moving backwards from the output layer to the input.
Regularization
Penalising the norm of the parameter vector — adding a term that drives small, redundant weights toward zero to reduce overfitting.
Convolution
A linear filtering operation, y_{m,n} = Σ_j Σ_k w_{j,k} x_{m−j,n−k}, where a small kernel slides over the input to reveal features.
Max pooling
Collapsing a feature-map region (e.g. 2 × 2) to its largest value, cutting the next layer's inputs by a factor of 4 to fight parameter explosion.
Softmax
A normalisation, e^{z_i} / Σ_l e^{z_l}, that converts L raw scores into a probability distribution summing to 1.
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
The regression loss (1/N) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)², the average squared error over the training data.
Cross entropy
The classification loss and a distance between distributions, H(P,Q) = −Σ P(x) log₂ Q(x); for a one-hot label it reduces to −log₂ p̂_y.

Q1

Why is a non-linear activation function essential in a neural network?

Answer: Without an activation function, even a complex multi-layered system remains linear and cannot model cognitive systems or other complex systems. Adding non-linearity lets increasing the network's complexity mimic cognitive functions such as image recognition.
Q2

Write the perceptron equation and identify which quantities are learned parameters.

Answer: ŷ = σ(w₁x₁ + ⋯ + w_K x_K + b) = σ(z). The weights w₁, …, w_K and the bias b are the parameters learned during training; the inputs x are features (first layer) or prior-layer outputs (hidden layers).
Q3

Compare the three common activation functions: sigmoid, tanh and ReLU.

Answer: Sigmoid 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ) squashes to (0,1) and is classically used for output probabilities. Tanh tanh(z) squashes to (−1,1), is zero-centred, and is common in hidden layers. ReLU max(0,z) keeps positives, zeroes negatives, is cheap and effective, and is the default for hidden layers today.
Q4

Distinguish parameters from hyperparameters, with examples of each.

Answer: Parameters (weights and bias) are learned from data during training. Hyperparameters (learning rate, mini-batch size, number of epochs, architecture/layers/neurons, CNN kernel size) are chosen by the designer before training.
Q5

Which loss function suits regression versus classification, and why?

Answer: Regression uses MSE, L(θ) = (1/N) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)², because the model outputs a continuous estimate. Classification uses cross entropy because the model outputs a probability distribution over L labels; the raw probability of error is not a suitable objective, so cross entropy is the proxy.
Q6

Using 𝒮_y = {1,2,3,4} and true label y = 2, explain why cross entropy becomes −log₂ p̂_y, and compute it for p̂₂ = 0.7.

Answer: The true distribution is one-hot: p₁ = p₃ = p₄ = 0, p₂ = 1. In H(P,Q) = −Σ P(x) log₂ Q(x), only the x = 2 term survives, leaving −log₂ p̂₂. For p̂₂ = 0.7, loss = −log₂ 0.7 ≈ 0.515 bits; at p̂₂ = 0.99 it is ≈ 0.0145 bits.
Q7

State the gradient-descent update rule and explain the stopping criterion.

Answer: Steepest ascent is ∇L(θ), so descent is its negative. With learning rate μ, update θ_{t+1} = θ_t − μ∇L(θ_t). Iterate until a stopping criterion is met — e.g. θ changes by less than a threshold, or a maximum number of iterations is reached. We cannot solve ∇L(θ) = 0 in closed form.
Q8

Distinguish batch, mini-batch and stochastic gradient descent. What is an epoch, and what is scikit-learn's default batch size?

Answer: Batch GD uses all samples per iteration (N_b = N); mini-batch GD uses 2 ≤ N_b < N; stochastic GD uses one sample (N_b = 1). Smaller N_b is noisier but faster and less complex per update. An epoch is one complete pass through the training data, and its number is arbitrary. scikit-learn's default is N_b = 200, resettable via batch_size.
Q9

What happens if the learning rate is too small or too large? How do you avoid choosing it manually?

Answer: Too small: training crawls toward the minimum. Too large: it overshoots and can oscillate or even diverge. Just right: it converges quickly and smoothly. You avoid manual tuning by using adaptive optimizers such as Adam, which adjust the step size automatically.
Q10

Explain overfitting with the 1000-weights / 500-samples example, and how to reduce it.

Answer: With more parameters (1000 weights) than training samples (500), the network can drive the loss down by matching every training input perfectly to its class — an extreme case of overfitting, with poor generalisation. Reduce it by ensuring training samples exceed parameters, gathering more data, and penalising the norm of the parameter vector (regularization) to shrink small, redundant weights toward zero.
Q11

What is backpropagation, and write the chain-rule expression for an output-layer weight.

Answer: Backpropagation computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight using the chain rule, starting at the output layer and progressing backwards. For an output-layer weight, dL/dw₁ = (dL/dŷ) · (dŷ/dw₁) = (dL/dŷ) · σ'(z) · x₁; all terms on the right can be calculated.
Q12

State the two problems of feeding an image to an MLP, and do the parameter arithmetic for a 128 × 128 grayscale image with a 128-neuron first hidden layer.

Answer: (1) Parameter explosion: flattening a 2D image into a 1D vector creates huge input counts. (2) No shift invariance: a detail shifted by one pixel is not recognised as the same pattern, because adjacency is lost. Arithmetic: 128 × 128 = 16,384 inputs; with 128 neurons that is 16,384 × 128 = 2,097,152 weights — over 2 million in the first layer alone.
Q13

How do convolution and pooling address the two MLP image problems? Include the factor-of-4 reduction.

Answer: Convolution solves the shift problem: kernels are learned from data and slide over the image, so a feature is detected regardless of position. Pooling solves parameter explosion: max pooling collapses a 2 × 2 region to its largest value, reducing the next layer's inputs by a factor of 4 (e.g. 128 × 128 → 64 × 64) while keeping the strongest evidence the feature was found. LeNet-5 is the pioneering CNN.
Q14

What does softmax do, why is it preferred, and what are the outputs for raw scores (2.0, 1.0, 0.1)?

Answer: Softmax normalises L raw scores into a probability distribution that sums to 1. It is preferred because it accepts any real input, exponentiation magnifies the gap between the largest and smallest values, and it has a simple derivative with respect to any z_i. For (2.0, 1.0, 0.1) the output is (0.659, 0.242, 0.099) — the top-two ratio grows from 2 to 2.72.
