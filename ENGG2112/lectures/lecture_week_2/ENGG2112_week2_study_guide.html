Academic Integrity and the Responsible Use of AI

Professor Mary Lou Maher's Week 2 lecture asks when generative AI use is honest and when it is cheating, covering Sydney's two-lane policy, how to acknowledge AI, and how to write your own policy.

Read this first: the through-line is using AI is not the crime — hiding it is. What decides whether a chatbot helps or harms you is whether you did your own thinking and disclosed the tool's role.

Why Academic Integrity Matters

Integrity is not a rule to avoid punishment — it is what makes your degree mean something.

Integrity is central to learning: honest work builds knowledge, skills, and self-esteem; cheating skips that and harms others.

Honest work→ Real skills built→ Trusted degree→ Fair education

The five reasons the lecture gives

  • Central to learning. University should change what you can do, not just collect marks.
  • Misconduct gives an unfair advantage — a violation called academic misconduct rewards dishonest shortcuts.
  • Honest work builds you up: knowledge, skills, and self-esteem.
  • Cheating lowers everyone's education: it undermines teaching quality and degree worth.
  • Cheating damages the institution's reputation and your degree's credibility.
💡
Why it matters: If enough people cheat, trust in a Sydney degree falls and everyone's qualification loses value.

Plagiarism and the Use of AI

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's work as your own — and "someone" can be a machine.

Plagiarism — presenting someone else's work, ideas, or intellectual property as your own without acknowledgment or permission; copying or closely imitating another's work and claiming it as original.

Submitting AI-written text as your own is still presenting work you did not do. The tool changes; the principle does not.

Scenario 1 vs Scenario 2: the same tool, opposite verdicts

Two students use an AI chatbot on a report; the verdict depends on how it was used and whether it was disclosed.

⚠️ Scenario 1 — no attribution

AI generates entire paragraphs, analyses, or conclusions; they are submitted without citing the AI.

Verdict: likely plagiarism.

✅ Scenario 2 — proper attribution

AI helps brainstorm, check grammar, or outline; the student writes independently with their own analysis and acknowledges the AI.

Verdict: not plagiarism.

🧠
Remember: Same tool, opposite verdict. What decides it is not whether you used AI but whether you disclosed it and did your own thinking.

The Ambiguous Middle Ground

Not every case is a clean yes or no — the lecture leaves one open to show why transparency is safest.

Between an obvious "yes" and "no" is a grey zone; the deliberately ambiguous scenario shows responsible-use rules do not always give a neat answer.

The ambiguous scenario — a student uses AI to summarise research articles, paraphrases parts of the AI summaries, adds own interpretation, and cites the originals, stating they used AI for "assistance with summarizing sources."

Two defensible readings

Acceptable use

The student cited the originals, added analysis, and disclosed AI — responsible use as a research aid.

Plagiarism

The student relied heavily on AI content and never explicitly acknowledged the AI's full role.

ℹ️
Key idea: The verdict depends on AI transparency — how clearly the AI's role is acknowledged — and on institutional policy. Where rules are unclear, over-disclose.

The University of Sydney Two-Lane Policy

Assessments are like roads with two lanes — the lane decides whether AI is allowed.

From Semester 2, 2025, Sydney uses a two-lane approach: every assessment belongs to one lane, which says whether you may use AI.

FeatureLane 1: SecureLane 2: Open
SupervisionIn-person, supervisedUnsupervised
ExamplesExams, testsAssignments, reports
AI allowed?Generally no — unless expressly permittedYes, with appropriate acknowledgment
PurposeConfirm you personally have the skillsDevelop disciplinary knowledge and skills

Lane 1 (secure) tasks — exams and tests — generally ban AI unless the coordinator gives express permission in the Unit Outline; using it otherwise can breach integrity. In Lane 2 (open) tasks you may use AI provided you properly acknowledge it; doing so is not a breach.

Misusing AI and the Consequences

Misusing AI is not just "cheating" — it can be a privacy breach, and penalties scale with severity and history.

Three ways students misuse AI at Sydney

  1. Using prohibited tools. Generative AI — chatbots, paraphrasing or translation tools — where prohibited.
  2. Submitting without acknowledgment. AI-generated work with no appropriate acknowledgment.
  3. Feeding in protected material. Inputting University or another student's material, external partners' IP, or anyone's personal or health information.
⚠️
Common trap: Example 3 is a privacy rule — even when AI is allowed, what you feed into the tool can still be a breach.

Acknowledging the Use of AI

If AI is allowed, acknowledgment turns "cheating" into "responsible use" — and the rules are exact.

If your coordinator permits automated writing tools or generative AI, include a statement explaining your use.

The five required elements of an acknowledgment statement

  1. State that AI was used.
  2. Name and version of the tools.
  3. Publisher. Who publishes the tool.
  4. URL. The tool's uniform resource locator.
  5. Description. A brief description of how you used it.

Also follow any additional stipulations your coordinator makes.

N · V · P · U · D
"Name, Version, Publisher, URL, Description"
The five things every AI acknowledgment statement must contain.

The more comprehensive acknowledgment

A fuller acknowledgment adds an interaction transcript to the statement: sample prompts, the responses received, and how those exchanges influenced the final work. It ensures transparency, accountability, and verifiable evidence of ethical AI engagement.

Seven Principles for Responsible AI Use

Responsible use is a checklist of seven principles — the lens for critiquing any AI policy.

Because cases are ambiguous and AI advances rapidly, there must be ongoing discussion of AI's use, principles, and policy.

1. Academic Integrity

Work stays honest and attributed.

2. Disclosure

Be transparent about when and how AI was used.

3. Data Privacy

Protect personal and sensitive data.

4. Bias Awareness

Recognise AI outputs can be biased or wrong.

5. Human Oversight

A person reviews and directs AI output.

6. Informed Consent

Affected people should know and agree.

7. Continuous Monitoring

Watch and review AI use over time.

Data Privacy

Anything you type into AI may leave your control, so privacy is shared by you and the University.

Data privacy — safeguarding personal, sensitive, or identifiable information from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.

Treat an AI chat box like a public noticeboard: once you paste something in, you no longer control who reads it or how it is stored.

Who is responsible for what

WhoResponsibility
StudentsBe cautious what you input; avoid sensitive or personally identifiable data unless explicitly permitted.
UniversitiesSelect tools with clear privacy policies, train on best practices, and set ethical-use guidelines.

Policy considerations

  • Third-party AI platforms. External servers raise concerns about data residency, access control, and compliance.
  • Data breaches. Institutions need protocols to identify, respond to, and mitigate AI-system breaches.
  • Balancing privacy and utility. Keeping data private while getting AI value is complex; data anonymisation is essential.
Data anonymisation — removing identifying details so data can be used without exposing the individuals behind it.

Bias Awareness

AI is not neutral or infallible — it reflects its training data, so question it.

Uncritical reliance on biased output can compromise research and learning, and propagate stereotypes or misinformation. An AI model mirrors its training data: incomplete or unrepresentative data gives a skewed reflection that still sounds confident.

Four key elements of bias awareness

  • Critical evaluation of AI output. Question suggestions and analyses; watch for biased language, stereotypes, or inaccuracies.
  • Understanding data and model limitations. Training data may be incomplete or unrepresentative, affecting fairness and accuracy.
  • Promoting equity and fairness. Ask whether outputs reflect diverse perspectives; mitigate bias with diverse viewpoints or corroborating sources.
  • Ethical responsibility. Avoid discrimination, ensure neutrality, and advocate equitable practices.

Policy considerations

  • Bias audits. Regularly evaluate models, especially around race, gender, or socioeconomic status.
  • Inclusive datasets. Train on diverse, representative data.
  • Transparency and explainability. Promote explainable models so users can spot bias.
  • Training and awareness. Educate staff and students to recognise bias.

Risks of Over-Reliance and Deskilling

If AI produces the answer, you never build the skill — that loss is deskilling.

Over-reliance on generative AI carries four named risks, because an assignment's purpose is to build skills, not just produce an answer.

Figure adapted from Enago Academy (enago.com/academy/responsible-use-of-generative-ai).

1. Algorithmic bias

The model reproduces unfair patterns from its training data.

2. Impaired creativity

Leaning on generated answers dulls creative thinking.

3. Reduced critical thinking

If AI does the reasoning, your reasoning weakens.

4. Lack of interpretation

You accept output without genuinely interpreting it.

Deskilling — the loss of skills such as creativity and critical thinking from letting AI do work you were supposed to learn to do.

Like a calculator before arithmetic: the answer appears, but understanding never forms. Over-reliance is an integrity issue, not just a quality issue.

Seven Rules for Responsible Use in Homework

These rules turn the principles into a workflow for before, during, and after any assignment.

  1. Original thinking and input. Attempt each assignment independently; use AI only after genuine effort.
  2. Cite AI content appropriately. Identify and credit AI-generated content.
  3. Integrate AI content ethically. Use output so it supports the task and does not overpower your input.
  4. Review and edit AI content. Check accuracy, relevance, and coherence, and personalise it.
  5. Respect academic honesty policies. Never use AI to violate university or instructor policies.
  6. Ask instructors for clarification. If unsure whether or how to use AI, ask.
  7. Be transparent about AI usage. If allowed, disclose your use and explain it.
✅
Do this: Test a scenario against all seven rules in order; a "no" anywhere is a red flag.

Six Misuse Scenarios to Avoid

Learn these six named breaches so you can spot them instantly in a scenario question.

Plagiarism

Using AI content without citation and presenting it as your own.

Misrepresentation of knowledge and skills

Relying solely on AI misrepresents your understanding and inflates grades.

Fabrication of results or data

Using AI to generate data, results, or findings can fabricate outcomes.

Violating assignment instructions

Using AI where an assignment prohibits it violates the guidelines.

Inappropriate use for exams

Using AI during exams, directly or indirectly, breaks exam regulations.

Evading learning and understanding

Over-reliance on AI hinders critical thinking and deep understanding.

Four Case Studies on a Spectrum

Four students span the range from clearly irresponsible to clearly responsible, with two ambiguous cases between.

Alex — irresponsible (Python assignment)

Alex used a code-generating AI to produce the whole codebase, never reviewed or understood it, and submitted it without disclosing the AI — though the code had to be written independently.

Why: no originality, plagiarism, misrepresentation of skills, ignoring instructions, no transparency.

Dru — responsible (linguistics text analysis)

An NLP major used an AI NLP tool on a language-analysis assignment, reviewed the output for linguistic patterns, semantics, and syntax, incorporated it, and cited the tool.

Why: Dru understood the output, used AI as a supplement that aided critical thinking, reviewed for accuracy, and cited it properly.

Deng — ambiguous (calculus problem set)

A maths major used an AI solver to solve equations and derive theorems, then reviewed the solutions against calculus principles and integrated them.

Ambiguous because: the significant assistance may cross the line of acceptable aid and may undermine Deng's understanding, though the work was validated. A professor valuing accuracy over method may accept it; otherwise a lower grade or integrity concerns.

Taylor — AI study game for exam prep

A computer science student prompted a specialised AI tool to build a study game of questions, quizzes, and challenges, then used it to test knowledge and find weak areas.

Ambiguous because: it is resourceful, but classmates may lack similar tools, creating a possible unfair advantage and an access imbalance. A professor may accept it, or adjust the exam or add resources to level the field.

Writing an AI Policy

A good policy turns vague intentions into clear rules addressing coauthorship and automated generation.

The tutorial asks you to critique existing policies and write your own, focusing on where they fail to handle coauthorship (AI as collaborator) and automated generation (AI producing content itself).

The four tutorial activities

Critique AI use in a course report and in a technical task such as writing code; critique Sydney's AI policy against the responsible-use principles and write a replacement; and write an AI policy for this unit of study.

How to build a policy, step by step

  1. Identify the assessment type. Secure (supervised) or open (unsupervised)?
  2. Decide permitted uses — whether and where AI may be used.
  3. Set disclosure rules. Require an acknowledgment statement and, where appropriate, a transcript.
  4. Protect privacy. Ban inputting personal, health, or protected course and partner material.
  5. Address bias. Require critical evaluation, inclusive sources, and human review.
  6. Set consequences for minor, major, and persistent breaches.
  7. Keep it under review. Commit to continuous monitoring as AI evolves.
✅
Do this: To critique any policy, map it against the seven principles; any ignored principle — often disclosure, data privacy, or bias awareness — is a weakness to point out.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Academic integrity
The commitment to honest, fair scholarship that makes learning and qualifications trustworthy.
Academic misconduct
A violation of academic integrity — for example cheating, plagiarism, or collusion — that gives an unfair advantage to those who take dishonest shortcuts.
Plagiarism
Presenting someone else's work, ideas, or intellectual property as your own without proper acknowledgment or permission.
Generative AI
AI tools, such as chatbots, that produce text or other content in response to prompts.
Two-lane approach
The University of Sydney policy from Semester 2, 2025 that splits assessments into secure and open lanes with different AI rules.
Secure assessment (Lane 1)
An in-person, supervised assessment, such as an exam or test, where AI is generally not permitted unless the coordinator gives express permission.
Open assessment (Lane 2)
An unsupervised assessment where students may use AI provided they acknowledge it appropriately.
Acknowledgment statement
A declaration in your submission naming the AI tools used, their publishers and URLs, and how they were used.
AI interaction transcript
An appendix showing sample prompts, the responses received, and how those exchanges influenced the final work.
Contract cheating
Paying or asking someone else to do your assessed work. A major breach that can also expose you to blackmail.
Data privacy
Safeguarding personal, sensitive, or identifiable information from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.
Data anonymisation
Removing identifying details so data can be used without exposing the individuals it came from.
Algorithmic bias
Unfair or skewed patterns reproduced by an AI model from its training data.
Bias audit
A regular evaluation of an AI model for biases, especially around protected characteristics such as race, gender, or socioeconomic status.
Over-reliance
Depending on AI output so heavily that your own skills and understanding do not develop.
Deskilling
The loss of skills, such as creativity and critical thinking, caused by letting AI do the work.
Continuous monitoring
The principle that AI use and its effects should be watched and reviewed over time.
Human oversight
The principle that a person stays responsible for reviewing and directing AI output rather than delegating judgement to the tool.
Informed consent
The principle that people affected by AI use should know about it and agree to it.
Disclosure
The principle that you must be transparent about when and how AI was used.

Q1

Why does academic integrity matter beyond simply avoiding punishment?

Answer: It is crucial to the learning process. Honest work builds knowledge, skills and self-esteem; misconduct gives an unfair advantage to those who take shortcuts; cheating undermines the quality of education for everyone and reduces the real value of a university degree; and it damages the University's reputation and the credibility of a Sydney degree.
Q2

Define plagiarism and explain how it applies when AI writes the text.

Answer: Plagiarism is presenting someone else's work, ideas, or intellectual property as your own without proper acknowledgment or permission — copying or closely imitating another's work and claiming it as original. It applies to AI because submitting AI-generated content as your own original effort is still presenting work you did not do as if you did it. The tool changes; the principle does not.
Q3

Two students use the same AI tool on a report but get opposite verdicts. What distinguishes Scenario 1 from Scenario 2?

Answer: In Scenario 1 the AI generates entire paragraphs, analyses, or conclusions and the student submits them without citing the AI — likely plagiarism. In Scenario 2 the AI only assists with brainstorming, grammar, or outlines, the student writes independently with their own analysis, and acknowledges the AI — not plagiarism. The deciding factors are disclosure and how much of the student's own thinking went in, not whether AI was used.
Q4

Why is the AI-summarising scenario ambiguous, and what does the verdict hinge on?

Answer: The student cites the original articles and adds personal interpretation, which suggests acceptable use as a research aid; but they rely heavily on AI content without explicitly acknowledging the AI's full role, which suggests plagiarism. It hinges on AI transparency — how clearly the AI's role is acknowledged — and on institutional policy and expectations.
Q5

Describe the two-lane approach and state which lane each assessment type falls into.

Answer: From Semester 2, 2025, the University of Sydney split assessments into two lanes. Lane 1 (secure) covers in-person, supervised assessments such as exams and tests, where AI is generally not permitted. Lane 2 (open) covers unsupervised assessments such as assignments and reports, where AI is allowed with appropriate acknowledgment.
Q6

When, if ever, may you use AI in a secure assessment?

Answer: Generally never — students are not permitted to use AI in secure (supervised) tasks such as exams and tests. The only exception is if the Unit of Study coordinator gives express permission in the Unit Outline. Using AI when it is not allowed can amount to a breach of academic integrity and lead to an investigation.
Q7

Give three examples of misusing AI at the University of Sydney.

Answer: (1) Using generative AI — chatbots, paraphrasing tools, or translation tools — in an assessment where their use is prohibited. (2) Submitting AI-generated work without appropriate acknowledgment. (3) Inputting University teaching or course materials, another student's content, intellectual property from external partners, or any person's personal or health information into AI tools.
Q8

List the five required elements of an acknowledgment statement.

Answer: (1) That you used AI tools in completing the work. (2) The name and version of the tools. (3) The publisher. (4) The uniform resource locator (URL). (5) A brief description of how you used them. You must also follow any additional stipulations your unit coordinator makes.
Q9

What is an AI interaction transcript, and why is it required?

Answer: It is an appendix or transcript showing sample prompts you entered, the responses you received, and how those exchanges influenced the final work. It is required to ensure transparency, hold students accountable, and provide verifiable evidence of ethical AI engagement — demonstrating your role and engagement, not just the tool's output.
Q10

Name the seven principles for the responsible use of AI in higher education.

Answer: Academic integrity, Disclosure, Data privacy, Bias awareness, Human oversight, Informed consent, and Continuous monitoring.
Q11

Name the four risks of over-reliance and explain deskilling.

Answer: The four risks are algorithmic bias, impaired creativity, reduced critical thinking, and lack of interpretation. Deskilling is the loss of skills — such as creativity and critical thinking — caused by letting AI do the work you were supposed to learn to do. If AI produces the answer, the skill is never built.
Q12

Contrast Alex's case with Dru's. Why is one irresponsible and the other responsible?

Answer: Alex used a code-generating AI to produce the entire Python codebase, never reviewed or understood it, and submitted it as their own without disclosure — lacking originality, committing plagiarism, misrepresenting skills, ignoring instructions, and hiding the tool. Dru used an AI tool to assist a linguistics analysis, actively reviewed and validated the output, incorporated it thoughtfully, and cited the AI — so it was a supplementary tool that supported learning, integration, review, and proper citation.
