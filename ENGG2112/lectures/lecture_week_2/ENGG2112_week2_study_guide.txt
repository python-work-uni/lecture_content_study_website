SECTION 1: Why Academic Integrity Matters
------------------------------------------------
Academic integrity is essential to learning itself. It is not just rules to avoid punishment; it is what makes education meaningful and fair.

- Academic integrity is crucial to the learning process.
- Violating academic integrity (that is, **academic misconduct**) gives an unfair advantage to those who take dishonest shortcuts.
- Honest work builds knowledge, skills, and self-esteem.
- Cheating undermines the quality of education for everyone and reduces the real value of a university education.
- Cheating harms the University's reputation and diminishes the credibility of a University of Sydney degree.

💡 **Why It Matters:** Integrity protects the value of your own degree. When others cheat, your qualification loses credibility too.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2: Plagiarism and AI Use
------------------------------------------------
**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's work, ideas, or intellectual property as your own without proper acknowledgment or permission. It involves copying or closely imitating another's work and claiming it as original. The same principle applies when a computer writes the text.

### Scenario 1: AI-Generated Content Without Attribution
A student uses an AI tool to generate a significant portion of a report, such as entire paragraphs, analyses, or conclusions, and submits it as their own without citing the AI. This could be seen as **plagiarism**, because AI-generated work is presented as the student's original effort.

### Scenario 2: AI as a Writing Aid with Proper Attribution
A student uses AI to brainstorm, check grammar, or generate outlines, then writes the report independently with their own analysis. If they acknowledge the AI use and the final work reflects their own input, this is **not** plagiarism.

| Aspect | Scenario 1 (No attribution) | Scenario 2 (Proper attribution) |
|---|---|---|
| AI's role | Writes paragraphs, analyses, conclusions | Brainstorming, grammar, outlines |
| Student input | Minimal; submitted largely as-is | Independent writing and analysis |
| Acknowledgment | None | AI use disclosed |
| Verdict | Likely plagiarism | Not plagiarism |

🧠 **Memory Hook:** Same tool, opposite verdict. The deciding factor is not *whether* you used AI but *whether you disclosed it and did your own thinking*.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: The Ambiguous Middle Ground
------------------------------------------------
Not every case is clear-cut. The lecture gives a deliberately **ambiguous** scenario to show that responsible-use rules do not always give a yes/no answer.

A student uses AI to generate a summary of research articles for their topic, paraphrases parts of the AI summaries, adds some personal interpretation, and cites the original articles. They submit the report, stating they used AI for "assistance with summarizing sources."

### Ambiguity
Is this acceptable because of the citations and personal analysis, or is it plagiarism because of heavy reliance on AI without explicitly acknowledging the AI's role? It blurs the line between responsible use of AI as a research aid and potential intellectual dishonesty. The outcome depends on institutional policy and expectations about **AI transparency**.

SECTION 4: USyd Two-Lane AI Policy
------------------------------------------------
From **Semester 2, 2025**, the University of Sydney implemented a **two-lane approach** to assessments. The lane decides whether AI use is allowed.

### Lane 1: Secure Assessments
These are in-person, supervised assessments that ensure students have acquired the required skills and knowledge.

- Students are generally **not** permitted to use AI in **secure (supervised)** tasks, such as exams and tests.
- The only exception is if the Unit of Study coordinator gives **express permission** in the Unit Outline.
- Using AI when not allowed could amount to a **breach of academic integrity** and an investigation.

### Lane 2: Open Assessments
These are unsupervised assessments that support the development of disciplinary knowledge and skills.

- They allow students to engage productively and responsibly with AI as part of learning.
- In **open (unsupervised)** assessments, students **will** be able to use AI, provided they appropriately **acknowledge** it.
- If they do so, it is **not** a breach of academic integrity.

| Feature | Lane 1: Secure | Lane 2: Open |
|---|---|---|
| Supervision | In-person, supervised | Unsupervised |
| Examples | Exams, tests | Assignments, reports |
| AI allowed? | Generally no, unless expressly permitted | Yes, with acknowledgment |
| Purpose | Confirm you personally have the skills | Develop disciplinary knowledge and skills |

### Misusing AI at the University of Sydney
Misusing generative AI can breach the Academic Integrity Policy. Examples include:

- Using generative AI such as chatbots, paraphrasing tools, or translation tools where their use has been prohibited.
- Submitting AI-generated work without appropriate acknowledgment.
- Inputting University teaching or course materials, another student's content, Intellectual Property from external partners, or any person's personal or health information into AI tools.

💡 **Why It Matters:** The third example is a privacy rule, not just a cheating rule. Even when AI is allowed, what you *feed into* the tool can be a breach.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 5: Consequences of Breaches
------------------------------------------------
Breaches are not all equal. The University scales the response to the severity and the student's history.

- **Minor Breaches** (for example, poor referencing): Often handled by unit coordinators, involving a mark reduction or required academic honesty training.
- **Serious or Major Breaches** (for example, plagiarism, cheating, collusion): Can lead to a zero for the assessment, failing the unit, or exclusion from the university.
- **Persistent Breaches:** A history of breaches may lead to more severe penalties.
- **Suspension or Expulsion:** Severe cases can mean temporary or permanent removal from the university.
- **Visa or Legal Risks:** International students risk visa cancellation, and contract cheating can lead to blackmail.

SECTION 6: Acknowledging AI Use
------------------------------------------------
If your unit coordinator permits automated writing tools or generative AI, you must **include a statement** in your submitted work explaining your use.

### Required Elements of the Statement
- That you used AI tools in completing the work.
- The **name and version** of the tools used.
- The **publisher**.
- The **uniform resource locator (URL)**.
- A **brief description** of how you used them.
- Plus any additional stipulations your unit coordinator makes.

### More Comprehensive Acknowledgement
When permitted to use AI tools, students **must include**:

1. A clear **acknowledgment statement** detailing AI tool names and versions, publishers and URLs, and how the AI was used (for example, for drafting, editing, brainstorming).
2. An **AI interaction transcript or appendix** demonstrating the student's role and engagement:
  - Sample prompts entered.
  - Responses received.
  - How those exchanges influenced the final work.

**Justification:** This ensures transparency, holds students accountable, and provides verifiable evidence of ethical AI engagement.

```
Decide lane → Learn the rules → Do the work → Acknowledge AI → Attach transcript → Submit
```

SECTION 7: Responsible AI Principles
------------------------------------------------
The lecture lists seven principles for responsible use of AI in higher education. They form a checklist for ethical engagement, not a single rule.

- **Academic Integrity:** Work must stay honest and properly attributed.
- **Disclosure:** You must be transparent about when and how AI was used.
- **Data Privacy:** Personal and sensitive data must be protected.
- **Bias Awareness:** You must recognise that AI outputs can be biased or wrong.
- **Human Oversight:** A person stays responsible for reviewing and directing AI output.
- **Informed Consent:** People affected by AI use should know about and agree to it.
- **Continuous Monitoring:** AI use and its effects should be watched and reviewed over time.

Because specific cases are ambiguous and AI advances rapidly, there must be **ongoing discussion** of the use, principles, and policy of AI in education.

🧠 **Memory Hook:** **ADDBHIC** — "All Data Demands Better Human-Informed Care" — Academic integrity, Disclosure, Data privacy, Bias awareness, Human oversight, Informed consent, Continuous monitoring.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 8: Data Privacy
------------------------------------------------
**Data privacy** is the safeguarding of personal, sensitive, or identifiable information from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction. In AI in higher education, it is critical for ethical and responsible engagement.

| Who | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Students | Be cautious about what they input; avoid sensitive or personally identifiable data unless explicitly permitted and protected. |
| Universities | Select AI tools with clear privacy policies; train staff on data-privacy best practices; set guidelines for ethical AI use. |

### Policy Considerations
- **Third-party AI Platforms:** Many tools run on external servers, raising concerns about data residency, access control, and compliance.
- **Data Breaches:** Institutions must have protocols to identify, respond to, and mitigate breaches involving AI systems.
- **Balancing Privacy and Utility:** Keeping privacy while maintaining AI effectiveness is complex; strategies like **data anonymisation** are essential.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 9: Bias Awareness
------------------------------------------------
Responsible use of AI requires students and educators to recognise that AI tools may **not be neutral or infallible**. Uncritical reliance on biased output can compromise the integrity of research, assessments, or learning, and may spread stereotypes or misinformation.

### Key Elements of Bias Awareness
- **Critical Evaluation of AI Output:** Question AI suggestions rather than accepting them at face value; watch for biased language, stereotypes, or inaccuracies.
- **Understanding Data and Model Limitations:** AI uses training data that may be incomplete or unrepresentative, affecting fairness and accuracy.
- **Promoting Equity and Fairness:** Check whether outputs reflect diverse perspectives and avoid reinforcing inequalities; supplement with diverse viewpoints or corroborate with multiple sources.
- **Ethical Responsibility:** Prevent misuse of biased output, including avoiding discrimination, ensuring neutrality, and advocating for equity.

### Policy Considerations
- **Bias Audits:** Regularly evaluate AI models for biases, especially around protected characteristics such as race, gender, or socioeconomic status.
- **Inclusive Datasets:** Use diverse, representative datasets to minimise biased outputs.
- **Transparency and Explainability:** Promote explainable models that let users understand decisions and spot bias.
- **Training and Awareness:** Educate staff and students to recognise, understand, and address bias in AI.

💡 **Why It Matters:** AI output looks confident even when wrong or skewed. Bias awareness turns "the AI said so" into "is this accurate, fair, and representative?"

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 10: Risks of Over Reliance
------------------------------------------------
Over-reliance on generative AI carries four named risks.

- **Algorithmic Bias:** The model may reproduce unfair patterns from its training data.
- **Impaired Creativity:** Leaning on generated answers can dull your own creative thinking.
- **Reduced Critical Thinking:** If AI does the reasoning, your reasoning skills weaken.
- **Lack of Interpretation:** You may accept output without genuinely interpreting it.

These matter because an assignment's purpose is to build skills, not just produce an answer. If AI produces the answer, the skill is never built. This is **deskilling**.

🧠 **Memory Hook:** **AIRL** — "AI Reduces Learning" — Algorithmic bias, Impaired creativity, Reduced critical thinking, Lack of interpretation.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 11: Seven Responsible Use Rules
------------------------------------------------
The lecture gives seven principles for responsible use of generative AI in homework assignments. Together they form a practical workflow.

1. **Original Thinking and Input:** Independently attempt each task first; only use generative AI after a genuine effort to produce original ideas.
2. **Cite AI-Generated Content Appropriately:** Clearly identify and cite AI content, giving credit to the tool and acknowledging its contribution.
3. **Integrate AI-Generated Content Ethically:** Integrate AI content thoughtfully so it aligns with the task and enhances your understanding rather than overpowering your input.
4. **Review and Edit AI-Generated Content:** Thoroughly review and edit AI content for accuracy, relevance, and coherence, and personalise it to your style.
5. **Respect Academic Honesty Policies:** Follow university and instructor policies; never use AI to create or copy content that violates them, including plagiarism or unauthorized collaboration.
6. **Ask for Clarification from Instructors:** Seek guidance if unsure whether AI use is appropriate or how to use it ethically.
7. **Be Transparent about AI Usage:** If allowed, disclose your AI use and explain how you used it responsibly.

✅ **Exam Tip:** To judge a scenario, test all seven: Did the student try first? Cite? Integrate ethically? Review and edit? Follow policy? Ask when unsure? Be transparent?

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 12: Misuse Scenarios to Avoid
------------------------------------------------
The lecture lists specific ways AI use can breach academic integrity. Learn them so you can spot them in a scenario question.

- **Plagiarism:** Using AI-generated content without proper citation or acknowledgment, presenting it as your own.
- **Misrepresentation of Knowledge and Skills:** Relying solely on AI responses can misrepresent your true understanding, leading to inflated grades and inaccurate perceptions of your abilities.
- **Fabrication of Results or Data:** AI tools that generate data, results, or findings can be misused to fabricate research outcomes, leading to false conclusions.
- **Violating Assignment Instructions:** Using AI content when an assignment explicitly prohibits it violates the instructor's guidelines.
- **Inappropriate Use of AI for Exam Purposes:** Using AI during exams or assessments, directly or indirectly, violates integrity and exam rules.
- **Evading Learning and Understanding:** Over-reliance can hinder critical thinking, problem-solving, and deep understanding.

SECTION 13: Four Case Studies
------------------------------------------------
The lecture uses four students to contrast irresponsible, responsible, ambiguous, and exam-preparation uses of AI.

### Example 1: Alex — Irresponsible Use
Alex, a computer science student, must write a Python assignment independently. Alex uses a code-generating AI to produce the entire codebase, never reviews or understands it, and submits it as their own without citing the AI.

- **Lack of Originality:** Did not show Alex's own thinking or problem-solving.
- **Plagiarism:** AI-generated code submitted as their own without attribution.
- **Misrepresentation of Skills:** Falsely represented Alex's programming ability.
- **Failure to Follow Instructions:** The professor had said to write the code independently.
- **Lack of Transparency:** Alex did not disclose the AI tool.

### Example 2: Dru — Responsible Use
Dru, an NLP student, is assigned a linguistics text-analysis task. Dru uses an AI tool to assist, carefully reviews the generated analysis, incorporates the insights, and cites the AI tool as a reference.

- **Understanding and Learning:** Dru engaged actively with the output to grasp concepts.
- **Supplementary Tool:** The AI aided rather than replaced Dru's critical thinking.
- **Integration and Review:** Dru assessed relevance and accuracy before incorporating.
- **Proper Citation:** Dru cited the AI tool, giving transparency about its contribution.

### Example 3: Deng — Ambiguous Use
Deng, a mathematics student, uses an AI maths solver on a complex calculus problem set, reviews the solutions for alignment with calculus principles, and integrates them.

- **Level of AI Assistance:** It is debated whether the significant help crossed the boundary of acceptable aid.
- **Understanding vs. Tool Reliance:** Some say it undermines understanding; others say it helps grasp complex concepts.
- **Integration and Outcomes:** The debate is whether Deng's validation was sufficient; the professor may accept the AI as an aid and value correct answers, or deem it excessive and raise integrity concerns.

### Example 4: Taylor — AI Study Game for Exam Prep
Taylor, a computer science student, prompts an AI to build a customised study game with questions, quizzes, and interactive challenges for a programming exam.

- **Customised Study Tool:** Shows resourcefulness, but raises whether others have similar access.
- **Potential Unfair Advantage:** If others cannot use AI for customised study aids, Taylor may gain an edge.
- **Access and Outcomes:** Disparities in knowledge and access create an imbalance; the professor may accept it, or adjust the exam or add resources to level the playing field.

💡 **Why It Matters:** The cases build a spectrum. Alex is clearly wrong, Dru clearly right, and Deng and Taylor sit between, depending on policy, intent, and fairness.

SECTION 14: Writing an AI Policy
------------------------------------------------
The lecture's tutorial work asks you to critique existing policies and write your own. A good policy turns vague intentions into clear rules that address **coauthorship** and **automated generation**.

### Tutorial Activities
- Critique the use of AI while writing a report for a course assignment.
- Critique the use of AI while engaging in a technical assignment like writing code.
- Critique the University of Sydney AI policy using the principles of responsible use, and write a new AI policy for the University of Sydney.
- Write an AI policy for this unit of study.

### How to Build a Policy
1. **Identify the assessment type:** Is it secure (supervised) or open (unsupervised)?
2. **Decide permitted uses:** State clearly whether and where AI may be used.
3. **Set disclosure rules:** Require an acknowledgment statement and, where appropriate, an interaction transcript.
4. **Protect privacy:** Ban inputting personal, health, or protected course and partner material into AI tools.
5. **Address bias:** Require critical evaluation, inclusive sources, and human review of AI output.
6. **Set consequences:** Define what happens for minor, major, and persistent breaches.
7. **Keep it under review:** Commit to continuous monitoring as AI and cases evolve.

```
State context → Choose principles → Define allowed uses → Require disclosure → Protect data → Set penalties → Review
```

✅ **Exam Tip:** To critique a policy, map it against the seven responsible principles. Any principle it ignores (often disclosure, data privacy, or bias awareness) is a weakness to point out.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
================================================
1. **Integrity underpins learning** — honest work builds knowledge and self-esteem; cheating gives an unfair advantage and devalues everyone's degree.
2. **Plagiarism is unacknowledged use of others' work** — presenting AI-generated content as your own without attribution.
3. **The same AI tool can be plagiarism or not** — the deciders are disclosure and how much of your own thinking went in.
4. **Some cases are genuinely ambiguous** — when AI's role and yours are tangled, transparency is safest.
5. **The two-lane policy splits secure and open assessments** — secure forbids AI; open allows it with acknowledgment.
6. **Using AI when prohibited is itself a breach** — secure tasks need express permission in the Unit Outline.
7. **Acknowledgment has five elements** — tool name and version, publisher, URL, how it was used, plus any coordinator rule.
8. **A comprehensive acknowledgment adds a transcript** — sample prompts, responses, and their influence on the work.
9. **Consequences scale with severity and history** — from mark reduction to a zero, failing, exclusion, expulsion, and visa or legal risk.
10. **Privacy is separate from attribution** — never input personal, health, protected course, or partner IP into AI tools.
11. **AI is not neutral** — recognise bias, evaluate output critically, and promote equity.

TERMS TO KNOW
================================================
Academic integrity: The commitment to honest, fair scholarship that makes learning and qualifications trustworthy.
Plagiarism: Presenting someone else's work, ideas, or intellectual property as your own without proper acknowledgment or permission.
Generative AI: AI tools, such as chatbots, that produce text or other content in response to prompts.

Secure assessment: An in-person, supervised assessment, such as an exam, where AI is generally not permitted.
Open assessment: An unsupervised assessment where students may use AI provided they acknowledge it.

Deskilling: The loss of skills, such as creativity and critical thinking, caused by letting AI do the work.
Continuous monitoring: The principle that AI use and its effects should be watched and reviewed over time.

CONCRETE EXAMPLES / WORKED SCENARIOS
================================================
### Example 1: Labelling Scenario 1 versus Scenario 2
Two students use the same chatbot on a report. Student A lets it write entire paragraphs, analyses, and conclusions and submits without mentioning AI. Student B uses it only for brainstorming, grammar, and outlines, writes the report independently, and discloses the AI's role.

- Student A: **plagiarism** — AI-generated work presented as original without acknowledgment.
- Student B: **not plagiarism** — the final work reflects their own input and the AI use is acknowledged.

### Example 2: Sorting the ambiguous summary case
A student has AI summarise research articles, paraphrases parts, adds personal interpretation, cites the original articles, and states AI was used for "assistance with summarizing sources."

- Argument for acceptable: proper citations plus personal analysis and a disclosure statement.
- Argument for plagiarism: heavy reliance on AI content without explicit acknowledgment of the AI's full role.
- Lesson: the verdict depends on institutional policy and expectations about **AI transparency**.

### Example 3: Applying the two-lane test
You have two tasks: a supervised in-class test and an unsupervised take-home report.

- In-class test: **Lane 1 (secure)** — no AI unless the Unit Outline gives express permission; using it could trigger an investigation.
- Take-home report: **Lane 2 (open)** — AI allowed, but you must include the acknowledgment statement, and a transcript if required.

### Example 4: Drafting an acknowledgment
Suppose you used ChatGPT (version given by the publisher), published by OpenAI at its URL, to brainstorm and edit your open assessment.

- State that you used AI.
- Name the tool and version, the publisher, and the URL.
- Describe how you used it (for example, brainstorming and editing).
- Attach a transcript with sample prompts, responses, and how they influenced the final work.
