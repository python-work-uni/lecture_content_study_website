ENGG2112 Week 6 – Study Guide
AI Security, Privacy & Deepfakes
================================================

OVERVIEW
--------
This lecture, by Professor Mary Lou Maher, examines AI and security from two directions: using AI to defend systems, and protecting AI systems themselves. It defines **AI for Security** versus **Security of AI**, then shows how AI improves cybersecurity through faster detection and response. Two case studies — DeepMind's **Big Sleep** and Anthropic's **Project Glasswing / Claude Mythos** — show both the promise and danger of AI-driven vulnerability discovery. It then covers **data privacy**, the ethical and legal issues of generative AI, and privacy law (GDPR, CPRA, LGPD), before explaining how **deepfakes** arise from the "correctness" of **GANs** and erode public trust.

SECTION 1: AI for Security vs Security of AI
------------------------------------------------
Everything in this lecture hinges on one distinction: is AI the **tool** protecting something, or the **asset** being protected?

- **AI for Security:** AI is the tool used to enhance security.
- **Security of AI:** AI is the asset being protected.

| Question | AI for Security | Security of AI |
|---|---|---|
| Role of AI | The tool doing the defending | The asset under threat |
| What is protected | Networks, data, systems | The AI model itself |
| Example | AI scanning code for bugs | Attacking or manipulating a model |

🧠 **Memory Hook:** "Tool or Treasure?" If AI does the work, it is *AI for security*. If AI needs protecting, it is *security of AI*.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2: AI in Cybersecurity
------------------------------------------------
**Cybersecurity** uses technologies, processes, and policies to safeguard devices and information from threats like **malware**, **phishing**, and **ransomware**. Effective defence needs multiple layers: physical security, software tools, and user awareness.

### How AI improves security outcomes
AI techniques **detect, prevent, and respond** to threats, and analyse large volumes of data faster than humans. It finds **anomalies and patterns** in:

- Network traffic
- User behaviour
- System logs

Applications include:

- Intrusion and malware detection
- Fraud detection
- Threat intelligence and automated incident response

✅ **Exam Tip:** Cite the three data types AI scans (network traffic, user behaviour, system logs) and the applications (intrusion/malware detection, fraud detection, threat intelligence, automated incident response).

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: Why Cybersecurity Matters to AI Safety
------------------------------------------------
Cybersecurity is central to AI safety because AI both creates and inherits cyber risk.

- **Impact of vulnerabilities:** A single flaw can trigger large-scale exploitation affecting millions across interconnected systems.
- **AI's role in security:** AI automates code analysis and reduces the asymmetry between defenders and attackers.
- **Dual-use risk:** Vulnerability discovery helps defence but is dangerous if weaponised — the core dual-use challenge.
- **Governance and structural risk:** Managing AI requires governance on access and disclosure to address systemic cybersecurity risks.

💡 **Why It Matters:** Because systems are interconnected, one flaw can cascade — which is why cybersecurity is a model for governing AI.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 4: Traditional Security and What AI Adds
------------------------------------------------
Before advanced AI, security relied on humans and could not keep up.

- **Reliance on human expertise:** Skilled engineers manually audited complex source code.
- **Limitations of automated tools:** Static analysis and fuzz testing improved detection but missed subtle, complex vulnerabilities.
- **Vulnerability backlogs:** Many flaws stayed undiscovered for years, increasing risk.
- **Need for AI:** AI emerged to address the scalability and complexity limits of traditional approaches.

### What AI adds
- **Advanced reasoning:** Analyses large codebases like human experts, enabling system-level reasoning beyond pattern matching.
- **Vulnerability discovery:** Uncovers hidden, complex flaws missed by older tools.
- **Double-edged impact:** Can generate exploits useful for defence but also lowers the barrier for attackers.
- **Accelerated tempo:** Compresses the time between discovery and exploitation, so safety measures must be integrated.

⚠️ **Common Trap:** Faster discovery is not purely good — the same speed-up benefits attackers, so AI adoption needs integrated governance.

SECTION 5: Case Study Big Sleep
------------------------------------------------
**Google Project Zero and DeepMind's Big Sleep** is the lecture's flagship positive example of AI for security.

- **AI-augmented discovery:** Enhances human researchers by mimicking hypothesis-driven code analysis and testing in secure environments.
- **Collaborative approach:** Combines expert vulnerability research with advanced AI — interdisciplinary collaboration matters.
- **Safety and responsible use:** Operates in controlled research contexts emphasising defensive use and responsible disclosure.

### Big Sleep in action
- Autonomously found a subtle **memory safety flaw in SQLite**, previously undetected by traditional testing.
- This shows AI can exceed traditional tools, but it requires careful ethical governance.
- Success depended on governance ensuring the flaw was responsibly disclosed and swiftly patched.

### Why it is a positive example
- **Proactive detection:** Found flaws before attackers could exploit them.
- **Restricted trusted access:** Limited to trusted security teams to minimise misuse.
- **Human accountability:** Humans handled validation, disclosure, and remediation.
- **Systemic benefit:** Patching widely used components improved security for many downstream users.

✅ **Exam Tip:** Use Big Sleep for "how can AI be used ethically in security?" — proactive detection, restricted access, human accountability, broad downstream benefit.

SECTION 6: Case Study Project Glasswing
------------------------------------------------
**Anthropic's Project Glasswing** pairs its most capable model, **Claude Mythos**, with a *defender-first* deployment.

- Mythos showed **advanced ability** to discover and chain software vulnerabilities.
- Glasswing **limits model access** to trusted organisations to defensively identify and fix flaws.
- Anthropic shifted from alignment alone to **containment** and controlled deployment.
- It emphasises a **defender-first approach**, differing in scale and coordination from similar projects.

### What Glasswing is
- **Defensive initiative:** Uses AI to proactively find and fix vulnerabilities before attackers exploit them.
- **Collaborative sharing:** Scan findings are responsibly shared with maintainers for timely patches.
- **Collective defence:** Fosters cooperation to strengthen the whole digital ecosystem.

### Who is involved
- **Industry, open source, and finance:** Major tech companies form the backbone, the **Linux Foundation** protects software maintained by small teams, and firms like **JPMorgan Chase** secure critical financial systems.
- **Cross-sector collaboration:** Shows the need to work across sectors against AI-driven threats.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 7: Claude Mythos and Its Findings
------------------------------------------------
**Claude Mythos** is a powerful, **unreleased** AI that uncovers deep vulnerabilities across major systems and browsers.

### The superhacker narrative
- Anthropic restricts Mythos's access and directs it to defensive cybersecurity via Glasswing; Mythos shifts the balance between attackers and defenders, raising trust, governance, and resilience challenges.
- **Code analysis and discovery:** Finds bugs and turns them into functional exploits, and found vulnerabilities across major operating systems and web browsers, surpassing earlier AI.
- **Restricted, paradigm-challenging model:** A gated tool available only to trusted partners that challenges current AI safety and cybersecurity paradigms.

### Major vulnerabilities found
- A **27-year-old** flaw in **OpenBSD**, despite its strong security reputation.
- A **16-year-old** flaw in **FFmpeg**, a widely used multimedia suite.
- **Linux kernel exploit chain:** Chained multiple kernel vulnerabilities to gain full system control.

ℹ️ **Note:** OpenBSD is a secure Unix-like OS; FFmpeg is an open-source multimedia suite; Linux is the kernel created by Linus Torvalds in 1991.

### Why the tech world is alarmed
- **Scale and speed:** AI uncovers hidden flaws and analyses vast codebases rapidly, lowering the effort for sophisticated attacks.
- **Widespread impact and urgency:** Flaws in common components cascade across millions of systems, so cybersecurity must evolve before offence surpasses defence.

### Autonomy, risk, and restriction
- **Low autonomous risk:** Mythos is unlikely to act independently or develop malicious goals.
- **Human-directed misuse is the real danger:** The main risk is how effectively it performs harmful tasks when directed by humans.
- **Why it stays restricted:** Capabilities are too risky for public release without safeguards; restriction stops malicious actors, reflects responsible stewardship over commercial release, and uses access control, auditing, and usage restrictions to keep Glasswing ethical and defensive.

⚠️ **Common Trap:** Do not call Mythos "self-aware" or malicious. The danger is **human-directed misuse**, not autonomous malice.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 8: Governing Safe Defensive AI
------------------------------------------------
The Mythos case drives a governance shift: from managing AI as tools to managing AI as **capabilities**.

### From tools to capabilities
- **Beyond tools:** Governance must target underlying capabilities, not just outputs; post-hoc fixes are too slow, so AI needs **proactive** governance.
- **Core mechanisms:** Access control, capability evaluation, and pre-release decision-making.
- **Cybersecurity as model:** Its consequences are immediate and severe, so strict governance is justified.

### Design principles for safe defensive AI
- **Restricted access and control:** Limit access to powerful systems to prevent misuse.
- **Human oversight and accountability:** Supervision at critical decision points catches errors early.
- **Responsible disclosure norms:** Promote transparency in communicating vulnerabilities and updates.
- **Avoiding unsafe anti-patterns:** Unrestricted public release or no rollback undermines trust.

### Implications
- **Rapid advances, responsible stewardship:** Cybersecurity AI is progressing faster than many experts predicted, and limiting access contrasts with typical fast industry deployment.
- **Proactive defence and governance:** Initiatives like Glasswing align AI with defensive goals, and outcomes hinge on decisions about access, collaboration, and responsibility.

✅ **Exam Tip:** Remember "**beyond alignment**" — alignment alone is not enough; capability **containment** and institutional responsibility are essential.

SECTION 9: Implications for Engineers and Education
------------------------------------------------
AI safety changes what engineers are expected to do: technical skill alone is not enough.

### For engineers and researchers
- **Beyond technical skills:** Combine expertise with judgement about what to build or restrict.
- **Ethical dilemmas:** Discovering vulnerabilities or powerful methods raises responsibility questions.
- **Responsible practice:** Consider downstream impacts, access control, and institutional collaboration.
- **Professionalism through restraint:** Knowing when to refuse or restrain deployment is a mark of professionalism.

### For AI safety education
- **Comprehensive training:** Learn to build models *and* understand failure modes, risk analysis, and governance; cybersecurity offers accessible, accepted lessons on misuse consequences.
- **Integrated, case-based learning:** Blend technical, institutional, and ethical views, grounded in real-world cases rather than abstract theory.

💡 **Why It Matters:** Governance, oversight, and restraint matter as much as technical skill — knowing when not to deploy is part of being a professional.

SECTION 10: AI Privacy Foundations
------------------------------------------------
**AI privacy** means protecting individuals' data used in AI systems from misuse or unintended disclosure.

### Concerns
- Data leakage during training/testing
- Identity privacy of individuals in data sets
- Risk of re-identification from generated outputs

### Challenges to privacy in AI
- **Data collection and use:** Large datasets may contain personal information, sourced from public or private sources without consent.
- **Model training risks:** Sensitive data embedded in model weights; personal data extracted from trained models via **model inversion attacks**; generation of harmful content.
- **Ethical and legal:** Consent for using individuals' images/data, and accountability for malicious use.

🧠 **Memory Hook:** Privacy leaks at three moments — **in the data** (collection), **in the model** (weights and inversion), and **in the output** (re-identification).

⚠️ **Common Trap:** "It was public data" is not a defence. Consent is a separate ethical requirement from whether data was publicly available.

SECTION 11: Privacy Laws GDPR CPRA LGPD
------------------------------------------------
Widespread generative AI makes these legal frameworks central, because organisations process enormous amounts of personal data.

- **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR):** Enacted in the EU on **25 May 2018**. One of the strictest and most influential privacy laws worldwide; it applies to organisations anywhere if they target or collect data related to EU residents.
- **California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA):** Governs data privacy rights, passed in 2018.
- **Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (LGPD):** Passed by Brazil's National Congress in 2018.

| Law | Region | Year | Reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| GDPR | European Union | 2018 | Worldwide if EU residents are targeted |
| CPRA | California, USA | 2018 | Californian data privacy rights |
| LGPD | Brazil | 2018 | Brazilian national regulation |

✅ **Exam Tip:** GDPR is the only law here with explicit extraterritorial reach — it applies anywhere if EU residents' data is processed.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 12: GDPR Principles and Rights
------------------------------------------------
GDPR rests on principles governing *how* data is handled, plus rights empowering the *individual*.

### Core principles
- **Lawfulness, Fairness, and Transparency:** Processing must be legal, fair, and clear to the individual.
- **Purpose Limitation:** Data collected only for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes.
- **Data Minimization:** Only necessary data should be collected.
- **Accuracy:** Personal data must be kept accurate and up to date.
- **Storage Limitation:** Data retained only as long as necessary.
- **Integrity and Confidentiality:** Data processed securely to prevent unauthorised access or loss.

### Key rights for data subjects
- **Right to be Informed:** Know how your data is used.
- **Right of Access:** Request a copy of your data.
- **Right to Rectification:** Correct inaccurate data.
- **Right to Erasure** ("Right to be Forgotten"): Request deletion under specific conditions.
- **Right to Data Portability:** Transfer your data to another provider.
- **Right to Object:** Stop your data being used for specific purposes like marketing.

🧠 **Memory Hook:** Principles are about the **data** (how it is handled); rights are about the **person** (what they can demand).

SECTION 13: How GANs Create Deepfakes
------------------------------------------------
**Deepfakes** are AI-generated realistic images or videos of people, created using generative models, often **GANs**.

### Deepfakes as a privacy concern
- Unauthorised use of individuals' likenesses
- Potential defamation, blackmail, misinformation
- Difficulty removing deepfake data from the internet

### The two competing models
- **Generator:** Produces synthetic data (images, audio, video, text); its goal is to look *real*.
- **Discriminator:** Decides whether inputs are *real* (from training data) or *fake* (from the generator); its goal is to catch fakes.

### The adversarial training loop
- The cycle of generator errors, discriminator detection, and feedback repeats thousands or millions of times.
- Over time the discriminator becomes *less able to tell the difference*, and the generator's outputs become *increasingly realistic*.
- When the discriminator can no longer reliably distinguish real from fake, the generator is **successful**.

### Deepfakes as a by-product of error reduction
GANs learn fine-grained patterns in faces, voices, gestures, and speech; reduce artifacts that reveal manipulation; and optimise specifically for **human perceptual realism**. As error approaches zero: fake faces look real, synthetic voices sound authentic, fabricated videos convince.

✅ **Exam Tip:** A deepfake is not a *failure* of the GAN — it is the GAN succeeding at its objective of perceptual realism.

------------------------------------------------
SECTION 14: Misinformation Disinformation and Trust
------------------------------------------------
A technically "correct" GAN can cause serious social harm — accidentally, then deliberately, then by destroying trust.

### Misinformation (unintentional harm)
GAN content can be used mistakenly as real, shared without malicious intent, and believed because it *looks and sounds right*. Examples: fake images in news reporting, altered videos on social media, synthetic audio misattributed to real people.

### Disinformation (deliberate harm)
High-credibility fakes are powerful tools for intentional deception: political manipulation, fake evidence or fabricated statements, impersonation and scams, and erosion of trust in genuine media. Here, low error equals **high persuasive power**.

| Aspect | Misinformation | Disinformation |
|---|---|---|
| Intent | Unintentional | Deliberate |
| Mechanism | Mistaken belief, accidental sharing | Targeted deception |
| Examples | Fake news images, misattributed audio | Political manipulation, scams, fake evidence |

### Scenario: correctness to loss of trust
A media company trains a **GAN video system** on thousands of hours of news footage to make fast explainers, targeting *the discriminator can no longer tell real from generated*. In breaking news it fabricates an official's "likely" statement from past speeches, voice patterns, and facial movements; the clip looks and sounds real. Malicious actors copy it, releasing fabricated statements timed to influence opinion — **disinformation**. Once exposed, a correction cannot undo the damage: real footage is questioned, genuine evidence dismissed as "probably fake", and trust erodes.

### Correctness optimises the wrong thing
- Correctness = low detectable error
- Success = indistinguishability from reality
- There is no concept of truth, intent, or context

⚠️ **Common Trap:** The problem is not that the GAN malfunctioned. It achieved exactly what it was optimised for — the system performs exactly as designed, but that objective omits truth, intent, and context.

🧠 **Memory Hook:** Misinformation = **mistake**. Disinformation = **deliberate**. Both end in **distrust**.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
================================================
1. **AI for Security vs Security of AI** — In AI for security, AI is the tool protecting systems; in security of AI, the AI itself is the asset being protected.
2. **Cybersecurity needs multiple layers** — Technologies, processes, and policies combine with physical security, software tools, and user awareness to counter malware, phishing, and ransomware.
3. **AI scales cyber defence** — AI detects, prevents, and responds by analysing network traffic, user behaviour, and system logs faster than humans, enabling intrusion/malware detection, fraud detection, threat intelligence, and automated incident response.
4. **Cybersecurity is central to AI safety** — One flaw can cascade across interconnected systems; vulnerability discovery is dual-use, and AI both reduces and widens the defender–attacker asymmetry, so access and disclosure must be governed.
5. **AI solves traditional limits** — Human auditing, static analysis, and fuzz testing missed subtle flaws and left backlogs; AI adds system-level reasoning and deeper discovery.
6. **Big Sleep is the positive model** — DeepMind's Big Sleep autonomously found a SQLite memory-safety zero-day, proactively, with restricted trusted access, human accountability, and systemic benefit.
7. **Glasswing is defender-first** — Anthropic limits access to Claude Mythos and directs it to find and fix flaws, sharing findings responsibly with maintainers.
8. **Mythos shows offensive power** — It found a 27-year-old OpenBSD flaw, a 16-year-old FFmpeg flaw, and chained Linux kernel bugs for full system control; the real danger is human-directed misuse, not autonomy.
9. **Governance must target capabilities** — Post-hoc fixes are too slow, so use access control, capability evaluation, pre-release decisions, restricted access, oversight, disclosure norms, and avoid unsafe anti-patterns.
10. **Privacy has three leak points** — Collection without consent, sensitive data in model weights (model inversion), and re-identification from outputs.
11. **Privacy law is global** — GDPR (EU, 25 May 2018, extraterritorial), CPRA (California, 2018), and LGPD (Brazil, 2018) define data subject rights.
12. **Deepfakes are GAN correctness, not failure** — As adversarial error approaches zero, outputs become indistinguishable from reality, optimised for perceptual realism with no concept of truth, intent, or context; harm escalates from misinformation to disinformation to distrust.

TERMS TO KNOW
================================================
AI for Security: AI used as the tool to enhance security.
Security of AI: AI treated as the asset being protected.
Cybersecurity: Technologies, processes, and policies that safeguard devices and information from threats.
Anomaly detection: Finding unusual patterns in network traffic, user behaviour, or system logs that may signal an attack.
Dual-use: The property that the same AI capability can aid defenders or attackers.
Containment: Restricting a powerful AI's access and use rather than relying on alignment alone.
Big Sleep: Google Project Zero and DeepMind's AI that autonomously found a SQLite memory-safety zero-day.
Claude Mythos: Anthropic's powerful, unreleased, gated model that discovers and chains software vulnerabilities.
Project Glasswing: Anthropic's defender-first initiative limiting model access to trusted organisations.
Model inversion attack: Extracting personal data from a trained model by probing it.
Re-identification: Recovering an individual's identity from supposedly anonymous data or generated outputs.
Deepfake: AI-generated realistic images or videos of people, often made with GANs.
Generative Adversarial Network: A pair of neural networks, a generator and a discriminator, trained together.
Generator: The GAN network that produces synthetic data aiming to look real.
Discriminator: The GAN network that judges whether inputs are real or fake.
Misinformation: False content spread unintentionally.
Disinformation: False content shared intentionally to mislead.
