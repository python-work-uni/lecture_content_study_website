    AI in Cybersecurity

    Cybersecurity uses a combination of technologies, processes, and policies to safeguard devices and information from threats like malware, phishing, and ransomware.

    Effective cybersecurity involves multiple layers of defense, including physical security, software tools, and user awareness to mitigate risks.

    How AI improves security outcomes

    • Uses AI techniques to detect, prevent, and respond to threats
    • Analyses large volumes of data faster than humans
    • Identifies anomalies and patterns in:
      • Network traffic
      • User behaviour
      • System Logs
    • Applications include:
      • Intrusion and malware detection
      • Fraud detection
      • Threat intelligence and automated incident response

    Why Cybersecurity Is Central to AI Safety

    Impact of Vulnerabilities
    A single cybersecurity flaw can trigger large-scale exploitation affecting millions across interconnected systems.
    AI's Role in Security
    AI enhances cybersecurity by automating code analysis and reducing asymmetry between defenders and attackers.
    Dual-Use Risk
    Vulnerability discovery is valuable for defense but risky if weaponized, illustrating dual-use challenges in AI safety.
    Governance and Structural Risk
    Managing AI capabilities requires governance on access and disclosure to address systemic cybersecurity risks effectively.

    Traditional Cybersecurity Before Advanced AI

    Reliance on Human Expertise
    Traditional cybersecurity depended heavily on skilled engineers manually auditing complex source code.
    Limitations of Automated Tools
    Static analysis and fuzz testing improved detection but missed subtle and complex vulnerabilities.
    Challenges and Vulnerability Backlogs
    Traditional methods left many vulnerabilities undiscovered for years, increasing security risks.
    Need for AI Integration
    AI emerged to address scalability and complexity limits inherent in traditional security approaches.

    What AI Adds to Cybersecurity Capabilities

    Advanced AI Reasoning
    Modern AI analyzes large codebases like human experts, enabling system-level reasoning beyond pattern matching.
    Vulnerability Discovery
    AI uncovers hidden and complex vulnerabilities missed by traditional automated tools, improving threat detection.
    Double-Edged Impact
    AI can generate exploits useful for defense but also lowers barriers for attackers, posing safety challenges.
    Accelerated Cybersecurity Tempo
    AI compresses the time between vulnerability discovery and exploitation, necessitating integrated safety measures.

    AI Privacy

    Protecting individuals' data used in AI systems from misuse or unintended disclosure.

    Understanding AI Privacy

    Purpose: Protecting individuals' data used in AI systems from misuse or unintended disclosure

    Concerns

    • Data leakage during training/testing
    • Identity privacy of individuals in data sets
    • Risk of re-identification from generated outputs

    Challenges to Privacy in AI

    Data Collection and Use

    • Large datasets may contain personal information
    • Data sourced from public or private sources without consent

    Model Training Risks

    • Embedding sensitive data in model weights
    • Extracting personal data from trained models (model inversion attacks)
    • Generation of Harmful Content

    Ethical and Legal Considerations

    Ethical Challenges:

    • Consent for using individuals' images/data
    • Accountability for malicious use

    Regulations & Laws:

    • General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
      • Enacted in the European Union (EU) on May 25, 2018.
      • One of the strictest and most influential privacy laws worldwide.
      • Applies to organizations anywhere, if they target or collect data related to EU residents.
    • California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA) governing data privacy rights passed in 2018
    • The Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados, or LGPD, is a data privacy regulation passed by the National Congress of Brazil in 2018.

    GDPR Core Principles

    Lawfulness, Fairness, and Transparency
    Data processing must be legal, fair, and clear to the individual.
    Purpose Limitation
    Data collected only for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes.
    Data Minimization
    Only necessary data should be collected.
    Accuracy
    Personal data must be kept accurate and up-to-date.
    Storage Limitation
    Data should only be retained as long as necessary.
    Integrity and Confidentiality
    Data must be processed securely to prevent unauthorized access/loss.

    GDPR: Key Rights for Data Subjects

    Right to be Informed
    Know how your data is used.
    Right of Access
    Request a copy of your data.
    Right to Rectification
    Correct inaccurate data.
    Right to Erasure
    ("Right to be Forgotten"): Request deletion of your data under specific conditions.
    Right to Data Portability
    Transfer your data to another provider.
    Right to Object
    Stop your data from being used for specific purposes like marketing.

    GDPR vs CCPA vs LGPD: Key Rights Comparison

    GDPR Comprehensive Rights
    GDPR provides extensive rights including data access, correction, erasure, restriction, objection, portability, and automated decision protections.
    CCPA Consumer Focus
    CCPA emphasizes consumer transparency, choice, data deletion, opt-out of data sale, and non-discrimination protections.
    LGPD Alignment and Transparency
    LGPD aligns with GDPR, focusing on data access, correction, anonymization, portability, consent revocation, and clear transparency.

    Deepfakes as a Privacy Concern

    What are Deepfakes?

    • AI-generated realistic images or videos of people
    • Created using generative models, often GANs

    Privacy Concerns

    • Unauthorized use of individuals' likenesses
    • Potential defamation, blackmail, misinformation
    • Difficulty in removing deepfake data from the internet

    Adversarial Networks

    Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) — the mechanics behind deepfakes.

    The Two Competing Models

    GANs consist of two neural networks trained together:

    Generator
    • Produces synthetic data (images, audio, video, text)
    • Goal: create outputs that look real
    Discriminator
    • Evaluates inputs and decides whether they are real (from training data) or fake (from the generator)
    • Goal: correctly identify fakes

    The Adversarial Training Loop

    • The generator makes errors (produces outputs the discriminator can spot)
    • The discriminator detects those errors
    • Feedback is used to reduce errors in the generator
    • This cycle repeats thousands or millions of times

    Over time:

    • The discriminator becomes less able to tell the difference
    • The generator's outputs become increasingly realistic
    Key idea When the discriminator can no longer reliably distinguish real from fake, the generator is considered successful.

    Deep Fakes as a By-Product of Error Reduction

    Deep fakes emerge because GANs:

    • Learn fine-grained patterns in faces, voices, gestures, and speech
    • Reduce visual or auditory artifacts that reveal manipulation
    • Optimise specifically for human perceptual realism

    As error approaches zero:

    • Fake faces look real
    • Synthetic voices sound authentic
    • Fabricated videos appear convincing

    Risks: Misinformation (Unintentional Harm)

    GAN-generated content can be:

    • Used mistakenly as real
    • Shared without malicious intent
    • Believed because it looks and sounds right

    Examples:

    • Fake images used in news reporting
    • Altered videos shared on social media
    • Synthetic audio misattributed to real people

    Risks: Disinformation (Deliberate Harm)

    Because adversarial networks can produce high-credibility fakes, they are powerful tools for intentional deception:

    • Political manipulation
    • Fake evidence or fabricated statements
    • Impersonation and scams
    • Erosion of trust in genuine media
    Warning Here, low error equals high persuasive power.

    The Deeper Risk: Loss of Trust

    As adversarial realism improves:

    • People can no longer easily trust what they see or hear
    • Genuine evidence can be dismissed as "just another deep fake"

    Scenario: From Adversarial Correctness to Loss of Trust

    A media company uses a GAN-based video system to generate realistic video content for fast news explainers and reconstructions.

    The system was trained on thousands of hours of real news footage.

    Its goal is to reduce visual and audio errors so generated videos are indistinguishable from real recordings.

    "Correctness" is defined internally as:

    The discriminator can no longer tell real footage from generated footage

    During a breaking news event, the system generates a video of a public official making a short statement that reconstructs what was "likely said," based on:

    • Past speeches
    • Voice patterns
    • Facial movements

    The generated video:

    • Looks real
    • Sounds real
    • Matches known communication style

    Disinformation becomes possible

    • Later, malicious actors realise:
      • They can generate similar videos with targeted messages
      • The system's strength—minimal error—makes deception easy

    They deliberately:

    • Release fabricated statements
    • Time releases to influence public opinion
    • Rely on realism to bypass scepticism

    This becomes disinformation:

    • False content
    • Shared intentionally
    • Designed to mislead

    Trust Begins to Collapse

    Once the deep fake is exposed:

    • The company issues a correction
    • But damage is already done

    Public reaction shifts:

    • "You can't trust video anymore"
    • Real footage is questioned
    • Genuine evidence is dismissed as "probably fake"

    This leads to:

    • Erosion of trust in media
    • Confusion about what is real
    • Increased cynicism toward legitimate information

    Adversarial AI "Correctness" Optimises the Wrong Thing

    • Correctness = low detectable error
    • Success = indistinguishability from reality
    • There is no concept of truth, intent, or context
    Key idea The system performs exactly as designed.
