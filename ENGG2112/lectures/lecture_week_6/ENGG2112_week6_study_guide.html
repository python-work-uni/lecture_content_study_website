AI Security and Privacy: When AI Defends, When It Threatens

This week you look at AI and security from both directions: AI as the tool that protects systems, and AI as the asset that must itself be protected. Along the way you meet two real vulnerability-hunting systems, the privacy laws that decide what data is fair game, and the strange way a GAN succeeding at its job produces deepfakes.

Read this first: Almost everything this week hangs on one question — is AI doing the defending, or being defended? Once you can answer that, the pieces click together: cybersecurity gives you the language of defence, governance decides who may even touch a powerful model, privacy law decides whose data can be used, and GANs show what happens when "correctness" is defined as fooling people instead of telling the truth.
~20 min read 16 teaching sections 15 practice questions 28 key terms

By the end you will be able to

  • Distinguish AI for security from security of AI, and explain why the difference matters.
  • Describe what AI adds to cybersecurity and why vulnerability discovery is dual-use.
  • Summarise the Big Sleep and Project Glasswing / Claude Mythos cases, including the flaws each found.
  • Explain the governance shift from managing AI "tools" to managing AI "capabilities".
  • Name the three privacy leak points in AI and the laws — GDPR, CPRA, LGPD — that govern them.
  • Explain how a GAN works and why a deepfake is a by-product of correctness, not a malfunction.

The Big Picture

One lecture, three connected stories: AI as defender, AI as danger, and the people whose data and faces get caught in the middle.

Professor Mary Lou Maher's lecture is really an argument in three movements. First, AI becomes a tool for defending computer systems — scanning code, spotting attacks. Second, as AI gets powerful, it becomes something worth attacking and controlling in its own right. Third, the data and likenesses that all this AI depends on raise privacy and trust questions that no engineering fix alone can settle.

The thread that ties the whole week together is governance — the decisions about who gets access to a capability, who audits it, and when it is too risky to release at all.

AI defends systems→ AI becomes an asset→ Governance & containment→ Privacy & law→ Deepfakes & trust

AI for Security vs Security of AI

The single most examinable distinction in the lecture: is AI the tool doing the protecting, or the thing being protected?

The lecture opens by splitting one phrase into two. Both halves use the words "AI" and "security", but the AI sits on opposite sides of the relationship.

AI for Security — AI is the tool used to enhance security. The AI does the defending; networks, data and systems are what is protected.
Security of AI — AI is the asset being protected. Now the AI model itself is the thing under threat, and someone is trying to attack, manipulate or steal from it.

AI for Security (AI = tool)

  • AI scans code and traffic for threats.
  • What is protected: networks, data, systems.
  • Example: an AI finding a bug before attackers do.

Security of AI (AI = asset)

  • Someone attacks or manipulates the model.
  • What is protected: the AI model itself.
  • Example: probing a model to extract its training data.
T · T
"Tool or Treasure?"
If AI is the Tool doing the work, it is AI for security. If AI is the Treasure needing protection, it is security of AI.
⚠️
Common trap: The same AI model can sit on both sides. Use it to scan code and it is AI for security; probe it to steal data and that is security of AI. The role depends on the situation, not the model.

AI in Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is layered defence; AI makes each layer faster and more thorough than a human team could be.

Cybersecurity — the combination of technologies, processes and policies used to safeguard devices and information from threats such as malware, phishing and ransomware.

Effective cybersecurity is not one product but multiple layers of defence, because any single layer can fail. Think of it like a house: a locked door is useless if the window is open and a stranger is already inside.

LayerWhat it covers
Physical securityLocks, access to buildings and hardware.
Software toolsDetection, filtering and monitoring systems.
User awarenessPeople who recognise and avoid threats like phishing.

How AI improves security outcomes

AI techniques are used to detect, prevent and respond to threats. Their core advantage is scale: they analyse large volumes of data faster than humans and surface anomalies and patterns that would otherwise be buried. They look in three places in particular:

🌐 Network traffic

Unusual connections or data flows that hint at a breach.

🧑 User behaviour

Odd login times or actions that suggest a compromised account.

🗒️ System logs

Records of events that reveal abnormal activity over time.

Where it is applied

  • Intrusion and malware detection — catching attackers and malicious software in the act.
  • Fraud detection — flagging suspicious transactions or behaviour.
  • Threat intelligence and automated incident response — understanding threats and reacting to them automatically.

This framing is adapted from Fortinet's cybersecurity glossary (fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary).

✅
Do this: Memorise the trio of data sources — network traffic, user behaviour, system logs — and the four applications. These are easy marks because they are a fixed list.

Why Cybersecurity Is Central to AI Safety

Cybersecurity is a concrete case study of AI safety, because AI both creates and inherits risk in a world of connected systems.

The lecture gives four reasons cybersecurity matters so much to the wider field of AI safety.

🧨 Impact of vulnerabilities

A single cybersecurity flaw can trigger large-scale exploitation affecting millions of people across interconnected systems. One crack, many dominoes.

🤝 AI's role in security

AI enhances cybersecurity by automating code analysis and reducing the asymmetry between defenders and attackers — historically, attackers only needed one opening while defenders had to close them all.

↔️ Dual-use risk

Vulnerability discovery is valuable for defence but dangerous if weaponised. The same capability serves both sides — the core dual-use challenge in AI safety.

🏛️ Governance and structural risk

Managing AI capabilities requires governance over access and disclosure to address systemic cybersecurity risk. Technical skill alone is not enough.

💡
Why it matters: Because systems are interconnected, one flaw can cascade. That is exactly why cybersecurity has become a model for how to govern AI: the consequences are immediate, severe, and shared.
🤔

From Traditional Security to AI

Old security depended on human experts and imperfect automation; AI was brought in to close the gap it could never fully close.

Traditional cybersecurity before advanced AI

  • Reliance on human expertise: skilled engineers manually audited complex source code, one file at a time.
  • Limitations of automated tools: static analysis and fuzz testing improved detection but missed subtle and complex vulnerabilities.
  • Challenges and vulnerability backlogs: traditional methods left many flaws undiscovered for years, quietly increasing risk.
  • Need for AI integration: AI emerged to address the scalability and complexity limits inherent in the old approach.

What AI adds to cybersecurity capabilities

Old way

  • Humans read code by hand.
  • Tools pattern-match known issues.
  • Subtle flaws survive for years.

AI-augmented way

  • Analyses huge codebases like a human expert.
  • System-level reasoning beyond pattern matching.
  • Uncovers hidden, complex vulnerabilities.
  • Advanced AI reasoning — reasons about a whole system, not just matching known signatures.
  • Vulnerability discovery — finds flaws traditional tools missed, improving threat detection.
  • Double-edged impact — can generate exploits that help defenders, but also lowers the barrier for attackers.
  • Accelerated cybersecurity tempo — compresses the time between a flaw being discovered and being exploited, so safety measures must be built in, not bolted on later.
⚠️
Common trap: Faster discovery is not purely good news. The same speed-up benefits attackers, which is why adopting AI in security without governance can leave you worse off.

Case Study: Google Project Zero and DeepMind's Big Sleep

The lecture's flagship positive example: an AI that proactively hunts vulnerabilities with humans firmly in charge.

Big Sleep is a collaboration between Google's Project Zero security researchers and DeepMind. It is AI-augmented vulnerability discovery: it enhances human researchers by mimicking hypothesis-driven code analysis and testing inside secure environments. Two things define it:

  • Collaborative and interdisciplinary: it combines expert vulnerability research with advanced AI — a reminder that the human experts are not replaced.
  • Safety and responsible use: it operates in controlled research contexts, emphasising defensive use and responsible vulnerability disclosure.

Big Sleep in action: a real zero-day

Big Sleep autonomously found a subtle memory-safety flaw in SQLite that traditional testing had missed. A zero-day is a flaw unknown to the people who could fix it, so finding one before attackers do is enormously valuable.

The discovery proved AI can exceed traditional security tools, but it also showed that this power demands ethical governance. The flaw was responsibly disclosed and swiftly patched through a coordinated process.

Why this is a positive AI safety example

🛡️ Proactive detection

Used to identify vulnerabilities before attackers could exploit them.

🔐 Restricted trusted access

Access limited to trusted security teams to minimise misuse.

🧑 Human accountability

Humans were responsible for validation, disclosure and remediation.

🌍 Systemic benefit

Patching a widely used component improved security for many downstream users.

✅
Do this: For any "is this good AI use?" question, reach for these four points: proactive detection, restricted access, human accountability, systemic benefit.

Case Study: Anthropic's Project Glasswing

Same discovery power as before, but deliberately pointed only at defence — and kept on a short leash.

Claude Mythos is Anthropic's model that demonstrated an advanced ability to discover and chain software vulnerabilities. Rather than release it, Anthropic built Project Glasswing, which limits model access to trusted organisations so it can defensively identify and fix vulnerabilities.

This marks a philosophical shift: Anthropic moved focus from alignment alone to containment and controlled deployment for safer AI use. Glasswing emphasises a defender-first approach, differing in scale and coordination from similar projects. (The lecture references an article in The Conversation, 13 April 2026: "Claude Mythos and Project Glasswing: Why an AI Superhacker Has the Tech World on Alert".)

What Project Glasswing is

🛡️ Defensive initiative

Uses AI to proactively identify and fix vulnerabilities before attackers exploit them.

📤 Collaborative sharing

Scan findings are responsibly shared with maintainers so timely patches can be built.

🌐 Collective digital defence

Fosters cooperation among organisations to strengthen the whole digital ecosystem.

Who is involved

  • Tech industry leaders — major tech companies form the backbone, addressing worldwide systemic risk.
  • Open-source organisations — groups such as the Linux Foundation help protect essential software maintained by small teams.
  • Financial institutions — firms such as JPMorgan Chase participate to secure critical financial systems.
  • Cross-sector collaboration — the point of Glasswing is that no single sector can fight advanced AI-driven threats alone.
ℹ️
Key idea: The power of Mythos did not change; the governance wrapping it did. Glasswing is the controlled environment that keeps a dangerous capability pointed at defence.

Claude Mythos and What It Found

The "AI superhacker" narrative: a gated model that finds deep flaws across major systems — and the reason the danger is human-directed, not autonomous.

The lecture frames Claude Mythos as a powerful unreleased AI that uncovers deep software vulnerabilities across major systems and browsers. It is a gated tool, available only to trusted partners, reflecting concern about its offensive potential.

Claude Mythos — an advanced code-analysis model that finds bugs and can transform them into functional exploits. It identifies vulnerabilities across major operating systems and web browsers, surpassing earlier AI capabilities, and challenges current AI safety and cybersecurity paradigms.

Examples of major vulnerabilities found

TargetWhat Mythos found
OpenBSDA 27-year-old flaw — remarkable because OpenBSD is known for a strong security reputation. (OpenBSD is a free, secure, portable Unix-like OS based on 4.4BSD, focused on proactive security, code correctness and integrated cryptography.)
FFmpegA 16-year-old flaw, highlighting hidden risk in widely used software. (FFmpeg is a free, open-source suite and industry-standard command-line tool for recording, converting, streaming and editing multimedia.)
Linux kernelChained multiple kernel vulnerabilities to gain full system control — an advanced attack method. (Linux is the open-source kernel created by Linus Torvalds in 1991, managing CPU, memory and devices.)

Why the tech world is alarmed

  • AI accelerates cyberattacks — it quickly uncovers hidden flaws that humans and traditional tools miss.
  • Scale and efficiency — unlike human hackers, AI analyses vast codebases rapidly, lowering the effort for sophisticated attacks.
  • Widespread impact risk — flaws in common software components risk cascading effects across millions of systems worldwide.
  • Urgency for countermeasures — cybersecurity must evolve before offensive capabilities surpass defences.

Can Mythos act autonomously?

The lecture is careful here. The risk of autonomous harm is low: Mythos is unlikely to act independently in harmful ways or develop its own malicious goals. The real danger is human-directed misuse — how effectively it performs harmful tasks when a person directs it. That is why access control, auditing and usage restrictions are described as crucial, and why Glasswing provides a controlled setting.

⚠️
Common trap: Do not call Mythos "self-aware" or malicious. The danger is human-directed misuse, not autonomous malice. Getting this wrong loses marks.

Governing Safe Defensive AI

The Mythos case forces a shift in thinking: stop governing AI as a product and start governing it as a capability.

From tools to capabilities

  • Governance beyond tools: AI governance must focus on underlying capabilities, not just tools and outputs, to manage risk effectively.
  • Limitations of post-hoc mitigation: traditional software fixes harms after release, but AI vulnerabilities require proactive governance measures.
  • Central governance mechanisms: access control, capability evaluation, and pre-release decision-making are essential for safe deployment.
  • Cybersecurity as a model: because misuse consequences can be immediate and severe, strict governance is justified.

Design principles for safe defensive AI

R · O · A · D
"Restrict, Oversee, Announce, Deny"
Restricted access and control · Oversight and human accountability · Announce (responsible disclosure norms) · Deny unsafe anti-patterns.

🔐 Restricted access and control

Safe AI design mandates restricted access to powerful systems to prevent misuse and unauthorised exploitation.

🧑 Human oversight and accountability

Human supervision at critical AI decision points catches errors early and keeps someone answerable.

📢 Responsible disclosure norms

Adhering to disclosure norms promotes transparency in communicating AI vulnerabilities and updates.

🚫 Avoiding unsafe anti-patterns

Practices like unrestricted public release or a lack of rollback risk undermine AI security and trust.

Implications

  • Rapid advances: AI capabilities in cybersecurity are progressing faster than expected, surpassing many expert predictions.
  • Responsible stewardship: limiting access contrasts with the industry's usual fast deployment.
  • Proactive cyber-defence models: initiatives like Glasswing align AI tools with defensive goals.
  • Governance shapes impact: the outcome hinges on decisions about access, collaboration and responsibility.
✅
Do this: Remember the phrase "beyond alignment". Alignment alone is not enough — capability containment and institutional responsibility are essential.

Implications for Engineers and Education

AI safety changes what it means to be a professional engineer: technical skill is now only half the job.

For engineers and researchers

  • Beyond technical skills: engineers must combine technical expertise with judgement about what AI developments should be built — or restricted.
  • Ethical and safety dilemmas: discovering vulnerabilities or powerful methods raises responsibility questions that need careful consideration.
  • Responsible practice: consider downstream impacts, access control, and collaborate with institutions to ensure safe AI use.
  • Professionalism through restraint: recognising when to refuse or restrain deployment is itself a mark of professionalism.

For AI safety education

  • Comprehensive training: students must learn to build models and understand failure modes, risk analysis and governance.
  • Cybersecurity as a teaching domain: it offers a concrete area where lessons on misuse consequences are accessible and widely accepted.
  • Integration of perspectives: blend technical, institutional and ethical views to prepare students for restricted system environments.
  • Use of real case studies: real-world cases give practical frameworks for responsible decision-making beyond abstract theory.
💡
Why it matters: Knowing when not to deploy a capability is presented as part of being a professional, not a failure of ambition.

AI Privacy Foundations

AI is built from data — and that data is very often about people. Privacy is about keeping it from being misused or exposed.

AI privacy — protecting individuals' data used in AI systems from misuse or unintended disclosure.

The three core concerns

  • Data leakage during training or testing.
  • Identity privacy of the individuals whose data sits in a dataset.
  • Re-identification risk from generated outputs — recovering who someone is from data that was meant to be anonymous.

Challenges to privacy in AI

🗃️ Data collection and use

Large datasets may contain personal information, sourced from public or private sources without consent.

🧠 Model training risks

Sensitive data becomes embedded in model weights; personal data can be extracted from a trained model via a model inversion attack; models can also generate harmful content.

⚖️ Ethical and legal questions

Consent for using people's images and data, and accountability when a model is used maliciously.

D · M · O
"Data, Model, Output"
Privacy leaks at three moments — in the data (collected without consent), in the model (weights and inversion attacks), and in the output (re-identification from generated content).
⚠️
Common trap: "It was public data" is not a defence. Consent is a separate ethical requirement from whether data happened to be publicly available.

Privacy Laws: GDPR, CPRA and LGPD

Because generative AI must process enormous amounts of personal data, three privacy regimes decide what is legal.

LawRegionYearReach and character
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)European Union2018Enacted 25 May 2018. One of the strictest and most influential privacy laws worldwide; applies to organisations anywhere if they target or collect data related to EU residents.
California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA)California, USA2018Governs data privacy rights for Californians.
Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (LGPD)Brazil2018Passed by Brazil's National Congress.

How the three compare in focus

GDPR — comprehensive rights

Extensive rights including data access, correction, erasure, restriction, objection, portability, and protections around automated decision-making.

CCPA / CPRA — consumer focus

Emphasises consumer transparency, choice, data deletion, opt-out of data sale, and non-discrimination protections.

LGPD — aligns with GDPR, focusing on data access, correction, anonymisation, portability, consent revocation, and clear transparency.
✅
Do this: GDPR is the one with explicit extraterritorial reach — it follows EU residents' data anywhere in the world. That is the detail most likely to be tested.

GDPR: Principles and Rights

GDPR governs how an organisation must handle data, and separately, what an individual can demand.

Core principles (duties on the organisation)

  • Lawfulness, fairness and transparency: processing must be legal, fair, and clear to the individual.
  • Purpose limitation: data collected only for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes.
  • Data minimisation: only necessary data should be collected.
  • Accuracy: personal data must be kept accurate and up to date.
  • Storage limitation: data retained only as long as necessary.
  • Integrity and confidentiality: data processed securely to prevent unauthorised access or loss.
L · P · D · A · S · I
"Lawful Purposes Don't Always Store Information"
Lawfulness/fairness/transparency, Purpose limitation, Data minimisation, Accuracy, Storage limitation, Integrity and confidentiality.

Key rights for data subjects (powers of the person)

  • Right to be informed: know how your data is used.
  • Right of access: request a copy of your data.
  • Right to rectification: correct inaccurate data.
  • Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten"): request deletion under specific conditions.
  • Right to data portability: transfer your data to another provider.
  • Right to object: stop your data being used for specific purposes, such as marketing.
🧠
Remember: Principles are about the data (how it is handled). Rights are about the person (what they can demand). A data subject is simply the individual the data is about.

Deepfakes: How GANs Work

A deepfake is not a glitch. It is what you get when two neural networks compete until the fake is indistinguishable from the real.

Deepfake — an AI-generated, realistic image or video of a person, created using generative models, most often GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks).

Why deepfakes are a privacy concern

  • Unauthorised use of individuals' likenesses.
  • Potential defamation, blackmail and misinformation.
  • Difficulty removing deepfake data from the internet once it spreads.

The two competing models

Generator

Produces synthetic data — images, audio, video or text. Its goal is to create outputs that look real.

Discriminator

Evaluates inputs and decides whether they are real (from the training data) or fake (from the generator). Its goal is to correctly identify fakes.

The adversarial training loop

  1. The generator makes errors. It produces outputs the discriminator can spot.
  2. The discriminator detects those errors. It judges real versus fake.
  3. Feedback reduces the generator's errors. The generator adjusts to fool the discriminator next time.
  4. The cycle repeats thousands or millions of times. Over time the discriminator becomes less able to tell the difference, and the generator's outputs become increasingly realistic.

When the discriminator can no longer reliably distinguish real from fake, the generator is considered successful.

Deepfakes as a by-product of error reduction

Deepfakes emerge because GANs learn fine-grained patterns in faces, voices, gestures and speech; reduce the visual or auditory artifacts that reveal manipulation; and optimise specifically for human perceptual realism. As error approaches zero: fake faces look real, synthetic voices sound authentic, and fabricated videos appear convincing.

✅
Do this: A deepfake is not a failure of the GAN — it is the GAN succeeding at its objective of perceptual realism. Say it that way in an exam.
🤔
Pause & predict: If a GAN is judged only on whether it fools a discriminator, what does it never learn about? (Hint: truth, intent, context — you will meet this again in the next section.)

Misinformation, Disinformation and Trust

A technically "correct" GAN can cause serious harm — first by accident, then on purpose, and finally by destroying trust in everything.

Misinformation (unintentional harm)

GAN-generated content can be used mistakenly as real, shared without malicious intent, and believed because it looks and sounds right. Examples include fake images used in news reporting, altered videos shared on social media, and synthetic audio misattributed to real people.

Disinformation (deliberate harm)

Because adversarial networks can produce high-credibility fakes, they are powerful tools for intentional deception: political manipulation, fake evidence or fabricated statements, impersonation and scams, and erosion of trust in genuine media. Here, low error equals high persuasive power.

AspectMisinformationDisinformation
IntentUnintentionalDeliberate
MechanismMistaken belief, accidental sharingTargeted deception timed to influence
ExamplesFake news images, misattributed audioPolitical manipulation, scams, fake evidence

The deeper risk: loss of trust

As adversarial realism improves, people can no longer easily trust what they see or hear — and genuine evidence can be dismissed as "just another deep fake".

Scenario: from adversarial correctness to loss of trust

A media company uses a GAN-based video system to generate realistic footage for fast news explainers and reconstructions. It was trained on thousands of hours of real news footage. Its goal is to reduce visual and audio errors so the generated video is indistinguishable from real recordings. "Correctness" is defined internally as: the discriminator can no longer tell real footage from generated footage.

During a breaking news event, the system generates a video of a public official making a short statement that reconstructs what was "likely said", based on past speeches, voice patterns and facial movements. The clip looks real, sounds real, and matches the official's known communication style.

Later, malicious actors realise they can generate similar videos with targeted messages — the system's strength, minimal error, makes deception easy. They deliberately release fabricated statements, time the releases to influence public opinion, and rely on realism to bypass scepticism. That is disinformation: false content, shared intentionally, designed to mislead.

Trust begins to collapse

Once the deepfake is exposed, the company issues a correction — but the damage is already done. Public reaction shifts: "You can't trust video anymore". Real footage is questioned and genuine evidence is dismissed as "probably fake". The result is erosion of trust in media, confusion about what is real, and increased cynicism toward legitimate information.

Why "correctness" optimises the wrong thing

Adversarial AI's internal definition of success
Correctness = low detectable error
Success = indistinguishability from reality
Truth, intent, context = not in the objective

There is no concept of truth, intent or context inside this objective. The system performs exactly as designed — which is precisely the problem.

M · D · T
"Mistake, Deliberate, Trust lost"
Misinformation = mistake. Disinformation = deliberate. Both end in loss of Trust.
⚠️
Common trap: The problem is not that the GAN malfunctioned. It achieved exactly what it was optimised for. The system performs as designed — but that objective omits truth, intent and context.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

AI for Security
Using AI as the tool that enhances security; systems, not the AI, are what is being protected.
Security of AI
Treating the AI model itself as the asset under threat and protecting it from attack or manipulation.
Cybersecurity
Technologies, processes and policies that safeguard devices and information from threats such as malware, phishing and ransomware.
Zero-day
A previously unknown software flaw that defenders have had no time to patch; finding one before attackers is highly valuable.
Dual-use
The property that the same AI capability can aid defenders or attackers — for example, vulnerability discovery.
Containment
Restricting a powerful AI's access and use, rather than relying on alignment alone.
Claude Mythos
Anthropic's powerful, unreleased, gated model that discovers and can chain software vulnerabilities; the engine behind Project Glasswing.
AI privacy
Protecting individuals' data used in AI systems from misuse or unintended disclosure.
Data subject
The individual that personal data is about; GDPR gives data subjects specific rights.
Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
A pair of neural networks — a generator and a discriminator — trained together in competition.
Generator
The GAN network that produces synthetic data (images, audio, video, text) aiming to look real.
Discriminator
The GAN network that judges whether an input is real (from training data) or fake (from the generator).
Deepfake
An AI-generated realistic image or video of a person, typically produced with a GAN.
Misinformation
False content spread unintentionally — believed and shared in good faith because it looks and sounds right.
Disinformation
False content shared intentionally to mislead, such as political manipulation or impersonation scams.
OpenBSD
A free, secure, portable Unix-like operating system based on 4.4BSD, focused on proactive security, code correctness and integrated cryptography; Mythos found a 27-year-old flaw in it.
FFmpeg
A free, open-source suite and industry-standard command-line tool for recording, converting, streaming and editing multimedia; Mythos found a 16-year-old flaw in it.
Anomaly detection
Finding unusual patterns in network traffic, user behaviour or system logs that may signal an attack.
Static analysis
Examining source code without running it; useful but prone to missing subtle, complex vulnerabilities.
Fuzz testing
Feeding a program large amounts of malformed or random input to provoke crashes; improved detection but missed complex flaws.
Adversarial training loop
The generator/discriminator feedback cycle, repeated thousands or millions of times, until fakes become indistinguishable.
Defender-first approach
Directing a powerful AI capability at defence — finding and fixing flaws — rather than allowing open or offensive use, as in Project Glasswing.
Responsible disclosure
Privately reporting a vulnerability to those who can fix it so it can be patched before being made public.
Access control
Restricting who may use a powerful AI system, combined with auditing and usage restrictions, to prevent unethical deployment.
Model inversion attack
Extracting personal data from a trained model by probing it, exploiting sensitive information embedded in the weights.
Re-identification
Recovering an individual's identity from supposedly anonymous data or from generated outputs.
Perceptual realism
How convincingly a generated output mimics reality to human senses; the target GANs optimise for.
Exploit chain
Combining several separate vulnerabilities so that, together, they achieve full system control.

Q1

A team uses an AI to scan source code for bugs. Later, an attacker probes that same model to extract its training data. Which scenario is AI for security and which is security of AI?

Answer: Scanning code for bugs is AI for security — the AI is the tool doing the defending. Probing the model to steal data is security of AI — now the AI itself is the asset under attack. The model is the same; only its role changes.
Q2

Name the three kinds of data AI analyses to find anomalies, and list four applications of AI in cybersecurity.

Answer: Data: network traffic, user behaviour and system logs. Applications: intrusion and malware detection, fraud detection, threat intelligence, and automated incident response.
Q3

Give at least two reasons the lecture says cybersecurity is central to AI safety.

Answer: A single flaw can trigger large-scale exploitation affecting millions across interconnected systems; AI reduces the asymmetry between defenders and attackers; vulnerability discovery is dual-use (valuable for defence, dangerous if weaponised); and managing AI capabilities requires governance over access and disclosure to address systemic risk.
Q4

What did pre-AI security rely on and keep missing, and what does modern AI add?

Answer: Traditional security relied on skilled engineers manually auditing source code, while static analysis and fuzz testing improved detection but missed subtle, complex vulnerabilities — leaving flaws undiscovered for years. AI adds system-level reasoning over large codebases, deeper vulnerability discovery, the ability to generate exploits (double-edged), and a compressed discovery-to-exploitation tempo.
Q5

What did Big Sleep discover, and what four features make it a positive AI safety example?

Answer: Big Sleep (Google Project Zero and DeepMind) autonomously found a subtle memory-safety flaw in SQLite that traditional testing had missed. It is positive because it was proactive (found before attackers), used restricted trusted access, kept humans accountable for validation, disclosure and remediation, and delivered systemic benefit by patching a widely used component.
Q6

What is Project Glasswing, and how does it differ from simply releasing a powerful vulnerability-finding model?

Answer: Project Glasswing is Anthropic's defender-first initiative that limits access to Claude Mythos to trusted organisations so it can defensively find and fix vulnerabilities. Findings are shared responsibly with maintainers for timely patches, and it fosters cross-sector collective defence. It reflects a shift from alignment alone to containment and controlled deployment — unlike open release, which would let anyone wield the capability.
Q7

List the three headline vulnerabilities attributed to Claude Mythos.

Answer: A 27-year-old flaw in OpenBSD (despite its strong security reputation); a 16-year-old flaw in FFmpeg; and a chain of Linux kernel vulnerabilities that together gave full system control.
Q8

Does Claude Mythos pose an autonomous threat? Where does the lecture locate the real danger?

Answer: No — the risk of autonomous harm is low; Mythos is unlikely to act independently or develop malicious goals. The real danger is human-directed misuse: how effectively it performs harmful tasks when a human directs it. Hence access control, auditing and usage restrictions, and Glasswing's controlled environment.
Q9

Explain the governance shift "from tools to capabilities", and why post-hoc mitigation is not enough.

Answer: Governance must target the underlying capabilities of an AI, not just the tools or outputs it produces. Traditional software fixes harms after release, but AI vulnerabilities can be exploited immediately, so fixing later is too slow — hence proactive measures: access control, capability evaluation and pre-release decision-making. Cybersecurity serves as the model because consequences are immediate and severe.
Q10

Name the four design principles for safe defensive AI, and give one unsafe anti-pattern the lecture warns against.

Answer: (1) Restricted access and control; (2) human oversight and accountability; (3) responsible disclosure norms; (4) avoiding unsafe anti-patterns. Unsafe anti-patterns include unrestricted public release and a lack of rollback capability — both undermine AI security and trust.
Q11

What are the three main AI privacy concerns, and what is a model inversion attack?

Answer: Data leakage during training/testing, identity privacy of individuals in datasets, and re-identification risk from generated outputs. A model inversion attack extracts personal data from a trained model by probing it — sensitive information becomes embedded in the model weights during training and can be recovered.
Q12

How do GDPR, CPRA and LGPD compare in region, timing and focus?

Answer: GDPR — EU, enacted 25 May 2018, one of the strictest and most influential laws worldwide, with extraterritorial reach (applies to organisations anywhere that target or collect data about EU residents); provides comprehensive rights including access, correction, erasure, restriction, objection, portability and automated-decision protections. CPRA — California, 2018, consumer-focused: transparency, choice, deletion, opt-out of data sale, non-discrimination. LGPD — Brazil, 2018, aligns with GDPR: access, correction, anonymisation, portability, consent revocation, transparency.
Q13

GDPR sets out principles and data-subject rights. Give three of each and explain the difference.

Answer: Principles (duties on the organisation about the data): lawfulness/fairness/transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality. Rights (powers of the individual): to be informed, of access, to rectification, to erasure ("right to be forgotten"), to data portability, and to object. In short: principles are about how the data is handled; rights are about what the person can demand.
Q14

Describe the generator–discriminator loop. When is the generator considered "successful", and why is a deepfake a by-product of that success?

Answer: The generator makes outputs; the discriminator judges real versus fake; feedback reduces the generator's errors; the cycle repeats thousands or millions of times. The discriminator becomes less able to tell the difference, while the generator's output becomes increasingly realistic. The generator is successful when the discriminator can no longer reliably distinguish real from fake. A deepfake is therefore a by-product of error reduction: GANs learn fine-grained patterns in faces, voices, gestures and speech, remove tell-tale artifacts, and optimise for human perceptual realism — so as error approaches zero, fakes look and sound real.
Q15

Distinguish misinformation from disinformation, and explain the claim that adversarial "correctness" optimises the wrong thing.

Answer: Misinformation is false content spread unintentionally — used mistakenly as real, shared without malice, believed because it looks and sounds right. Disinformation is false content shared deliberately to mislead, e.g. political manipulation, fake evidence, impersonation and scams; here low error equals high persuasive power. On correctness: the system defines correctness as low detectable error and success as indistinguishability from reality, so there is no concept of truth, intent or context in its objective. It performs exactly as designed — but that design omits what actually matters.
