One Key to Lock and Unlock: Symmetric Cryptography

This week you learn how secret-key cryptography actually works, from Caesar's shift cipher through to the AES that protects most of the Internet. Along the way you meet the maths the lecture assumes — XOR, modular arithmetic, finite fields — plus the ideas of confusion and diffusion, the one-time pad, Feistel and substitution-permutation networks, cipher block modes, and random number generation.

Read this first: Hold one mental model for the whole week — a symmetric cipher is a keyed scramble, and every design choice is a fight against patterns. Natural language and real data are full of repeating structure; encryption is good only when that structure is hidden. Historical ciphers failed because they left patterns visible. Modern ciphers (DES, AES) exist to smash those patterns with substitution, transposition, many rounds, and the confusion and diffusion that Shannon named. Everything else this week — S-boxes, key schedules, block modes, random seeds — is machinery in service of that one fight.

What Is Cryptography?

Cryptography is the science of keeping data safe across distance and across time — and it is a double-edged sword that punishes careless use.

Cryptography is the science of securely transmitting data, spatially and temporally. Both words matter:

📡 Spatially — across distance

Keeping data safe as it travels a physical distance. For example, securing the traffic between one of your browser tabs and a remote web server such as www.google.com.

🗄️ Temporally — across time

Keeping data secure far into the future. Encrypting archival data today so it stays unreadable decades from now, even as computing power and cryptanalysis improve.

What cryptography is used for

Cryptography is an indispensable tool in secure protocols and systems. It is used to ensure four things:

GoalWhat it meansAchieved by
ConfidentialityOnly the intended reader can understand the data.Encryption (symmetric and asymmetric)
IntegrityThe data has not been altered.Signatures, Message Authentication Codes (MACs), hash functions
Authentication & data origin verificationYou know who sent the data.Signatures, MACs, and protocols
Non-repudiationThe sender cannot later deny sending it.Signatures

This unit focuses on the first three — confidentiality, integrity and authentication. Other applications such as privacy and anonymity exist but are out of scope here. The unit teaches cryptography from a functional point of view, with less emphasis on formal mathematical proofs — though you still need a solid base of maths, which is why the number-theory and abstract-algebra handouts are assessable.

A double-edged sword

Cryptography is complex and full of subtleties. Applying it carelessly can produce an insecure system — and worse, one that looks secure to its developers and users. Remember that the attacker does not know the system is "supposed" to be secure, and will happily take it apart.

⚠️
Historical warning — GSM A5/1: the second-generation GSM mobile systems of the 1990s used a secret, proprietary stream cipher called A5 (with variants A5/1 and A5/2) to encrypt traffic between the phone and the base station. It used a 64-bit secret key. Serious weaknesses were found by 1994, and by the late 1990s cryptanalytic plus hardware attacks made it practically breakable. Because of the GSM protocol's structure, a ciphertext-only attack was possible: an eavesdropper who simply gathered a run of encrypted frames could recover the secret key with only moderate computing power. A system its designers believed secure was not.
✅
Rule number one of implementation: do not implement (or "cook") your own cryptography for real-world use. Always use known, standardised methods. Implementing crypto needs deep experience with the quirks of hardware and programming languages. The recognised path to proficiency is to practise, find a mentor, and discuss with other implementers — a long career path with no shortcuts.
🤔
Pause & predict: A5/1 was secret, yet it was broken. If the algorithm had been published instead, would it have been more or less secure in the long run? Hold that thought — it is exactly what Kerkhoff's Principle answers in a couple of sections.

Symmetric vs Asymmetric Cryptography

The whole field splits in two, based on one question: is the decryption key the same as the encryption key?

Cryptography comes in two forms, and the difference is entirely about the keys.

Symmetric cryptography

  • The encryption key and decryption key are the same: ke = kd.
  • Or, at least, the decryption key can be derived from the encryption key (kd ← ke).
  • Also called shared-key cryptography.
  • Built from algorithms that shuffle symbols around and map them to others.
  • This week's topic.

Asymmetric cryptography

  • The keys are different: ke ≠ kd.
  • It is infeasible to compute the decryption key kd from the encryption key ke.
  • Also called public-key cryptography.
  • Built on the hardness of some mathematical problem.
  • Covered in a later week (e.g. RSA).

The practical difference is the key-distribution problem. With symmetric crypto, both parties must already share one secret key — which is awkward if they have never met. Asymmetric crypto removes that problem by letting one key be public, at the cost of being much slower. In practice, the two are combined: asymmetric crypto is often used to agree on a symmetric key, and then symmetric crypto does the bulk encryption.

The symmetric cipher model

Picture the basic operation of symmetric-key cryptography as a pipeline with one shared key feeding both ends:

Plaintext p→ Encrypt with key k→ Ciphertext c→ Decrypt with the same key k→ Plaintext p

Notice that the same key appears on both sides. An eavesdropper who sees only the ciphertext must recover the key to read the message.

💡
Why the distinction matters: symmetric algorithms get their security from shuffling and mapping symbols so thoroughly that patterns vanish. Asymmetric algorithms get theirs from a maths problem that is believed hard. That is why this week's maths — finite fields and modular arithmetic — underpins symmetric ciphers such as AES, while a different hardness problem (factorisation, discrete logs) underpins asymmetric ones.

Kerkhoff's Principle

Security must live in the key alone — never in the secrecy of the design.

Kerkhoff's Principle
The security of a cryptographic mechanism must depend only on the security of the keys, and never on the mechanism (or anything else except the keys) being secret.

(You may also see the name spelled Kerckhoffs's Principle. The lecture uses "Kerkhoff's".) The idea is simple but powerful: assume the enemy knows your algorithm completely. The only secret left is the key.

This is why almost every mainstream algorithm in use — AES, RSA, DES — is publicly disclosed. We know exactly how they work. Their security rests purely on the secrecy of the keys.

The "many eyes" argument

Open design is not a weakness; it is a strength. When the design is public, many researchers and engineers can inspect it and find flaws. That is why NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology) runs open competitions, inviting the global cryptographic community to submit algorithms for public review and scrutiny. The winners — like AES — have been attacked by experts worldwide and survived. The transparency of the process is exactly what makes the resulting standards robust and free of hidden vulnerabilities.

🧠
Memory hook: "The lock is public; only the key is secret." Every real-world lock works this way — everyone can see how a deadbolt is made, but only you hold the key.

When designers ignore the principle

History has repeatedly punished secrecy-based design:

  • GSM A5/1 and A5/2 — initially secret, later found weak (as we saw earlier).
  • DVD Content Scrambling System (CSS) — the next example.
⚠️
DVD Content Scrambling System (CSS): CSS was created to stop DVDs sold in one region being played in another, to stop copying of DVD content, and — because the algorithm was not disclosed — to stop unapproved software or hardware players (players not licensed by the Copy Control Association) from playing discs. It relied on secrecy of the algorithm for its security. The DVD Consortium assumed that keeping the algorithm secret would keep the system safe. Once the algorithm was reverse-engineered and published, the entire copyright system was compromised. A textbook violation of Kerkhoff's Principle.
🤔
Pause & predict: If a company tells you "our encryption is secure because our algorithm is proprietary and nobody knows it", which principle are they violating, and what is the likely outcome?

Basic Definitions and Notation

Five words — plaintext, encryption, ciphertext, decryption, key — plus the functions Enc and Dec. Learn the symbols now and the rest of the week reads smoothly.

Plaintext — the non-encrypted, unprotected data, written p. It is built from symbols of an alphabet R = {r1, r2, …, rn}. For example, ordinary English comes from the alphabet of letters, digits and punctuation: {[A-Z], [a-z], [0-9]}.
Encryption — the process that encodes plaintext using the cipher function Enc and a key ke, producing ciphertext c. In symbols: Encke(p) = c.
Ciphertext — the encrypted output, written c. It need not come from the same alphabet as the plaintext, and its alphabet may be a different size. We denote the ciphertext alphabet S = {s1, s2, …, sm}.
Decryption — the process that decodes ciphertext using the decipher function Dec and a key kd, producing the original plaintext p. In symbols: Deckd(c) = p.
Key — the secret value that parameterises the cipher. In symmetric cryptography ke = kd (or kd can be derived from ke); in asymmetric cryptography ke ≠ kd.
ℹ️
Why ciphertext alphabets differ: a Caesar cipher maps letters to letters, so plaintext and ciphertext share one alphabet. A modern block cipher turns a 128-bit plaintext block into a 128-bit ciphertext block, so the "alphabet" is the set of all 2128 possible blocks. The size of the plaintext alphabet n and the ciphertext alphabet m need not match.
🤔
Pause & predict: In Encke(p) = c and Deckd(c) = p, which symbols are inputs, which is output, and what must be true of ke and kd for the scheme to be symmetric?

Maths You Need: XOR, Modular Arithmetic, Groups and Fields

The lecture assumes a maths toolkit. Here is all of it, in plain English, so you are never blocked by notation.

The slides list the maths you are expected to know: the XOR function, modular arithmetic, the Extended Euclidean Algorithm, primitive roots, the discrete logarithm problem, and groups, rings and fields (including finite fields). These are assessable and you will need them all unit long. Let us build them up from zero.

1. The XOR function

XOR (exclusive OR, written ⊕) is a bitwise operation. It takes two bits and returns 1 if they are different, and 0 if they are the same:

aba ⊕ b
000
011
101
110

Applied to whole bit strings, XOR works bit by bit. Four properties make it the favourite tool of cryptography:

  • Self-cancelling: a ⊕ a = 0 (a value XORed with itself vanishes).
  • Identity: a ⊕ 0 = a.
  • Commutative and associative: order and grouping do not matter.
  • Self-inverse: (a ⊕ b) ⊕ b = a, which is why encryption and decryption with XOR are the same operation.
Worked example
10111011 ⊕ 00111101 = 10000110

Check the first bit: 1 ⊕ 0 = 1. Second: 0 ⊕ 0 = 0. Third: 1 ⊕ 1 = 0. Third from last: 1 ⊕ 1 = 0, and so on. Notice that XORing the ciphertext with the plaintext recovers the key: 10000110 ⊕ 10111011 = 00111101.

2. Modular arithmetic and congruence

Modular arithmetic is "clock arithmetic". a mod n is the remainder when a is divided by n, always a value from 0 to n − 1. For example, 26 mod 5 = 1 (5 goes into 26 five times with 1 left over), and 74 mod 11 = 8.

Two numbers are congruent modulo n, written a ≡ b (mod n), when they have the same remainder — equivalently, when n divides their difference (a − b). So 26 ≡ 11 (mod 5), because both leave remainder 1. Congruence is an equivalence relation:

  • Reflexive: a ≡ a.
  • Symmetric: if a ≡ b then b ≡ a.
  • Transitive: if a ≡ b and b ≡ c, then a ≡ c. (Careful: this does not mean a = c — 26, 11 and 16 are all congruent mod 5 yet all different.)

Congruence is preserved by addition and multiplication: if a ≡ b and c ≡ d, then a + c ≡ b + d and a × c ≡ b × d. This is what lets us do arithmetic inside a modulus.

Residue classes
Every integer belongs to exactly one class mod n. Mod 5 the classes are {…, −5, 0, 5, …}, {…, 1, 6, 11, …}, and so on.

3. The Extended Euclidean Algorithm

The greatest common divisor (gcd) of two integers is the largest number dividing both. Euclid's algorithm finds it by repeated remainder-taking. The Extended Euclidean Algorithm goes further: it finds integers x and y such that

Bézout's identity
a·x + b·y = gcd(a, b)

Its main use in cryptography is finding a modular inverse. The inverse of a modulo n is the number a−1 with a · a−1 ≡ 1 (mod n). Such an inverse exists if and only if gcd(a, n) = 1 (that is, a and n are coprime). To find it, solve a·x + n·y = 1; the value x is the inverse.

ℹ️
Worked example: find the inverse of 3 mod 11. Run Euclid: 11 = 3·3 + 2, then 3 = 1·2 + 1. Back-substitute: 1 = 3 − 1·2 = 3 − 1·(11 − 3·3) = 4·3 − 1·11. So 4·3 + (−1)·11 = 1, giving x = 4. Check: 3 × 4 = 12 ≡ 1 (mod 11). The inverse of 3 mod 11 is 4. Note that 2 has no inverse mod 6, because gcd(2, 6) = 2 ≠ 1.

4. Groups, rings and fields

These are sets with operations that obey tidy rules. Read the table as a ladder: a ring is a group plus a second operation; a field is a ring where you can also divide.

StructureOperationsRequirements (informally)Example
Groupone (say +)Closed, associative, has an identity, and every element has an inverse. If order does not matter it is Abelian.(Zn, +)
Ringtwo (+, ×)A group under +, plus × is associative and distributes over +.Zn under + and ×
Fieldtwo (+, ×)A commutative ring in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse (so division works).Zp when p is prime

Here Zn means the integers {0, 1, 2, …, n − 1} under arithmetic mod n. The single most examinable fact is this: Zp is a field if and only if p is prime. For example:

  • Mod 7 → {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} is a field, because 7 is prime.
  • Mod 6 → {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5} is not a field. Take 2: its multiples mod 6 are 0, 2, 4, 0, 2, 4 — the value 1 never appears, so 2 has no inverse.
🧠
Memory hook: Prime ⇒ Field. If the modulus is composite, some element is a zero divisor and cannot be divided by. The zero divisors are exactly the elements sharing a factor with the modulus.

5. Finite fields and GF(28)

A finite field (also called a Galois field, written GF) is simply a field with finitely many elements. A finite field must have pk elements for some prime p and integer k. There are two flavours:

  • Prime fields GF(p) — the integers mod a prime, e.g. GF(7).
  • Extension fields GF(pk) — built from polynomials, e.g. GF(28), the field with 256 elements that AES uses.

In GF(28) each element is a byte (8 bits), treated as a polynomial with coefficients in {0, 1}. For example the byte 11010111 is the polynomial x7 + x6 + x4 + x2 + x + 1. The operations are:

  • Addition = bitwise XOR (because coefficients are mod 2). So A + B = (ai XOR bi) for each bit position.
  • Multiplication = ordinary polynomial multiplication, then reduction modulo an irreducible polynomial. AES uses m(x) = x8 + x4 + x3 + x + 1. "Irreducible" means it cannot be factored further, which is what guarantees every non-zero element has an inverse.
💡
Why GF(28)? A byte has 8 bits, so GF(28) is the natural field in which to do byte-wise arithmetic on a computer. Addition is a single cheap XOR instruction, and multiplication is a small table or a short shift-and-XOR routine. AES exploits exactly this.

6. Primitive roots and the discrete logarithm problem

A primitive root (or generator) modulo a prime p is an element g whose powers generate every non-zero residue:

Generator property
g1, g2, g3, …, gp−1 (mod p) hit every value 1, 2, …, p−1 exactly once.

The order of an element is the smallest positive k with gk ≡ 1. A primitive root has order p − 1. For example, mod 7 the powers of 3 are 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 — all six non-zero values — so 3 is a primitive root mod 7.

The discrete logarithm problem is the reverse: given g, p and h = gx mod p, find the exponent x. It is believed to be computationally hard for large primes, and it is the foundation of asymmetric schemes such as Diffie–Hellman key exchange and ElGamal — you will meet it again later in the unit.

🤔
Pause & predict: Mod 6, why does the element 2 fail to have a multiplicative inverse, and what does that tell you about whether {0,1,2,3,4,5} is a field? (Answer: its multiples only ever hit 0, 2 and 4, never 1; the set is not a field because 6 is composite.)

Historical Ciphers: Substitution and Transposition

Every classical cipher is one of two moves — change the symbols, or change their positions — and both leave patterns behind.

Historical symmetric ciphers are built from two fundamental techniques:

Transposition

  • Shifts the positions of symbols according to a rule.
  • Changes the order of symbols, not the symbols themselves.
  • Example: the rail fence cipher (later this section).

Substitution

  • Replaces symbols with other symbols according to a rule.
  • The symbols change; their positions stay put.
  • Example: the Caesar cipher.

Both techniques are usually parameterised by a key (for example, the shift amount), so that only someone with the key can reverse the rule. It is also common for the same cipher to combine transposition and substitution to increase its strength.

The Caesar cipher

The Caesar cipher is the simplest form of substitution cipher, and specifically a monoalphabetic one: a symbol from the plaintext alphabet is always mapped to the same symbol in the ciphertext alphabet. Every letter is shifted by n positions. With n = 3: a → d, b → e, c → f, and so on.

Plaintext symbol→Ciphertext symbol
r1→s5
r2→s2
r3→s9
…→…
rn→s17

Roman historian Suetonius claimed the cipher was used by Gaius Iulius Caesar, though others used similar methods earlier. By today's standards its security is very low — there are only 25 useful shifts to try — but thousands of years ago, when few people were literate, it may have been effective enough.

Why substitution fails: frequency analysis

Every natural language has a characteristic frequency at which each letter occurs. In English, 'E' is the most common letter. A monoalphabetic substitution cipher preserves these frequencies in the ciphertext: the most frequent ciphertext symbol is very likely the encryption of 'E'. An attacker can then guess the mapping, perhaps aided by a dictionary, and recover the message. This technique is called frequency analysis, and it is the classic weakness of monoalphabetic ciphers. On top of that, brute-forcing a substitution cipher would take only seconds on a modern computer.

💡
The lesson: a cipher is only as strong as its ability to hide statistical patterns. This single observation drives the entire rest of the week — polyalphabetic ciphers, confusion and diffusion, and eventually AES.

The Vigenère Cipher and How to Break It

Use a different Caesar shift for each letter, cycling through a keyword — polyalphabetic encryption that flattens letter frequencies, yet still falls to clever statistics.

The Vigenère cipher is a polyalphabetic cipher: each plaintext character is encrypted with a different Caesar cipher, and which one to use is determined by the corresponding letter of a keyword. This directly attacks the frequency-analysis weakness, because the same plaintext letter can map to different ciphertext letters at different positions.

Encryption and decryption, step by step

Use a 26-letter alphabet A with values A = 0, B = 1, …, Z = 25, so N = 26. Take plaintext P = "HELLO" → {7, 4, 11, 11, 14} and keyword K = "KEY" → {10, 4, 24}, so |K| = 3.

Encryption
Ci = (Pi + Ki mod |K|) mod 26
Decryption
Pi = (Ci − Ki mod |K| + 26) mod 26

The subscript i mod |K| is the trick: the keyword repeats, so its letters are used cyclically. Working the example:

PositionPlaintextKey (cycling)CalculationCiphertext
1H = 7K = 10(7 + 10) mod 26 = 17R
2E = 4E = 4(4 + 4) mod 26 = 8I
3L = 11Y = 24(11 + 24) mod 26 = 35 mod 26 = 9J
4L = 11K = 10(11 + 10) mod 26 = 21V
5O = 14E = 4(14 + 4) mod 26 = 18S

So "HELLO" with key "KEY" becomes "RIJVS". Decryption reverses the arithmetic: R = 17 with key K = 10 gives (17 − 10 + 26) mod 26 = 7 = H, and so on.

ℹ️
A longer worked example: with key "CRYPTO" and plaintext "THISISAGREATDAY", the first letters give C(2) + T(19) = 21 = V, C(2) + A(0) = 2 = C, and C(2) + D(3) = 5 = F, so the ciphertext begins "VYGHBGCXPTTHFRW". The keyword CRYPTO repeats to cover all 15 letters.

Breaking Vigenère when you know the key length

The key insight is that there will be positions where the same key letter encrypts different plaintext letters. Collect all ciphertext characters that were encrypted by the first key letter, and run frequency analysis on them — they now behave like a single Caesar cipher. Suppose the most common such ciphertext character is "G" (value 6), and you assume it encrypts "E" (value 4). Then 6 − c ≡ 4 (mod 26), so c = 2, and the first key letter is "C". Repeat for each key position to recover the whole key. This step still involves some guesswork and trial and error, as all cryptanalysis does.

Breaking Vigenère when you do not know the key length

You could simply try different key lengths and repeat the process until sensible text appears. But natural language lets you do better with the Kasiski examination, which finds the key length.

The idea: if the plaintext contains the same sequence of symbols at intervals that are multiples of the key length, those sequences are encrypted by the same part of the key and so produce the same repeated pattern in the ciphertext. This is not a coincidence — natural languages repeat words like "the" constantly, and Vigenère keys are short. So: find repeated patterns, measure the distances between them, and the greatest common divisor of those distances is likely the key length.

ℹ️
Worked Kasiski example (key = "KEY"): In the example text, the distance between the 1st and 2nd repetitions is 33, so the key size must be one of its factors: 1, 3, 11 or 33. The distance between the 2nd and 3rd repetitions is 27, so the key size must be one of 1, 3, 9 or 27. The common factors are 1 and 3; ignore 1 because it is a very weak key that can be brute-forced. The possible key size is therefore 3.
🧠
Memory hook for Kasiski: Repeats reveal rhythm. Repeated ciphertext chunks are echoes of repeated plaintext, and the gcd of the gaps between echoes is the key length. Also note the other statistical test mentioned in the slides: the Friedman test.
🤔
Pause & predict: If a ciphertext shows repeated chunks at distances of 12 and 18, what key lengths should you consider, and which common factors survive? (Answer: factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12; factors of 18 are 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18; common factors are 1, 2, 3 and 6 — with 6 a plausible key length.)

From Classic to Modern: Rail Fence, Confusion and Diffusion

Transposition in action (rail fence), then Shannon's two design goals that every modern cipher must satisfy.

The rail fence cipher

The rail fence cipher is a transposition cipher. The plaintext is written down as a sequence of diagonals (like a zig-zag), then read off as a sequence of rows.

Example: "MEET ME AFTER THE TOGA PARTY" with a rail fence of depth 2 (remove the spaces). Write the letters alternately onto two rows:

Two rails
Row 1 (odd positions): M E M A T R H T G P R Y
Row 2 (even positions): E T E F E T E O A A T

Reading row 1 then row 2 gives the ciphertext: MEMATRHTGPRYETEFETEOAAT. To decrypt, you count how many letters go on each rail, split the ciphertext accordingly, and interleave them back into the zig-zag. With only two rails, the pattern is easy to guess — which is exactly why real ciphers combine transposition with substitution and many rounds.

Shannon's two design goals

Polyalphabetic ciphers fixed one shortcoming of monoalphabetic ciphers: that frequency analysis can yield the key. Modern ciphers push the same idea much further. Claude Shannon identified two design goals that a modern cipher must meet:

🌫️ Diffusion

Blur statistically significant characteristics in the plaintext. Every ciphertext symbol must depend on many plaintext symbols. On average, flipping one plaintext bit should change half the ciphertext bits. Achieved mainly through transposition — spreading the influence of each plaintext bit across the whole ciphertext.

🌀 Confusion

Blur the dependency between the ciphertext and the key. Each ciphertext symbol must depend on several symbols of the key, so an attacker cannot relate ciphertext to key even with lots of ciphertext. Achieved mainly through substitution — replacing data with other data in a complex way.

🧠
Memory hook: Diffusion = plaintext spreads; Confusion = key hides. Diffusion scrambles the message's fingerprints; confusion stops you reading the key off the ciphertext. Every modern cipher you meet this week is judged on both.
🤔

Pause & predict: Which goal does the Caesar cipher satisfy — diffusion or confusion — and why is it terrible at the other? (Answer: Caesar is pure substitution, so it offers weak confusion and almost no diffusion: changing one plaintext letter changes only one ciphertext letter, and the frequency pattern survives.)

The One-Time Pad and Perfect Secrecy

The only cipher with provably perfect secrecy — and the reason almost nobody can use it.

The One-Time Pad (OTP) is a symmetric scheme, and it is a special case of a stream cipher. Do not confuse it with the One-Time Password used in multi-factor authentication (MFA) — different thing entirely.

The OTP is a random sequence of bits o with |o| ≥ |p|, meaning the key is at least as long as the plaintext. The ciphertext is simply:

One-time pad
c = o ⊕ p

Decryption uses the same XOR: p = c ⊕ o. (XOR is convenient but not mandatory — you could equally use modular addition mod 26.) Because the key never repeats, encrypting the same plaintext twice produces completely unrelated ciphertexts. This leads to the OTP's defining property.

Perfect security (perfect secrecy) — given a ciphertext, every possible plaintext is equally likely to be the correct one. The ciphertext conveys no information about the plaintext content. Equivalently, the probability distribution of possible plaintexts is independent of the ciphertext.

It can be proved that any perfectly secret scheme must use at least as much key material as there is plaintext to encrypt. The OTP is perfectly secure if and only if:

  1. The key o is truly random.
  2. The key is at least as long as the plaintext: |o| ≥ |p|.
  3. The key is never used twice — not even in part.

Why the OTP is impractical

OTPs have been used in extremely high-value situations (such as the famous "red telephone" hotline), but only rarely, because the conditions on o are much too hard to achieve:

  • Key distribution is a problem. How do Alice and Bob securely exchange a key as long as the message? If they already had a secure channel to do that, they would not need the OTP in the first place.
  • Generating that much true randomness is hard. Applications that communicate frequently would need enormous volumes of genuinely random key material.

Breaking the OTP when the key is reused

If a one-time pad key is reused (a "many-time pad", or a looping one-time pad), it can be broken. Suppose two plaintexts P1 and P2 are encrypted with the same key K, giving C1 = P1 ⊕ K and C2 = P2 ⊕ K. An attacker who captures both ciphertexts can XOR them together:

Cancelling the key
C1 ⊕ C2 = (P1 ⊕ K) ⊕ (P2 ⊕ K) = P1 ⊕ P2   (because K ⊕ K is all zeros)

The key has vanished! This also means P1 = (P1 ⊕ P2) ⊕ P2 and P2 = (P1 ⊕ P2) ⊕ P1. The attacker now uses crib dragging: guess a word likely to appear in one plaintext (in English, "the" is a good bet) and slide it along all positions, XORing it with the combined string P1 ⊕ P2 to see whether readable text appears in the other. If it does, extend the guess and continue. (See the worked example linked in the notes by Travis Dazell.)

⚠️
The cardinal sin: never reuse any part of a one-time pad. Reuse collapses two "perfectly secret" messages into a solvable puzzle — essentially a known-plaintext attack once you guess a crib.
🤔
Pause & predict: Two messages are encrypted with the same OTP. If you can guess the word "ATTACK" appears at the start of one, what do you instantly learn about the other message at that position? (Answer: XOR "ATTACK" into the combined stream to reveal the other message's first six characters.)

Attacks on Cryptographic Systems

A system is compromised if the attacker learns plaintexts they should not, or the decryption key — and the attacker's power is defined by what they are allowed to see.

A cryptographic system is compromised if an adversary can do either of the following:

  • Obtain plaintexts for certain ciphertexts, or
  • Obtain the decryption key kd when given the encryption key ke.

Cryptanalysis is the science (and art) of decrypting without knowledge of the key. Attacks are classified by how much the attacker is allowed to see — the more access, the stronger the attacker, and the harder the cipher must work to stay safe.

Attack modelWhat the attacker may doReal-world example
Ciphertext-onlySee only ciphertexts.World War II: intercepting encrypted military messages without knowing their plaintext, and trying to decrypt purely from the ciphertext.
Known plaintextSee some plaintexts and their corresponding ciphertexts.Bletchley Park breaking Enigma because the Allies knew likely plaintext phrases (weather reports, common military terms) and could match them to ciphertext.
Chosen plaintextChoose a plaintext and see the resulting ciphertext. (This is the standard model for asymmetric cryptography.)The BEAST attack on SSL/TLS: attackers chose plaintext blocks and studied the corresponding ciphertext blocks to uncover vulnerabilities.
Chosen ciphertextChoose a ciphertext and see its decrypted plaintext — a so-called "oracle attack".Bleichenbacher's attack on PKCS#1 v1.5: exploiting a decryption oracle to infer details about the key.
💡
Why this matters for design: a cipher must be secure even against the strongest realistic attacker. A scheme that is only safe against ciphertext-only attacks but leaks under chosen plaintext is dangerous — and "chosen plaintext" is often easy to arrange in the real world, since an attacker can often influence the data being encrypted.
🤔
Pause & predict: If an attacker knows one plaintext/ciphertext pair for a simple XOR stream cipher, which attack model are they using, and what can they derive? (Answer: known plaintext; for a raw XOR they can XOR the two to recover the keystream, then decrypt everything encrypted with it.)

Stream Ciphers vs Block Ciphers

Symmetric ciphers come in two shapes: those that process a trickle of bits, and those that process fixed-size blocks.

Stream ciphers

  • Encrypt data one bit or byte at a time.
  • Combine each plaintext bit/byte with a corresponding bit/byte of a keystream derived from the secret key.
  • The process runs continuously, like a stream — hence the name.
  • Examples: RC4 (now considered insecure) and Salsa20.

Block ciphers

  • Encrypt data in fixed-size blocks (typically 64 or 128 bits).
  • Take a block of plaintext, process it with the key, output a block of ciphertext of the same size.
  • If the plaintext is smaller than the block, padding is added to fill it.
  • Examples: DES (now considered insecure) and AES.

How a stream cipher works

The idea is to generate a keystream from the secret key. Apply a cryptographic function f to the key k to obtain a stream of bits b = b0, b1, b2, …, bn. Then combine this keystream with the plaintext p = p0, p1, p2, …, pn using a combination function — most commonly XOR. The ciphertext is:

Stream cipher
c = b ⊕ p

The central challenge is that the keystream must be as unpredictable as possible without knowledge of the key k. Rephrased: it must be computationally infeasible to determine whether the keystream is random or deterministic — because if it were recognisably deterministic, that would imply a discoverable pattern. This is exactly the job of a pseudo-random function (see the PRNG section).

ℹ️
Where the OTP fits: the one-time pad is a stream cipher whose keystream is completely random. Ordinary stream ciphers use a pseudo-random keystream generated from a much shorter key. That difference is exactly why the OTP is impractical but real stream ciphers are not.

Block Cipher Structures: S-boxes, P-boxes, Feistel and SPN

Inside a block cipher: substitution boxes and permutation boxes, wired together into rounds, and the two famous network shapes that arrange them.

In a block cipher, the two classical techniques become hardware-friendly building blocks:

  • Substitution is implemented via substitution boxes (S-boxes).
  • Permutation (transposition) is implemented via permutation boxes (P-boxes).

These are executed over multiple rounds of the algorithm to improve diffusion and confusion. The rounds are arranged into structures called "networks". Two networks dominate this unit.

The Feistel cipher

The Feistel cipher defines the steps of a block cipher over multiple rounds. Every round has the same structure:

  1. Split the data block X into two halves: Ln (left) and Rn (right).
  2. Substitution is performed on one half — the right half goes through a keyed function F.
  3. Permutation is the interchange of the two halves: the output of F is XORed with the left half, and the halves swap.
Feistel round
Ln+1 = Rn
Rn+1 = Ln ⊕ F(Rn, keyn)

Because the halves swap each round, the same structure can be run backwards for decryption — a major design advantage. The Feistel structure is used in many block ciphers: DES, Blowfish, Twofish, RC6. We will look inside the function F when we reach DES.

The substitution-permutation network (SPN)

An alternative network structure is the Substitution-Permutation Network (SPN), which is what AES uses. Rather than swapping halves, an SPN alternates layers of S-boxes and P-boxes across the whole block each round, mixing everything together. The two structures are the main ways of arranging S-boxes and P-boxes.

🧠
Memory hook: Feistel swaps halves; SPN mixes the whole block. DES = Feistel. AES = SPN. If you remember only that pairing, you can answer a lot of exam questions.
🤔
Pause & predict: In a Feistel round, which half of the data passes through the function F, and what is done with F's output? (Answer: the right half passes through F; its output is XORed with the left half, and the halves are then interchanged.)

DES, 3DES and Block Cipher Design Principles

DES was the first modern standard: a beautiful 16-round Feistel design with a fatal flaw — a 56-bit key.

The Data Encryption Standard (DES) was published in 1977 and standardised in 1979. It is a block cipher with a Feistel structure between rounds. Key facts:

  • The key in each round is a subkey derived from the actual key (the round key).
  • There are a total of 8 S-boxes.
  • Outputs from the S-boxes are sent through one P-box to achieve a good spread over the S-boxes in the next round.
  • It runs for 16 rounds, and it can be shown that 16 really is necessary to achieve security.

S-boxes and why decryption still works

S-boxes are look-up tables: they map an input block of bits to an output block of bits, and the mapping is carefully chosen and vetted. Changing one input bit flips, on average, half the output bits — a diffusion effect. But how can decryption be possible if the S-box scrambles bits? There are two options:

  • Invertible S-boxes: every possible output has a unique input that produced it, so the transformation can simply be reversed.
  • Invertible arrangement: even if individual S-boxes are not invertible, the way they are combined in the overall algorithm can still make the whole cipher reversible. This is what DES uses.
ℹ️
Worked S-box example (S[0] of DES): the input is 6 bits, (b0, b1, b2, b3, b4, b5). Bits b0 and b5 give the row; bits (b1, b2, b3, b4) give the column. For input (1,1,0,1,1,1): row = (11)2 = (3)10, column = (1011)2 = (11)10. The table cell at that position reads 14, so the output is (14)10 = (1110)2 = 1110. DES has eight S-boxes like this one.

P-boxes

Transposition is handled by P-boxes, which are simple index permutations — re-orderings of bits. P-boxes are used together with S-boxes to form a chain: S-boxes → P-box → S-boxes, and so on. A P-box takes the output from the S-boxes and distributes the bits of one S-box across as many of the following S-boxes as possible, ensuring effective diffusion.

The operation of DES in detail

DES accepts 64-bit data blocks and has a 56-bit key. The round-key generator produces 16 round keys of 48 bits each. Inside each round:

  1. The 64-bit input is split into two 32-bit halves (left and right).
  2. The right half goes through the function F. First, its 32 bits are expanded to 48 bits using an expansion box, so it matches the round-key size.
  3. The expanded output is XORed with the 48-bit round key.
  4. The resulting 48-bit intermediate is sent to the 8 S-boxes. Each S-box takes a 6-bit input (48 ÷ 8 = 6) and produces a 4-bit output, giving 32 bits total — exactly right for the next round.
  5. This process repeats 16 times.

The security of DES

DES is no longer considered secure. Its major limitation is the short key length. There are 256 possible keys, a key space of about 7.2 × 1016. Modern rates make this far too small:

Attacker's speedTime to break DES
109 keys/second (a multicore computer)≈ 1.2 years
1013 keys/second (a supercomputer)≈ 1 hour
ℹ️
Example calculation (learn this method): key space = 256 = 7.2 × 1016. Brute force tries all keys in the worst case, but on average you find the key after trying half of them, so assume 255 attempts. At 109 keys/second: 255 ÷ 109 ≈ 36,028,797 seconds ≈ 1.2 years. At 1013 keys/second: 255 ÷ 1013 ≈ 3,600 seconds ≈ 1 hour. (The lecture notes flag a typo in the last row of the summary table — work it out yourself using this method.)

3DES — the intermediate fix

As an intermediate solution to the risks of DES, 3DES (Triple DES) was introduced. It applies DES three times, but with a twist. First encrypt with K1: EK1(p). Then decrypt with K2: DK2(EK1(p)) — note we use the decryption function, but with a different key, so we do not get the plaintext back. Then encrypt again with K3:

3DES (EDE)
C = EK3( DK2( EK1(p) ) )

3DES uses either two keys (K1, K2) or three keys (K1, K2, K3), giving effective key sizes of 112 bits (56 × 2) or 168 bits (56 × 3). The two options balance performance against security, and 168 bits puts brute force out of reach.

💡
Why Encrypt–Decrypt–Encrypt and not Encrypt–Encrypt–Encrypt? One key reason is backward compatibility: if you set all three keys equal (K1 = K2 = K3), then E then D then E collapses back to a single DES encryption. That means 3DES hardware can still speak plain DES when needed. The middle D is what makes this collapse possible.

Block cipher design principles

Three principles govern how a block cipher like DES or AES is designed:

  • Number of rounds. More rounds make cryptanalysis harder even if the function F is weak, providing the element of confusion in a Feistel cipher.
  • Design of function F. It should be nonlinear and have good avalanche properties — defined by the Strict Avalanche Criterion (SAC) and the Bit Independence Criterion (BIC). Both strengthen the confusion function.
  • Key schedule algorithm. It should generate one subkey per round from the main key, guaranteeing key/ciphertext SAC and BIC. No fully general principles exist yet, but SAC and BIC are the desired properties.
Strict Avalanche Criterion (SAC) — whenever a single input bit is complemented (flipped), each of the output bits changes with a 50% probability.
Bit Independence Criterion (BIC) — output bits j and k should change independently when any single input bit i is inverted, for all i, j and k.
🤔
Pause & predict: A colleague proposes protecting data by running DES three times with the same key. What have they actually built, and why is it not helpful? (Answer: with all three keys equal, EDE collapses to plain DES — same 56-bit security, three times the cost.)

The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)

Today's de-facto Internet cipher: an SPN over the finite field GF(28), chosen in an open competition, with four elegant round stages.

The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is the current de-facto block cipher on the Internet and the successor to DES. It was chosen in an open competition to replace DES. Key properties:

  • Operates at key lengths of 128, 192 and 256 bits, with a block size of 128 bits.
  • Uses S-boxes and P-boxes, but — unlike DES — it does not use a Feistel network. It is built on a substitution-permutation network (SPN).
  • Its S-boxes actually describe a mathematical function believed to achieve excellent diffusion and confusion. Cryptanalysts have tried to describe that function in simpler terms, but so far all attempts have failed.
ℹ️
Fun fact: strictly speaking, "AES is not a cipher". The algorithm that won the competition is the Rijndael cipher, and it was selected to become the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES). People use the names interchangeably.

Finite field arithmetic in AES

AES does its arithmetic in the finite field GF(28), using the irreducible polynomial m(x) = x8 + x4 + x3 + x + 1. That means the usual addition and multiplication are replaced by the finite-field versions we built earlier:

  • Multiplication of two bytes is multiplication in GF(28): polynomial multiply, then reduce modulo m(x).
  • Addition of two bytes is the bitwise XOR operation. For bytes A = (a7 a6 … a1 a0) and B = (b7 b6 … b1 b0), the sum is A + B = (c7 c6 … c1 c0) where each ci = ai XOR bi.

AES structure and key expansion

AES takes 128-bit blocks and an M-bit key (128, 192 or 256) and runs 10, 12 or 14 rounds respectively. For clarity, the lecture focuses on the 128-bit key case.

The key expansion algorithm takes the 16-byte key (4 words) and expands it into a linear array of 44 32-bit words — a total of 176 bytes (44 words × 4 bytes). This gives 11 round keys, each 128 bits (16 bytes): 11 × 16 = 176 bytes. The round key used in each round is 4 distinct words for AES-128, 6 words for AES-192, and 8 words for AES-256.

The four stages inside each AES round

Each full round applies four stages — one permutation and three substitutions:

StageTypeWhat it does
Substitute bytes (SubBytes)SubstitutionUses an S-box to perform a byte-by-byte substitution of the block.
ShiftRowsPermutationA simple permutation that shifts rows of the state.
MixColumnsSubstitutionA substitution that uses arithmetic over GF(28).
AddRoundKeyCombinationA simple bitwise XOR of the current block with a portion of the expanded key.

This structure is repeated round after round, with the S-boxes and finite-field arithmetic spreading and hiding information, which is why AES achieves such strong diffusion and confusion.

🧠
Memory hook for the four stages: Sub, Shift, Mix, Add. SubBytes changes values, ShiftRows moves them, MixColumns blends columns using GF(28) maths, and AddRoundKey folds in the key with XOR.
🤔
Pause & predict: Which of the four AES stages relies on the finite field GF(28), and which is the only one that uses the secret key directly? (Answer: MixColumns uses GF(28) arithmetic; AddRoundKey is the stage that XORs in the round key.)

Cipher Block Modes

Block ciphers fix one block; real messages are longer. Block modes chain the blocks — and a badly used mode can destroy an otherwise strong cipher.

Ciphers must handle messages of arbitrary length. The solution is to split messages into blocks and process them according to a cipher block mode. Crucially, these modes of operation can introduce new security problems if they are not designed and used properly.

Classic block modes

Only encrypt data: Electronic Codebook (ECB), Cipher Block Chaining (CBC), Counter (CTR), and others such as CFB and OFB.

Modern modes

Provide authenticated encryption (AE, AEAD), combining encryption with integrity protection: Galois Counter Mode (GCM), Counter-with-CBC-MAC (CCM), and others.

Electronic Codebook (ECB)

In ECB, each plaintext block is encrypted independently: ci = Enck(mi). It is simple and good for teaching, but do not use it beyond that.

⚠️
The problem with ECB: plaintext usually contains repeating patterns (think of the uniform white background of an image). In ECB, the same plaintext block always produces the same ciphertext block, so those patterns remain visible in the ciphertext. The classic demonstration is an image encrypted with ECB where the picture's outline is still recognisable. This is why ECB is not used in practice today.

Cipher Block Chaining (CBC)

CBC fixes ECB by chaining the blocks: each plaintext block is XORed with the previous ciphertext block before being encrypted, so the encryption of each block depends on all previous ones.

CBC
Encrypt: ci = Enck( ci−1 ⊕ mi )
Decrypt: mi = Deck( ci ) ⊕ ci−1

The first block has no previous ciphertext, so it is XORed with an initialisation vector (IV). This ensures that even identical plaintext blocks produce different ciphertexts, masking patterns. The same plaintext no longer maps to the same ciphertext.

Initialisation Vector (IV) — a random or pseudo-random value used as the first input in the encryption process. Its purpose is to introduce randomness so that encrypting the same plaintext twice with the same key gives different ciphertexts each time. The IV does not need to be kept secret and is often transmitted alongside the ciphertext so the receiver can decrypt. Its security comes from unpredictability, not secrecy.
⚠️
The IV must never repeat with the same key. If the same IV and key encrypt identical plaintext blocks, the resulting ciphertext blocks will be identical — revealing patterns and reintroducing exactly the ECB vulnerability. This is a real-world failure, not a theoretical one.

Case study: Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP)

WEP, an early wireless-network security protocol, uses the RC4 stream cipher with a secret key and an IV. Its IV is only 24 bits long — far too small. After a modest amount of traffic, the same IV is likely to be reused; on a busy network, IVs repeat very quickly. When the same IV is reused with the same key, RC4 generates the same keystream. An attacker who captures multiple packets with the same IV can observe how that keystream interacts with different plaintexts. If parts of the plaintext are known or predictable (headers, protocol fields), the attacker can recover other plaintexts, eventually deduce the keystream, and finally recover the secret key itself. WEP is a textbook lesson in mode/IV misuse.

Counter Mode (CTR)

Although CBC overcomes ECB's limitations, it is inefficient: encryption is sequential, and each step depends on the previous output, so CBC cannot be parallelised. Counter mode solves this.

In CTR mode, a counter is incremented for each block. The counter combined with a nonce is encrypted to produce a keystream, which is then XORed with the plaintext. Because each block's keystream can be generated independently, the work can be parallelised — giving both efficiency and security.

Nonce — a "number used once", playing the same role in CTR mode as the IV plays in CBC. Like an IV, it does not need to be secret, but it must never be reused with the same key. Reusing a nonce with the same key regenerates the same keystream, letting an attacker recover plaintext by comparing repeated patterns.
🧠
Memory hook: ECB: same in, same out (bad). CBC: chain + IV (no parallel). CTR: counter + nonce (parallel). The one rule that unites CBC and CTR: the IV/nonce is public but must be unique per key.
🤔
Pause & predict: Your team needs to encrypt a large file on a GPU, and speed matters. Which classic mode would you pick — CBC or CTR — and why? (Answer: CTR, because its blocks are independent and can be processed in parallel; CBC is inherently sequential.)

Pseudo-Random Number Generation

Cryptography runs on randomness — keys, IVs, nonces, keystreams — but computers are deterministic, so we need generators whose output only looks random.

Random numbers are essential in cryptography because they introduce unpredictability and underpin security. They are used for:

  • Key distribution and reciprocal authentication schemes.
  • Session key generation — a fresh key for each communication session.
  • Generating keys for RSA public-key encryption (next week's lecture).
  • Generating a bit stream for symmetric stream encryption (the keystream we discussed).
  • Generating IVs and nonces, which prevent patterns emerging in encrypted data.

If an attacker could predict or reproduce these values, they could potentially decrypt messages or forge signatures. Without strong randomness, systems become vulnerable to attacks that exploit predictable patterns.

Pseudorandom Number Generator (PRNG) — outputs a deterministic sequence of numbers. It takes a seed value as its starting point, and the output sequence depends entirely on that seed. Given the same seed, you get the same sequence.
Cryptographically Secure PRNG (CSPRNG) — a PRNG that is cryptographically secure. It must satisfy two conditions: (1) it is computationally infeasible to brute-force the seed (try all possible values) to predict the output, and (2) it is computationally infeasible to distinguish the output sequence from true randomness. The only realistic option left to an attacker is to somehow know the seed.
💡
Where does the seed come from? If a PRNG is only as good as its seed, the seed itself must be unpredictable. In practice it is gathered from physical sources of entropy — timing jitter, hardware noise, mouse movements and so on — then fed into the generator. Predictable seeds are a classic real-world catastrophe.

Stream cipher standards you should know

ChaCha20 and Salsa20

A family of related stream ciphers by DJ Bernstein. They are the current de-facto standard for stream ciphers on the Internet, and form the basis of the CSPRNG in OpenBSD and Linux.

RC4

Used to be the de-facto standard for TLS/SSL. A feasible breakage was demonstrated in 2013. Avoid it today.

🧠
Memory hook: PRNG = deterministic from a seed. CSPRNG = that, plus you cannot brute-force the seed or spot the pattern. Remember the standards pairing: ChaCha20/Salsa20 = good, RC4 = avoid.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else, remember this.

1

Cryptography is the science of secure transmission across space and time. It delivers confidentiality (encryption), integrity (MACs, hashes), authentication (signatures, MACs) and non-repudiation (signatures).

2

Symmetric cryptography uses one key for both directions (ke = kd); asymmetric uses different keys and is based on hard maths problems. Symmetric ciphers shuffle and map symbols.

3

Kerkhoff's Principle: security must rest only on the secrecy of the key, never on the algorithm. Open design invites the "many eyes" that find flaws. CSS and A5/1 are cautionary tales.

4

Maths toolkit: XOR is self-inverse; a ≡ b (mod n) means the same remainder; the Extended Euclidean Algorithm finds modular inverses; Zp is a field iff p is prime; AES works in GF(28).

5

Historical ciphers are substitution or transposition. Caesar is monoalphabetic and falls to frequency analysis; Vigenère is polyalphabetic but falls to Kasiski plus frequency analysis; rail fence is pure transposition.

6

Shannon's goals: diffusion (each ciphertext bit depends on many plaintext bits; flip one bit, half the output flips) and confusion (each ciphertext symbol depends on several key symbols).

7

The one-time pad is perfectly secure (ciphertext reveals nothing about plaintext) if the key is truly random, at least as long as the message, and never reused. It is impractical because key distribution and true randomness are too hard. Reuse falls to crib dragging.

8

Attacker models, weakest to strongest: ciphertext-only, known plaintext, chosen plaintext, chosen ciphertext. A system is compromised if plaintexts or the decryption key leak.

9

Stream ciphers XOR a keystream (bit by bit); block ciphers encrypt fixed blocks (64 or 128 bits) with padding. Inside block ciphers: S-boxes (substitution) and P-boxes (permutation), arranged in Feistel (DES) or SPN (AES) networks.

10

DES is a 16-round Feistel cipher with eight S-boxes, 64-bit blocks and a 56-bit key (256 ≈ 7.2 × 1016). That key is too short — about 1 hour on a supercomputer — so it is insecure. 3DES chains E–D–E for 112- or 168-bit effective keys, and collapses back to DES when the keys match.

11

AES is the Internet's de-facto cipher: Rijndael, 128-bit blocks, 128/192/256-bit keys, 10/12/14 rounds, an SPN (not Feistel). Each round does SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns and AddRoundKey; MixColumns uses GF(28) arithmetic.

12

Block modes handle long messages: ECB leaks patterns (never use it), CBC chains blocks with an IV (no parallel), CTR uses a counter and nonce (parallelisable). Modern modes (GCM, CCM) add authenticated encryption. CSPRNGs turn unpredictable seeds into keystreams; ChaCha20/Salsa20 are current, RC4 is broken.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Cryptography
The science of securely transmitting data spatially (across distance) and temporally (across time). It provides confidentiality, integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.
Plaintext (p)
The non-encrypted, unprotected data, built from symbols of an alphabet R = {r1, …, rn}. English, for example, uses letters and digits.
Ciphertext (c)
The encrypted output of the cipher. It need not use the same alphabet, or alphabet size, as the plaintext; its alphabet is S = {s1, …, sm}.
Key
The secret value that parameterises a cipher. Encryption uses ke and decryption uses kd; in symmetric cryptography ke = kd or kd is derivable from ke.
Symmetric cryptography
Cryptography where the encryption and decryption keys are the same (ke = kd), also called shared-key cryptography. Algorithms shuffle and map symbols.
Asymmetric cryptography
Cryptography where the keys differ (ke ≠ kd) and it is infeasible to derive kd from ke. Also called public-key cryptography; based on hard maths problems.
Kerkhoff's Principle
Security must depend only on the secrecy of the keys, never on the secrecy of the algorithm. Open design lets many experts find flaws faster.
Confusion
Shannon's goal that each ciphertext symbol depends on several key symbols, so the relationship between ciphertext and key is complex. Achieved mainly by substitution.
Diffusion
Shannon's goal that every ciphertext symbol depends on many plaintext symbols; on average, flipping one plaintext bit changes half the ciphertext bits. Achieved mainly by transposition.
Caesar cipher
The simplest substitution cipher: every letter is shifted by n positions (n = 3 gives a → d). Monoalphabetic, so it is trivially broken by frequency analysis.
Vigenère cipher
A polyalphabetic cipher that applies a different Caesar shift per letter, cycling through a keyword. Ci = (Pi + Ki mod |K|) mod 26.
Kasiski examination
A technique to find a Vigenère key length: find repeated ciphertext patterns, measure the distances between them, and take the greatest common divisor. Repeated patterns arise when the same plaintext is encrypted by the same key part.
One-Time Pad (OTP)
A stream cipher with c = o ⊕ p where the random key o is at least as long as the plaintext and never reused. Perfectly secure under those conditions, but impractical to distribute.
Stream cipher
A cipher that encrypts one bit or byte at a time by XORing the plaintext with a pseudo-random keystream derived from the key. Examples: RC4 (insecure), Salsa20.
Block cipher
A cipher that encrypts fixed-size blocks (typically 64 or 128 bits), padding the last block if needed. Examples: DES (insecure), AES.
S-box (substitution box)
A carefully chosen look-up table mapping an input bit block to an output bit block; implements substitution. Flipping one input bit flips about half the output bits.
P-box (permutation box)
A simple index permutation that re-orders bits (transposition). Chained with S-boxes to spread the bits of one S-box across many later S-boxes.
Feistel cipher
A block cipher structure that splits the data into halves and repeats the same round: substitute on one half, then swap. Used by DES, Blowfish, Twofish and RC6.
DES
Data Encryption Standard: a 16-round Feistel block cipher with eight S-boxes, 64-bit blocks and a 56-bit key. Standardised in 1979 and now insecure due to its short key.
3DES
Triple DES: applies Encrypt–Decrypt–Encrypt with two or three keys, giving effective key sizes of 112 or 168 bits. Collapses to plain DES if all keys are equal.
AES
Advanced Encryption Standard: the Rijndael cipher, the Internet's de-facto block cipher. 128-bit blocks, 128/192/256-bit keys, 10/12/14 rounds, an SPN using GF(28) arithmetic.
XOR (⊕)
Bitwise exclusive OR: output 1 when the bits differ, 0 when they match. Self-inverse (a ⊕ a = 0), so encrypting and decrypting with a keystream use the same operation.
Modular arithmetic & congruence
"Clock arithmetic": a mod n is the remainder after dividing a by n, always in 0…n−1. Values are congruent, a ≡ b (mod n), when n divides (a − b), i.e. they share the same remainder. Congruence is transitive (a ≡ c) but is not equality (a = c).
Finite field (GF)
A field with finitely many elements, of size pk for prime p. GF(p) is the integers mod a prime; GF(28) is AES's 256-element field of bytes.
Discrete logarithm problem
Given g, p and h = gx mod p, find x. Believed hard for large primes; the basis of Diffie–Hellman and ElGamal. A primitive root g generates every non-zero residue.
Electronic Codebook (ECB)
A block mode that encrypts each block independently: ci = Enck(mi). Identical plaintext blocks give identical ciphertext blocks, so patterns leak. Do not use it.
Cipher Block Chaining (CBC)
A block mode that XORs each plaintext block with the previous ciphertext before encrypting: ci = Enck(ci−1 ⊕ mi). Masks patterns but is sequential, so it cannot be parallelised.
Initialisation Vector (IV)
A random value XORed with the first block in CBC. It need not be secret but must never repeat with the same key, otherwise patterns reappear. Transmitted with the ciphertext.
Nonce
A "number used once", combined with a counter in CTR mode to build a keystream. Like an IV: public, but never reused with the same key.
CSPRNG
Cryptographically Secure Pseudo-Random Number Generator: a PRNG whose seed cannot feasibly be brute-forced and whose output cannot be distinguished from true randomness. Only the seed need be secret.

No terms in this category.

Q1

In a cryptosystem, c = p XOR k. With p = 10111011 and k = 00111101, state which are true: (a) c = 10000110; (b) c XOR p = k; (c) p = c XOR k; (d) an attacker needs only one plaintext/ciphertext pair to derive the key.

Answer: All of (a), (b), (c) and (d) are true. (a) 10111011 XOR 00111101 = 10000110, so c = 10000110. (b) 10000110 XOR 10111011 = 00111101 = k — a property of XOR. (c) 10000110 XOR 00111101 = 10111011 = p — again the self-inverse property. (d) Since c = p XOR k, one known pair lets the attacker compute k = c XOR p; this is the known-plaintext attack.
Q2

'BWGWBHQSJBBKNF' is a Vigenère ciphertext produced with a two-letter key. You also know the second plaintext letter is "I". What is the correct plaintext, and how do you know? (Options: AIMISTHEANSWER, SIXISTHEANSWER, AIDISTHEANSWER, HITISTHEANSWER.)

Answer: SIXISTHEANSWER. The key idea is that the same key letter plus the same plaintext letter gives the same ciphertext. With a two-letter key, positions 2, 4, 6, … share the second key letter; the ciphertext is W at positions 2 and 4, so if position 2 is "I" then position 4 must also be "I". Also positions 1, 5 and 11 share the same key letter and all show ciphertext B, so their plaintext letters must be equal. Only SIXISTHEANSWER has its 1st, 5th and 11th characters the same. (The other options can be eliminated on this basis.)
Q3

Which statement about the one-time pad is NOT true: (a) once generated, OTPs can be reused; (b) OTP length must be at least as long as the plaintext; (c) practical use of OTP is difficult; (d) OTP gives perfect security?

Answer: (a) is NOT true — an OTP can be used only once. If the key is reused and an attacker has one message in both plaintext and ciphertext form, they can recover the key (a known-plaintext attack). The other three are true: the key must be at least as long as the plaintext (to avoid repetition); key distribution makes practical use difficult; and the OTP provides perfect secrecy because the ciphertext gives no information about the plaintext.
Q4

What is TRUE about the initialisation vector (IV) in symmetric cryptography: (a) the IV must be kept secret; (b) IVs can be repeated; (c) the IV ensures the same plaintext block does not generate the same ciphertext; (d) IVs are used in ECB mode?

Answer: Only (c) is true. The IV's purpose is to ensure that identical plaintext blocks do not produce identical ciphertext blocks. (a) is false — the IV need not be secret and is usually sent in plaintext with the ciphertext. (b) is false — repeating an IV with the same key reintroduces the OTP-reuse problem and reveals patterns. (d) is false — ECB is precisely the mode with no IV; IVs are used in modes such as CBC to fix ECB's weaknesses.
Q5

Which are TRUE about congruence modulo n: (a) 26 ≡ 11 (mod 5); (b) 74 ≡ 30 (mod 11); (c) if a ≡ b (mod n) and b ≡ c (mod n) then a ≡ c (mod n); (d) if a ≡ b (mod n) and b ≡ c (mod n) then a = c?

Answer: (a), (b) and (c) are true; (d) is false. (a) 26 mod 5 = 1 and 11 mod 5 = 1. (b) 74 mod 11 = 8 and 30 mod 11 = 8. (c) Congruence is transitive: write a = k1·n + p1 and b = k2·n + p1, and b = k3·n + p2 and c = k4·n + p2; combining gives c = (k4 + k2 − k3)·n + p1, so a ≡ c. (d) False — give a counter-example: a = 26, b = 11, c = 16, n = 5. All are congruent mod 5, yet a ≠ c. Congruence is not equality.
Q6

Which of the following is NOT a finite field under the given modulus: (a) {0,1} mod 2; (b) {0,1,2,3,4,5} mod 6; (c) {0,1,2,3,4,5,6} mod 7; (d) {0,1,…,30} mod 31?

Answer: (b) is not a field. The rule is that {0,1,…,p−1} is a field when p is prime, so (a) mod 2, (c) mod 7 and (d) mod 31 are all fields. For (b), the modulus 6 is not prime, so some elements lack a multiplicative inverse. Take 2: 2×0 = 0, 2×1 = 2, 2×2 = 4, 2×3 = 0, 2×4 = 2, 2×5 = 4 (all mod 6) — the value 1 never appears, so 2 has no inverse and the set is not a field.
Q7

"DES is still considered secure." TRUE or FALSE?

Answer: FALSE. DES is insecure because of its short 56-bit key. With 256 keys (≈ 7.2 × 1016), brute force takes roughly 1.2 years at 109 keys/second on a multicore computer, and only about an hour at 1013 keys/second on a supercomputer. This is why AES, with 128-, 192- and 256-bit keys, replaced it.
Q8

AES uses a … bit block size and a key size of … bits. (a) 128; 128 or 256 (b) 64; 128 or 192 (c) 256; 128, 192 or 256 (d) 128; 128, 192 or 256.

Answer: (d) 128; 128, 192 or 256. AES is a block cipher with a fixed 128-bit block size and three supported key sizes: 128, 192 or 256 bits, giving 10, 12 or 14 rounds respectively.
Q9

"Like DES, AES also uses a Feistel structure." TRUE or FALSE?

Answer: FALSE. AES does not use a Feistel structure; it uses a substitution-permutation network (SPN). Each full AES round consists of four separate functions: byte substitution (SubBytes), permutation (ShiftRows), arithmetic operations over a finite field (MixColumns), and XOR with a key (AddRoundKey). DES is the Feistel one.
Q10

Compute the output of the DES S-box S[0] for the input 010000. (Options: 0011, 0001, 0000, 1010.)

Answer: 0011. Split the 6-bit input 010000: bits b0 and b5 give the row — b0 = 0 and b5 = 0, so row = (00)2 = 0. Bits b1 b2 b3 b4 give the column — (1000)2 = 8. The table cell at row 0, column 8 reads 3, which is (0011)2. So the output is 0011.
Q11

Distinguish confusion from diffusion, name the classical technique used to achieve each, and state what a one-bit plaintext change should do on average.

Answer: Confusion hides the relationship between ciphertext and key: each ciphertext symbol must depend on several key symbols, and it is achieved mainly by substitution. Diffusion spreads the influence of each plaintext bit across the ciphertext: every ciphertext symbol must depend on many plaintext symbols, and it is achieved mainly by transposition. On average, flipping one plaintext bit should change half of the ciphertext bits.
Q12

Explain why ECB is unsafe and how CBC fixes it. What is the IV's role, and what single rule must the IV obey?

Answer: In ECB each block is encrypted independently, so identical plaintext blocks always produce identical ciphertext blocks — patterns in the plaintext (such as an image's background) remain visible in the ciphertext. CBC XORs each plaintext block with the previous ciphertext block before encrypting, so each block's encryption depends on all the earlier ones and identical plaintexts produce different ciphertexts. The first block is XORed with the IV, which introduces randomness. The IV need not be secret, but it must never be reused with the same key, otherwise identical plaintext blocks again map to identical ciphertext blocks and the ECB weakness returns.
Q13

Describe 3DES's operation and explain why it uses an Encrypt–Decrypt–Encrypt sequence rather than Encrypt–Encrypt–Encrypt.

Answer: 3DES encrypts with K1, then applies DES decryption with K2, then encrypts again with K3: C = EK3(DK2(EK1(p))). Using two keys gives 112 bits of effective key; using three gives 168 bits, making brute force infeasible. The middle Decrypt step is not a mistake: it provides backward compatibility. If all three keys are set equal, the E–D–E sequence collapses back to a single DES encryption, so existing DES hardware can still be used. It is not a matter of convenience either — the goal is simply to avoid DES's 56-bit key weakness.
Q14

Two messages are encrypted with the same one-time pad. Show how an attacker can attack them without knowing the key, and describe crib dragging.

Answer: With C1 = P1 ⊕ K and C2 = P2 ⊕ K, the attacker XORs the two ciphertexts: C1 ⊕ C2 = (P1 ⊕ K) ⊕ (P2 ⊕ K) = P1 ⊕ P2, because K ⊕ K is all zeros. The key cancels out, leaving the XOR of the two plaintexts. The attacker then uses crib dragging: guess a likely word (for example "the" in English), slide it along every position, XOR it with P1 ⊕ P2, and look for readable text appearing in the other message. Extend successful guesses and repeat. Reusing any part of an OTP therefore destroys its perfect secrecy.
