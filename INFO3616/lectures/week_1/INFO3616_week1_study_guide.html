INFO3616 · Week 1 · Introduction to Security Engineering

Programming Satan's Computer: Building Systems That Stay Dependable

This week asks a deceptively simple question: what does it take to build a computer system that keeps behaving correctly when a clever, hostile opponent is actively trying to make it fail? You will meet a practical framework for thinking about security, the goals a secure system must achieve, the classic design principles that guide engineers, and the attackers you are up against.

Read this first: Hold onto one mental model for the whole week — security engineering is engineering under attack. Ordinary engineering deals with accidents, wear and tear; security engineering deals with an opponent who reads your design and deliberately chooses the worst possible moment to break it. Every framework, goal, principle and attack you meet this week is a tool for that single problem.
~25 min read 16 teaching sections 12 practice questions 30 key terms

By the end you will be able to

  • Define engineering, security engineering, safety engineering and reliability engineering, and tell them apart.
  • Explain the "Programming Satan's Computer" idea and why a hostile opponent makes security so hard.
  • Name and apply the four components of the security framework: policy, mechanisms, assurance and incentives.
  • Use the framework to analyse a real incident, including the 2013 Target data breach.
  • Define each security goal — confidentiality, integrity, authenticity, authorisation, accountability, non-repudiation, deniability, availability and privacy — and spot overlaps.
  • Recall the fundamental security design principles and give an example of each.
  • Classify attacks as passive or active, and distinguish attack vectors, attack surfaces and attack trees.
  • Describe the main categories of opponent and explain why security is a process, not a product.

Start with Engineering

Before we can define security engineering, we need a clear picture of what engineering itself is.

Engineering — the application of science and maths to solve problems. Scientists and inventors come up with theories and innovations; engineers take those discoveries and apply them to the real world.

Two everyday examples make the point. The principles of thermodynamics, studied in physics, are applied by mechanical engineers to design efficient heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) systems that control the climate in buildings. The principles of aerodynamics are applied by aerospace engineers to shape wings and fuselages so that aircraft reduce drag, increase lift and burn less fuel. In both cases, an abstract scientific idea becomes a practical, working product.

Security engineering follows exactly the same pattern. Just as thermodynamics is applied to HVAC and aerodynamics to aircraft, scientific and mathematical principles are applied to protect information and systems. Security engineering uses those principles to develop methods for protecting data, ensuring secure communication, and mitigating risks in both digital and physical environments.

💡
Why it matters: security is not a bolt-on or a separate discipline from "real" engineering. It is the same discipline — applying science and maths — pointed at a harder problem, because the "environment" now includes a thinking adversary.
🤔
Pause & predict: Name one engineering field you know, and one scientific principle it borrows. Now ask yourself: what changes when the thing your system must survive is not physics, but a person trying to defeat you?

What Is Security Engineering?

The defining feature of security is not complexity — it is the presence of a hostile opponent who can alter messages at will.

Programming Satan's Computer

The early definition of security engineering comes from an essay by Ross Anderson and Roger Needham titled "Programming Satan's Computer" (1995). Their key insight, in their own words:

ℹ️
"Our task is to program a computer which gives answers which are subtly and maliciously wrong at the most inconvenient possible moment." The problem, they wrote, is "the presence of a hostile opponent, who can alter messages at will."

Cryptographic protocols — the rules that distributed systems use to identify users and authenticate transactions — often involve only about 2–5 messages. You might think such a small program would be easy to get right. It is not. Bugs are routinely found in well-known protocols years after they were first published, precisely because the opponent is not a passive observer: they can tamper with messages in ways that are hard to anticipate. In effect, the security engineer must write software that stays correct even when an adversary is actively lying to it.

A historical illustration is the Enigma machine of World War II. The Germans believed their cryptographic device was unbreakable, yet Allied cryptanalysts — including Alan Turing — cracked its codes. The lesson is twofold: not only must you build robust encryption, but you must also anticipate and defend against your adversary's creative, evolving strategies.

Distributed systems

Throughout this unit, the term distributed system means any system where components located on different networked computers communicate and coordinate their actions by passing messages. Distributed systems commonly use a client–server architecture, but they also include peer-to-peer networks, cloud computing and other decentralised models. The Satan's Computer problem applies to all of them, and in fact to anything requiring security: hardware, software or networks.

The formal definition

Security engineering — conceiving, designing and implementing hardware and software that produces only the expected answers, even when confronted with malice, error or mischance. As a discipline, it focuses on the tools, processes and methods needed to design, implement and test complete systems, and to adapt existing systems as their environment evolves.

A secure system tolerates the activities of the opponent. But be careful with the word "secure": there is no such thing as a perfectly secure system. Security is multi-faceted and requires cross-disciplinary expertise — from cryptography and computer security, through hardware tamper-resistance, all the way to economics, applied psychology, organisations and the law.

This is what separates security from ordinary systems engineering. Business-process analysis, software engineering, testing and evaluation are all important, but they are not sufficient, because they deal only with error and mischance — not malice. The security engineer also needs adversarial thinking, much like a chess player. You must study the attacks that have worked in the past: their openings, their development and their outcomes.

🧠
Memory hook: Safety and reliability engineering ask "what if something goes wrong?" Security engineering adds "what if someone makes it go wrong on purpose, at the worst possible moment?"
🤔
Pause & predict: A protocol exchanges just three messages. How many ways could an attacker who can rewrite those messages break it? Keep this question in mind when we reach active attacks later in the week.

Why It Matters: The Threat Landscape

Cyber threats are growing in number and variety, and they cost real money — which is why we need structured thinking, not guesswork.

Cyber threats come in many forms, and they keep multiplying:

  • Ransomware — malware that encrypts your files and demands payment.
  • Data breaches — the unauthorised exposure of private data.
  • DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) — flooding a service so legitimate users cannot reach it.
  • Viruses, worms and trojans — malicious software of different kinds.
  • Phishing, spear phishing, vishing and smishing — fraudulent emails, targeted emails, phone calls and text messages that impersonate a trusted party.
  • Business Email Compromises (BEC) — attackers hijack or spoof a business email account to trick staff into paying or sharing data.

These attacks cost businesses and individuals millions. The global average cost of a single data breach in 2025 was 4.4 million USD. That figure alone explains why organisations cannot treat security as an afterthought.

What security actually looks like

Popular culture imagines cybersecurity as dramatic hacker-hunting. The reality is far more mundane — and far more about steady process than heroics.

What people think it is like

  • Catching hackers (a large slice of the job)
  • Drinking coffee (a small slice)

What it is actually like

  • Security awareness
  • Patching
  • Documentation
  • Troubleshooting
  • Meetings
  • Compliance assessments
  • Incident response
  • Vendor management
  • Resetting passwords
💡
Why it matters: because the threats are varied and costly, we need structured thinking about security rather than ad-hoc reactions. The framework in the next sections gives us that structure.

Security vs Safety vs Reliability Engineering

Three disciplines that all protect systems — but only one of them assumes a hostile opponent.

Safety engineering — primarily concerned with preventing accidents and ensuring systems operate without causing harm to people, property or the environment. Safety engineers identify hazards, assess risks, design safety features, develop risk-management procedures, and investigate accidents to prevent recurrence.
Reliability engineering — ensures systems and components perform their intended functions without failure over a specified period. Its goal is to enhance dependability and availability, minimising downtime and maintenance costs.

Neither of these considers a hostile opponent, and that is exactly what differentiates them from security engineering. Safety and reliability deal with known, predictable factors (a component wearing out, a power cut, a design flaw). Security engineering additionally assumes someone is deliberately trying to cause harm.

A Framework for Security Engineering

To reason about security systematically, we need a reference structure — and this one lets us trace any incident back to a component that failed.

Why do we need a framework at all? Three reasons:

  • We need a reference for designing and assessing the security of a distributed system.
  • A framework assists investigations after a security incident, helping us understand why security failures occur.
  • Later you will see that any cybersecurity incident can be traced back to a failure in one of the framework's components.

The framework used in this unit comes from Ross Anderson's textbook, Security Engineering. To build genuinely dependable distributed systems, four things must come together: policy, mechanisms, assurance and incentives. They are interconnected — a weakness in one weakens the whole.

📜 Policy

What we want to achieve — what it means for this system to be secure.

⚙️ Mechanisms

The tools and machinery at our disposal to enforce the policy.

🔍 Assurance

Confidence in the mechanisms' reliability — how much we can rely on them and how well they work together.

🎯 Incentives

The motivations of both the system's defenders and its attackers.

🧠
Memory hook: think of it as P-M-A-I — Policy, Mechanisms, Assurance, Incentives. "Please Make Attacks Irrelevant." Policy says the goal, mechanisms do the work, assurance checks the work, incentives explain who cares and why.
🤔
Pause & predict: A company buys the best firewall money can buy but has no written rule about who may access customer data. Which framework components are missing? (Answer: policy — and probably assurance and incentives too.)

The Four Components in Detail

Each component answers a different question: what do we want, how do we do it, how much can we trust it, and who is motivated to help or harm?

Policy — what we mean to achieve

Policy — defines what we mean to achieve; in other words, what it means to keep the system secure. Policy says what, never how.

Policies are usually set by higher management and aim to ensure business continuity and to comply with regulations and standards. Two examples from the lecture:

  • "Customer data must be stored only within our data centres."
  • "Only authorised employees must be able to access the personal information of clients."

Notice that neither policy says how to achieve the goal — that is deliberate. Policy defines the destination; mechanisms provide the vehicle.

Mechanisms — the machinery

Mechanism — the machinery we have to implement policies.

Examples include encryption for data confidentiality, tamper-resistant hardware for physical security, and cryptographic hashes for data integrity. Security engineers must be aware of and familiar with the range of mechanisms needed to implement policy.

⚠️
Common trap: for most beginners, security engineering is all about the machinery. Remember that mechanisms are only one aspect of the framework. A perfect lock on a door nobody has decided to lock is useless.

Assurance — how much can we rely on it?

Assurance — how much reliance you can place on each particular mechanism, and how well they work together.

Assurance is about honest professional estimates. How long would it take to break RSA encryption with today's computers? How long to brute-force a 10-character password? Security engineers must be able to give realistic answers.

Assurance is time-bound
RSA-2048: classical computer → trillions of years · quantum computer → around 10 seconds

The RSA-2048 example is the crucial lesson: a mechanism considered secure under current hardware may not be secure in the future. Assurance is never permanent.

Incentives — who is motivated, and why?

Incentive — the motive that the people guarding and maintaining the system have to do their job, and the motive that attackers have to try to defeat your policy.

Different businesses attract different attackers. The lecture names four main kinds:

  • State actors — governments and their agencies.
  • Cybercriminals / ransomware groups — motivated by money.
  • Script kiddies — inexperienced attackers using ready-made tools.
  • Hacktivists — motivated by political or ideological goals.

The methodical process of understanding the possible threats and prioritising defences is called threat modelling. Because security costs money, understanding incentives lets us decide how much security we need and which attacks to prioritise our defences against.

💡
Why it matters: security engineers must put risks and threats in context, make realistic assessments of what might go wrong, and give clients good advice. That depends on a wide understanding of what has gone wrong over time — which attacks worked, what they cost, and how they were stopped.
🤔
Pause & predict: A hospital and a fashion retailer both hold personal data. Would you spend the same on security for each? Use incentives to justify your answer.

Applying the Framework: Bank Security

Banks are the classic worked example: many systems, many people, a huge attack surface, and the constant threat of insiders.

Banks operate numerous security-critical computer systems. Many different applications run together and many people work together, producing a huge attack surface and real potential for insider attacks. Let us walk the framework through this setting.

Policy

  • Overall goal: maintaining correct account balances.
  • Sub-goals: safeguard the integrity, confidentiality and availability of ATMs and online banking; ensure the accountability of bank-to-bank transfers; and many more — too many to list.

Mechanisms

  • Strong bookkeeping procedures and access control, especially in the backend.
  • Anomaly detection systems that flag unusual transactions.
  • The four-eyes principle: large transfers typically need at least two people to authorise them.
  • ATMs use strong cryptographic protocols for authentication.

Assurance

  • Confidence in the bank's security depends heavily on how effective the mechanisms actually are.
  • The most significant perceived threat is internal — employees who might commit petty theft. Even the most robust bookkeeping and cryptography have weaknesses against insiders who know the systems.
  • Anomaly detection is genuinely hard: not all unusual transactions are illegitimate. Some are exceptional but authentic customer activity. The bank must balance sensitivity — flagging suspicious activity without disrupting legitimate banking.

Incentives

  • For attackers: obtain the customer's money without detection, using a series of transfers that obscure the trail so the funds become unrecoverable or are paid out.
  • For guards and admins: staff may want to leverage their position for personal advantage — an increasingly recognised attack vector. This is why employees must be carefully screened and vetted.
✅
Study tip: if you can retell this bank example from memory across all four components, you can handle almost any framework question in the exam.

Case Study: The 2013 Target Data Breach

A real incident analysed with the framework — and a lesson in how one missing component lets an attack run for weeks.

In late 2013, Target, one of the largest retail chains in the United States, suffered a massive data breach. Attackers installed malware in Target's POS (Point of Sale) systems and gained access to the credit and debit card information of approximately 40 million customers, plus the personal information of up to 70 million individuals.

ComponentWhat happened at Target
Policy Target had no policy ensuring third-party suppliers had a proper security posture. It granted network access to an HVAC contractor, Fazio Mechanical Services, without verifying their security practices. Attackers used the contractor's credentials to enter Target's network. Since then, many organisations have strengthened third-party risk management — requiring vendors to meet security standards, conducting audits and continuously monitoring third-party access.
Mechanisms Some mechanisms worked: the FireEye malware-detection tool detected the threat, and credit-card fraud-detection systems began flagging fraudulent transactions. But Target lacked proper incident-response mechanisms to act, so attackers continued for weeks.
Assurance FireEye was known to be reliable at detecting the malware. Whether other mechanisms — Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) and Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS) — worked as expected is unclear, mainly because of Target's inaction. The pass-the-hash method attackers used exposed the limits of Single Sign-On (SSO) without supplementary mechanisms such as the principle of least privilege.
Incentives Target was a lucrative target: a large company, a large customer base, and the high-volume holiday shopping season. It is unclear whether Target's security team had the incentive to do their job properly, as they failed to act on the FireEye alarms. Management appeared to prioritise holiday operations over promptly notifying affected customers.
⚠️
Lesson: a detection tool that works is not enough. If the surrounding policy, incident response and incentives are weak, an attack can continue long after it has been spotted. This is why the framework treats all four components as interconnected.
🤔
Pause & predict: If FireEye detected the malware, why did the breach still become one of the largest in US retail history? Name the component that failed most clearly.

Security Goals I: Confidentiality, Integrity, Authenticity

Security goals define what we are protecting — and they are what security policies are built from.

Security goals are used to define the security policies discussed earlier. The list is not exhaustive, and the goals sometimes overlap in context. We will meet nine of them. Here are the first three.

Confidentiality and secrecy

Confidentiality — preserves authorised restrictions on information access and disclosure, including means for protecting personal privacy and proprietary information. A loss of confidentiality is the unauthorised disclosure of information.

The most general understanding of confidentiality is the effect of mechanisms that limit who can access data in plaintext form. This means access control is a form of confidentiality. The related engineering term secrecy refers to the effect of mechanisms — such as cryptography or computer access controls — that limit the number of principals who can access information. Confidentiality adds an obligation to protect someone else's secrets if you know them.

The other common use of confidentiality is the goal of protecting the secret content of a message or data, usually by encryption. In this unit we do not distinguish between secrecy and confidentiality, and we rarely need the obligation aspect — so we simplify our terminology.

Data / message integrity

Integrity — the effect of a mechanism that allows a verifier to check whether a message has been modified in transit, or data has been altered since the last legitimate (honest) update. A loss of integrity is the unauthorised modification or destruction of information.

Defending against data modification in transit is generally hard: the attacker may hold a useful position in the network and can simply flip bits. The important effect of integrity is therefore that we can detect such changes. Integrity is very close in meaning to tamper-evident. It is not the same as tamper-resistance, which is the capability to withstand modification attempts and is more commonly used for hardware.

Authenticity

Authenticity — the effect that allows a verifier to determine the originator of a message or data item. It verifies that users are who they claim to be and that each input came from a trusted source.

Effectively implementing authenticity as a mechanism usually implies integrity as well. In cryptographic protocols, it also implies freshness — proof that the other party is active right now and not someone replaying a recorded message.

IdeaQuestion it answers
AuthenticityIs the origin of this message genuine?
IntegrityHas the message been altered in transit?
FreshnessIs this a new message, or an old one replayed?
⚠️
Common trap: authenticity is not the same as authentication. Authentication is the process of identifying the party we are communicating with; authenticity is the effect of being able to determine a message's origin.
🤔
Pause & predict: You receive a genuine, unaltered message — but it was recorded last year and replayed today. Which of the three ideas above has been violated? (Answer: freshness, and therefore authenticity.)

Security Goals II: Authorisation, Accountability, Non-repudiation, Deniability

These four goals are about who may do what, who did it, and whether they can later deny it.

Authorisation

Authorisation — the effect that a verifier can determine whether an entity is allowed to execute some action or access some data.

Authorisation often makes use of authentication but does not imply it. The classic example: when you enter a cinema, you must show a ticket (authorisation) — you are not required to prove who you are. Authorisation is closely linked to access control and accountability.

ℹ️
Worked example: Alice, a Finance Manager, tries to access the financial reporting system. Authorisation: the system checks her role and confirms she may access financial reports. Access control: based on that, she is allowed to view or edit the data. Accountability: every action she takes is logged with her user ID and timestamp for future audits.

Note that in this unit we treat access control as a mechanism, not a security goal, though some other materials classify it as a goal.

Accountability

Accountability — the effect that a verifier can determine which entity (or category of authorised entities) is responsible for a certain action. It lets an entity's actions be traced through the system.

Accountability implies authorisation and access control, and may imply authentication. It is closely related to non-repudiation. Because truly secure systems are not yet achievable, we must be able to trace a breach to a responsible party. That requires logging — and the logs must be:

  • Auditable — the verifier can access and trust the logs.
  • Protected — logs themselves must be secured, or an attacker will simply alter them.

Non-repudiation

Non-repudiation — the entity that caused an action cannot successfully deny responsibility. It guarantees that one cannot deny sending or receiving a transaction.

At its core, non-repudiation is a legal concept. Importantly, in law we do not need cryptographic proof — only plausible evidence to make a case. Accountability supports non-repudiation, and in cryptographic terms it requires authenticity, integrity and secure timestamping of the action (which is challenging to achieve).

Deniability

Deniability — the capability to successfully reject the notion of being responsible for some action.

In many ways deniability is the opposite of non-repudiation, and it can be more useful — especially in scenarios involving highly confidential communication. It can be achieved to a degree by cryptographic means. Be aware that the security understanding of deniability may differ from its interpretation by a court.

🤔
Pause & predict: A system logs every action with a user ID and timestamp. Which goal does that directly serve, and which related goal does it support? (Answer: it serves accountability and supports non-repudiation.)

Security Goals III: Availability and Privacy

A system that keeps secrets but cannot be used has failed; and privacy is about control, not just concealment.

Availability

Availability — a system's capability of providing its services when needed, even during an attack, error or mishap. It assures that systems work promptly and that service is not denied to authorised users. A loss of availability is the disruption of access to or use of information or an information system.

Availability includes the correct functioning of all relevant security mechanisms and network channels. Well-known DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) attacks are examples of attacks on availability.

Sometimes a lower level of security is exchanged for higher availability. In warfare, for example, the availability of communication channels may be prioritised over confidentiality — commanders must be able to communicate, so encryption might be switched off if necessary. Availability is one of the most important properties in security, and very hard to achieve: many systems are asymmetric by design (an exploitable property), and attackers have a huge attack surface to choose from.

Privacy

Privacy — an entity's ability or right to control what information relating to themselves they release or hide.

There is no single straightforward definition, because privacy relates to society, law and technology. Technology can support privacy to a degree. Three distinct aspects appear in the lecture:

  • Privacy of static data — released data sets.
  • Privacy of dynamic data — database queries.
  • Privacy when moving through the network — traffic as it travels.

Privacy is related to anonymity and may also imply secrecy or confidentiality — but it is not the same as either.

Anonymity — the state of being unidentified or unidentifiable within a particular context. An individual's identity is unknown and cannot be traced back to them.

Privacy

Alice joins a health forum using her real name and address, but she controls what others can see. The forum protects her data and allows only authorised personnel to access it. She is identifiable, but she controls the visibility.

Anonymity

Bob joins the same forum as "HealthSeeker123" and gives no real personal information. The forum logs no IP address or identifying data, so his identity remains completely unknown.

🧠
Memory hook: privacy = "I decide who sees my data." Anonymity = "nobody knows it is me." You can have privacy without anonymity (Alice) and anonymity without controlling much else (Bob).

Fundamental Design Principles I

No technique can eliminate all flaws — but these widely agreed principles guide the design of protection mechanisms.

Despite years of research, it has not been possible to develop design and implementation techniques that systematically exclude security flaws and prevent all unauthorised actions. Instead, we have a set of fundamental security design principles that guide the development of protection mechanisms. Here are the first seven.

Economy of mechanism

The design of security measures in hardware and software should be as simple and small as possible. Simple designs are easier to test and verify thoroughly; complex designs offer far more opportunities for an adversary to discover subtle weaknesses. Example: UNIX is modular with a small codebase, and its philosophy lives on in Linux and macOS. Windows Vista, by contrast, had many complex features and a much larger codebase, increasing the attack surface.

Fail-safe defaults

Access decisions should be based on permission rather than exclusion: the default is no access, and the protection scheme identifies the conditions under which access is permitted. This is a better failure mode than defaulting to allow. Example: file permissions default to denying access and require explicit permission. The idea extends beyond access control. Counter-example: the POODLE attack exploits the SSL/TLS fallback mechanism, where a connection downgrades to the older, less secure SSL 3.0 if the stronger protocol negotiation fails.

Complete mediation

Every access must be checked against the access control mechanism. Systems should not rely on cached access decisions. In continuously operating systems, if decisions are remembered, careful thought must go into how changes in authority propagate. File systems appear to comply, but typically once a file is open no further check occurs. True complete mediation checks every read of a field or record — resource-intensive and rarely used. Example: Zero Trust Networks (ZTN) validate every access request against policy, regardless of previous authorisation or network location.

Open design

The design of a security mechanism should be open rather than secret. Encryption keys must be secret, but encryption algorithms should be open to public scrutiny, so experts can review them and users can have high confidence. This is the philosophy behind NIST's standardisation of encryption and hash algorithms. Counter-example: the Content Scramble System (CSS) used in DVDs kept its algorithm secret. In November 1999 Frank A. Stevenson published three exploits, and a group reverse-engineered CSS to create the DeCSS program. Security through obscurity is not reliable.

Kerkhoff's principle — a cryptographic system should be secure even if everything about the system, except the key, is public knowledge. It aligns closely with open design: security should rely on the secrecy of keys, not the obscurity of algorithms.

Separation of privilege

A practice in which multiple privilege attributes are required to access a restricted resource. A good example is multi-factor authentication, which requires a password and a smart card. It also applies to dividing a program into parts limited to the privileges each needs, mitigating potential damage. Example: on Berkeley-based UNIX, becoming root requires both knowing the root password and being in the wheel group (GID 0). Meeting either condition alone is insufficient.

Least privilege

Every process and every user should operate using the least set of privileges necessary to perform the task. Role-based access control is a good example: each role is assigned only the permissions it needs, and unless a permission is explicitly granted, access is denied. There is also a temporal aspect: administrators should hold special privileges only when necessary, and they should be withdrawn during ordinary activity — leaving them in place invites accidents. Example: Apache's www-data account runs the web server with only minimal permissions, so a compromise cannot escalate to system-wide control.

Least common mechanism

The design should minimise the functions shared by different users, providing mutual security. This reduces unintended communication paths and the hardware and software on which all users depend, making it easier to verify. Operating systems provide distinct user environments to isolate activity. Example: passwords must not be reused between different systems or services.

🧠
Memory hook for Part I: E-F-C-O-S-L-L — Economy, Fail-safe, Complete mediation, Open design, Separation of privilege, Least privilege, Least common mechanism. "Every Fool Can Open Some Locked Latch."

Pause & predict: A developer hides the source code of an encryption algorithm "so attackers cannot find bugs". Which principle does this violate, and what is the likely outcome? (Answer: open design; history says the flaws surface anyway.)

Fundamental Design Principles II

The remaining principles cover usability, isolation and defence in depth — because security that people disable is not security.

Psychological acceptability

Security mechanisms should not interfere unduly with users' work, while still meeting the needs of those who authorise access. If mechanisms hinder usability, users may simply turn them off. Where possible they should be transparent or minimally obstructive. They must also match the user's mental model of protection: if procedures do not make sense, users make errors. Example: fingerprint or facial recognition combines strong security with ease of use.

Isolation

Public access systems should be isolated from critical resources (data, processes and so on) to prevent disclosure or tampering. Where sensitivity is high, organisations may limit the number of systems storing the data and isolate them physically or logically. Physical isolation may mean no physical connection between public and critical resources; logical isolation uses layers of security services between them. Individual users' processes and files should also be isolated from one another. Example: Apple's Secure Enclave is a coprocessor that handles encryption, decryption and key management independently of the main processor, so keys stay safe even if the operating system is compromised.

Encapsulation

A specific form of isolation based on object-oriented functionality. Protection comes from encapsulating a collection of procedures and data objects in a domain of its own, so the internal structure is accessible only to the procedures of the protected subsystem, and those procedures may be called only at designated entry points. Example: in a healthcare database, users cannot query sensitive tables directly. A stored procedure such as GetPatientRecord and a view such as PublicPatientInfo control all access, enforcing policy, maintaining integrity and enabling auditing.

Modularity

Refers both to developing security functions as separate, protected modules and to using a modular architecture. Rather than implementing cryptographic functions in every protocol, build one common cryptographic module that many applications invoke — so effort concentrates on securing that single module. Modular architecture also lets parts of the security design be upgraded without redesigning the whole system. Example: AWS Key Management Service (KMS) provides centralised encryption and key management for services such as S3, EBS, RDS and Lambda, and can be upgraded without disrupting them.

Layering (defence in depth)

The use of multiple, overlapping protection approaches addressing the people, technology and operational aspects of information systems. If one protection fails or is circumvented, the system is not left unprotected. This is often called defence in depth. Example: a company uses MFA, role-based access control, patch management, antivirus, host and network firewalls, encryption and security training. Even if phishing steals a password, the attacker still faces MFA, access controls and antivirus.

Least astonishment

A program or user interface should always respond in the way least likely to astonish the user. Authorisation mechanisms, for instance, should be transparent enough that users intuitively understand how security goals map to mechanisms. Example: a standard library's ParseInteger(string, radix) function conventionally defaults to base 10, and only uses other bases when explicitly specified. JavaScript originally defaulted to base 8 for strings beginning with "0", causing confusion and bugs; this was discouraged in ECMAScript 3 and dropped in ECMAScript 5.

⚠️
Common trap: do not confuse separation of privilege (multiple attributes required together, like password + card) with least privilege (each actor gets the minimum permissions) or least common mechanism (minimise what is shared between users).

Security Attacks: Vectors, Surfaces and Trees

A security attack is any action that compromises security goals. The classic split is passive versus active.

Security attack — any action (or event) that compromises the security of information or information systems owned by an organisation — that is, any action that compromises the security goals.

A useful classification, used in ITU-T Recommendation X.800 (Security Architecture for OSI) and RFC 4949, divides attacks into passive and active.

Passive attacks

Attempt to learn or make use of information from the system without affecting system resources. They are in the nature of eavesdropping on, or monitoring of, transmissions. The goal is to obtain information being transmitted. The attacker does not interfere.

  • Release of message contents
  • Traffic analysis

Active attacks

Attempt to alter system resources or affect their operation. They involve modification of the data stream or the creation of a false stream, and are subdivided into four categories:

  • Replay — resending a captured valid message.
  • Masquerade — pretending to be another entity.
  • Modification of messages — altering a legitimate message.
  • Denial of service — preventing legitimate use.

Common actors in cryptography

These personas make cryptographic roles easier to discuss:

ActorRole
Alice (A)The sender or originator of a message.
Bob (B)The recipient or intended receiver of the message.
Eve (E)An eavesdropper who tries to intercept and possibly decipher the communication. The classic passive attacker.
Mallory (M)A malicious attacker who intercepts, alters or disrupts communications. The classic active attacker.
Trent (T)A trusted third party, such as a certification authority.

Other personas such as Carol, Dave, Charlie, Peggy and Victor exist but are not required in this unit.

Attack vector

Attack vector — a specific method or pathway through which a hacker or malicious actor gains unauthorised access to a computer or network system.
  • Phishing — fraudulent emails or messages that appear to come from a trusted source, tricking people into revealing sensitive information or clicking malicious links.
  • Malware — software designed to harm or exploit a programmable device, service or network, introduced via email attachments, software downloads or operating-system vulnerabilities.
  • Person-in-the-Middle (PitM) — interception of communication between two parties to steal or manipulate the exchanged data.

Attack surface

Attack surface — the reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities in a system; the sum of all the possible points where unauthorised users can enter or extract data.

Examples include open ports on outward-facing servers and the code listening on them; services available inside a firewall; code that processes incoming data, email, XML, office documents and industry-specific formats; interfaces, SQL and web forms; and employees with access to sensitive information, who are vulnerable to social engineering. Attack surfaces fall into three categories:

🌐 Network attack surface

Vulnerabilities over an enterprise network, wide-area network or the Internet — for example network protocol flaws used for denial of service, disruption of links, and intruder attacks.

💾 Software attack surface

Vulnerabilities in application, utility or operating-system code, with a particular focus on web server software.

🧑 Human attack surface

Vulnerabilities created by personnel or outsiders — social engineering, human error and trusted insiders.

Attack surface analysis assesses the scale and severity of threats. A systematic analysis of vulnerability points makes developers aware of where security mechanisms are needed. Once the surface is defined, designers can try to make it smaller, making the adversary's task harder, and it guides priorities for testing and hardening.

Attack trees

Attack tree — a branching, hierarchical data structure representing a set of potential techniques for exploiting security vulnerabilities.

Attack trees let analysts exploit the information available on attack patterns and document attacks in a structured form that reveals key vulnerabilities. They can guide both system design and the choice and strength of countermeasures. In the lecture's example — an attack tree for internet banking authentication — the root is the attacker's objective: compromise a user's account. The branches show the routes to that goal, and the shaded leaf nodes are the individual events that make up the attacks.

Root: compromise account→ User credential compromise→ Malicious software→ Leaf: hidden code, worms, social engineering…
🧠
Memory hook: a vector is one path in; the surface is all the paths combined; an attack tree is a map of how the paths connect to the goal.
🤔
Pause & predict: An attacker records the sounds of keyboard presses and infers what was typed, without interfering. Passive or active? (Answer: passive — they observe an artefact and do not alter the system.)

Who Is the Opponent?

Understanding attackers' nature and motivations is crucial for building effective defences — this is the incentives component in action.

As we saw under incentives, knowing who attacks and why shapes how much and what kind of security you need. The main categories of attacker are:

OpponentDescription and motivation
CriminalsBotnet herders, malware developers, spam senders, bulk account compromisers, targeted attackers, cash-out gangs and ransomware operators. Primary motivation: financial gain.
Internal attackersFraud and malicious activity by insiders — employees or contractors — who misuse their access to organisational resources.
State actorsGovernments and state-sponsored groups, often pursuing intelligence, disruption or strategic advantage.
Whistle-blowersIndividuals within intelligence agencies and secretive firms who reveal sensitive information — the "insider threat" in another guise.
Security researchersResearchers and academics who investigate vulnerabilities and report them. Motivated by curiosity, knowledge and professional recognition.
HacktivistsIndividuals or groups using hacking for political ends, social change or ideological goals. Includes hate campaigns.
Script kiddiesInexperienced attackers who use existing tools and scripts without deep understanding. They can still cause significant harm.
AI (soon)Autonomous or AI-assisted attackers are an emerging category to watch.
💡
Why it matters: a script kiddie, a nation-state and a whistle-blower all threaten different things for different reasons. Prioritising defences without understanding incentives wastes money on the wrong threats.

Security Is a Process, Not a Product

You cannot simply buy security and be done — it is continuous, dynamic work.

Security is a dynamic, continuous process, not a static product you can purchase. History teaches us that all products come with flaws. Because security has so many facets, it is not enough to build — or buy — a product and then consider yourself "secure". A number of other factors come into play:

  • People: users, admins and insiders all affect the system's real security.
  • Connections: systems must be linked, which adds complexity and new attack surface.
  • A dynamic environment: the environment is not static. A change in business value brings a change in attacker incentive; systems must be updated and integrated with others.
  • Complexity: the sheer complexity of systems meant to be secured by a single "security product" undermines the idea that one product can do the job.

The takeaway is that security demands constant vigilance and adaptation. You do not achieve it once; you keep working at it as the system, the business and the attackers all change.

⚠️
Common trap: treating a purchase — a firewall, an antivirus, a "secure" platform — as the finish line. The finish line keeps moving, so the work never stops.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else, remember this.

1

Engineering is applying science and maths to solve real problems; security engineering applies those same principles to protect information and systems.

2

Security engineering means building hardware and software that produces only expected answers even under malice, error or mischance. It goes beyond fault tolerance because it assumes a hostile opponent.

3

Safety and reliability engineering deal with accidents and failure; security engineering adds deliberate, adversarial action. That is the key distinction.

4

The framework has four interconnected parts: policy (what to achieve), mechanisms (the machinery), assurance (how much to rely on it) and incentives (who is motivated). Any incident traces back to one of them.

5

Assurance changes over time: RSA-2048 takes trillions of years on a classical computer but around 10 seconds on a quantum computer.

6

The 2013 Target breach shows all four components failing: weak third-party policy, detection without incident response, unproven assurance, and misaligned incentives.

7

Security goals are confidentiality, integrity, authenticity, authorisation, accountability, non-repudiation, deniability, availability and privacy. They often overlap.

8

Integrity is tamper-evident (detect changes), not tamper-resistant (withstand them). Authenticity implies integrity and freshness, and is not the same as authentication.

9

Privacy is control over your information; anonymity is being unidentifiable. They are related but different.

10

The fundamental design principles — economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability, isolation, encapsulation, modularity, layering and least astonishment — guide secure design.

11

Passive attacks eavesdrop; active attacks interfere. Passive examples are release of message contents and traffic analysis; active examples are replay, masquerade, modification and denial of service.

12

An attack vector is one path in; the attack surface is all paths combined; an attack tree maps how the paths reach the attacker's goal. And security is a process, not a product.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Engineering
The application of science and maths to solve problems — turning theories and innovations into real-world solutions.
Security engineering
Conceiving, designing and implementing hardware and software that produces only expected answers even under malice, error or mischance. It assumes a hostile opponent.
Safety engineering
Preventing accidents and ensuring systems do not harm people, property or the environment. It does not consider a hostile opponent.
Reliability engineering
Ensuring systems perform their intended functions without failure over a specified period, enhancing dependability and availability.
Policy
What we mean to achieve — the high-level definition of a secure system. Policy states what, not how.
Mechanism
The machinery used to implement policy, such as encryption, tamper-resistant hardware and cryptographic hashes.
Assurance
How much reliance you can place on each mechanism and how well they work together. It changes as technology advances.
Incentive
The motive of the people guarding the system to do their job, and the motive of attackers to defeat the policy.
Threat modelling
The methodical process of understanding possible threats and prioritising defence mechanisms accordingly.
Confidentiality
Preserving authorised restrictions on information access and disclosure. A loss of confidentiality is unauthorised disclosure of information.
Integrity
Allowing a verifier to check whether a message or data has been altered since the last legitimate update. It is tamper-evident, not tamper-resistant.
Authenticity
Allowing a verifier to determine the originator of a message or data item. It usually implies integrity and freshness.
Authorisation
Allowing a verifier to determine whether an entity may execute an action or access data. It often uses authentication but does not imply it.
Accountability
Allowing a verifier to determine which entity is responsible for an action. It needs auditable, protected logging.
Non-repudiation
The entity that caused an action cannot successfully deny responsibility. It is at core a legal concept needing plausible evidence.
Availability
A system's capability of providing its services when needed, even during attack, error or mishap. DDoS attacks target it.
Privacy
An entity's ability to control what information about themselves is released or hidden. It relates to but differs from anonymity and confidentiality.
Anonymity
The state of being unidentified or unidentifiable in a particular context — one's identity cannot be traced back.
Economy of mechanism
Security designs should be as simple and small as possible, because complex designs are harder to test and verify.
Fail-safe defaults
Base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion: the default is no access, and conditions for access are explicit.
Complete mediation
Every access must be checked against the access control mechanism, not relied upon from a cache. Zero Trust Networks exemplify this.
Open design
A security mechanism's design should be open rather than secret, so experts can review it. Keys stay secret; algorithms do not.
Separation of privilege
Requiring multiple privilege attributes to access a restricted resource — for example a password plus a smart card.
Least privilege
Every process and user should operate with the least set of privileges necessary for the task, held only when needed.
Layering (defence in depth)
Using multiple, overlapping protection approaches across people, technology and operations, so one failure does not leave the system unprotected.
Passive attack
An attack that learns from or monitors the system without affecting its resources — eavesdropping. Examples: release of message contents and traffic analysis.
Active attack
An attack that alters system resources or operations by modifying the data stream or creating a false one. Categories: replay, masquerade, modification, denial of service.
Attack vector
A specific method or pathway through which an attacker gains unauthorised access — for example phishing, malware or person-in-the-middle.
Attack surface
The reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities in a system — the sum of all possible entry and extraction points. Categories: network, software and human.
Attack tree
A branching, hierarchical structure representing potential techniques for exploiting vulnerabilities. The root is the attacker's goal; leaf nodes are the attack events.

Practice Questions

Q1

You are designing a small network with a storage server using safety/reliability engineering practices. Which one of the following is not part of safety/reliability engineering: (a) preventing someone from erasing data, (b) RAID-1 for disk failures, (c) surge protection for electrical safety, (d) dimensioning the server for end-of-year load?

Answer: (a). The key difference is that security engineering defends against an adversary, while safety and reliability engineering defend against known, predictable factors such as hardware failure. Options (b), (c) and (d) all deal with hardware failures or predictable load; only (a) plans measures against an adversary.
Q2

Which statement about traditional security goals is FALSE: (a) privacy is my ability to control the visibility of my personal information, (b) authentication verifies an endpoint's identity, (c) accountability is the ability to trace an action back to an actor, (d) authorisation ensures an endpoint proves its identity before communicating?

Answer: (d). Authorisation determines whether an entity is allowed to execute an action or access data, and can be done without authentication — for example using authorisation tokens. Statement (d) actually describes authentication.
Q3

A CISO says: "We do not keep customer information for more than three years, and we comply with deletion requests within three working days." Which part of the security framework is this — assurance, policy, mechanisms or incentives?

Answer: Policy. Policy defines, at a high level, what security means to the organisation. Notice the CISO does not stipulate how to achieve it — implementation belongs to mechanisms.
Q4

A user's screen shows that all their files have been encrypted and are inaccessible — a classic ransomware message. Which security goal has been breached: malleability, accountability, availability or non-repudiation?

Answer: Availability. The user can no longer access their files or do their usual work. (Note: malleability is not even a security goal.)
Q5

A shipping company fears a rogue accountant could create a false vendor and then issue payment to it. Which security design principle best prevents this: economy of mechanism, open design, psychological acceptability or separation of privilege?

Answer: Separation of privilege. Under this principle, critical tasks are divided into discrete components so no single individual can perform both actions — here, creating a vendor and authorising payment. Open design, economy of mechanism and psychological acceptability are unrelated to the scenario.
Q6

Which are examples of passive attacks? Select all that apply. (a) recording keyboard sounds to infer keys, (b) a person-in-the-middle stealing bank credentials, (c) using a WiFi jammer to disturb communication, (d) monitoring CPU power consumption to infer cryptographic keys.

Answer: (a) and (d). In both, the attacker does not interfere with what the user or device is doing — they monitor artefacts of the main task and try to infer what is happening. (b) and (c) interfere with the message flow and are therefore active.
Q7

Fill in the blank: "______ is a path or technique by which someone gains illegal access to a computer system." Choose from attack vector, attack surface, attack point or attack arena.

Answer: Attack vector. It is one means of breaking a system's security goals — for example, password brute force is an attack vector. The sum of all such vectors is the attack surface. "Attack point" and "attack arena" are made-up terms.
Q8

For a web application, which of the following can be considered an attack vector? Select all that apply: (a) compromised credentials, (b) misconfigurations, (c) phishing, (d) zero-day vulnerabilities.

Answer: All of them. Compromised credentials expose usernames and passwords (especially without MFA); misconfigurations include enabled setup pages or default credentials; phishing lures users or admins into revealing data; and a zero-day vulnerability is one nobody is aware of until the breach — if no patch exists before exploitation, it becomes a zero-day attack.
Q9

An attacker steals an authentication token from a user's web session and uses it to impersonate the user. Which term best describes this: masquerading, denial of service, traffic analysis or modification?

Answer: Masquerading. A masquerade occurs when one entity pretends to be a different entity. A masquerade usually includes another active attack — for example, captured authentication sequences can be replayed so a low-privilege entity gains extra privileges. Traffic analysis is passive; modification alters, delays or reorders messages; denial of service floods a service.
Q10

Fill in the blank: "______ is the sum of all possible points in the software or system where unauthorised users can enter and extract data." Choose from attack vector, attack surface, attack point or attack arena.

Answer: Attack surface. It consists of all a system's reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities. One such means is an attack vector (for example, password brute force). "Attack point" and "attack arena" have no particular meaning.
Q11

Explain Kerkhoff's principle and how it relates to open design. Then use the CSS/DVD example to show what happens when the principle is ignored.

Answer: Kerkhoff's principle states that a cryptographic system should be secure even if everything about it, except the key, is public knowledge. This aligns with open design: security should rely on the secrecy of keys, not the obscurity of algorithms, so the design can be publicly scrutinised. The Content Scramble System (CSS) for DVDs kept its algorithm secret, preventing expert review. In November 1999 Frank A. Stevenson published three exploits, and a group reverse-engineered CSS into the DeCSS program — showing that security through obscurity is not reliable.
Q12

"Security is a process, not a product." Justify this claim using at least three factors from the lecture, and explain how the Target breach illustrates it.

Answer: Security is a dynamic, continuous process because all products have flaws; people (users, admins, insiders) affect real security; systems must be linked, adding complexity; the environment is dynamic, so changing business value changes attacker incentives and requires updates; and systems are too complex to be secured by a single product. At Target, a working detection tool (FireEye) was not enough — weak third-party policy, missing incident response and misaligned incentives let the attack run for weeks. Buying a product did not deliver security; ongoing process would have.
