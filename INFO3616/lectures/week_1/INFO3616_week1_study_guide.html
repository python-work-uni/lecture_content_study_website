INFO3616 · Week 1 · Introduction to Security Engineering

Programming Satan's Computer

Security engineering is what engineering becomes when the environment contains a hostile opponent. This week builds the foundation for the whole unit: a working definition of the discipline, a four-pillar framework (Policy, Mechanisms, Assurance, Incentives) for designing and auditing systems, nine security goals, thirteen design principles, and the vocabulary of attacks, adversaries, surfaces and trees. If you have never opened a security textbook, start here and read top to bottom.

Read this first: A secure system produces only the expected answers even when someone is actively trying to make it produce the wrong ones. Everything else this week is a tool for reasoning about that sentence.
~18 min read 16 teaching sections 14 practice questions 30 key terms

By the end you will be able to

  • Define security engineering and explain why it is not safety or reliability engineering.
  • Name and apply the four framework pillars (Policy, Mechanisms, Assurance, Incentives).
  • Reconstruct the 2013 Target breach as a failure of all four pillars.
  • Explain all nine security goals and tell the close ones apart.

  • Classify attacks as passive or active, and tell vectors, surfaces and trees apart.
  • Describe the main adversary categories and argue why security is a process, not a product.

What Is Security Engineering?

Security engineering makes hardware and software behave correctly even when an opponent is actively trying to make them misbehave.

Engineering is the application of science and mathematics to solve problems. Scientists and inventors develop theories and innovations; engineers apply those discoveries to the real world. The physics of thermodynamics becomes an HVAC system; the physics of aerodynamics becomes an aircraft wing. Security engineering does the same with security principles: it protects data, secures communication, and reduces risk in digital and physical environments.

Security engineering — conceiving, designing, and implementing hardware and software that produces only the expected answers, even when confronted with malice, error, or mischance.

That phrase "malice, error, or mischance" carries the idea. Error is an honest mistake, and mischance is bad luck, such as a disk failing at the wrong moment. Malice is the new ingredient: a hostile opponent who wants your system to fail and who can adapt.

Programming Satan's Computer

The field's early definition comes from Ross Anderson and Roger Needham's essay Programming Satan's Computer. Cryptographic protocols are small, often two to five messages, so you might expect them to be easy to get right. They are not: bugs are routinely found in well-known protocols years after publication. The reason is the opponent.

ℹ️
Context: "Our task is to program a computer that gives answers which are subtly and maliciously wrong at the most inconvenient possible moment." The problem is a hostile opponent who can alter messages at will. Anderson and Needham contrast this with programming "Murphy's computer", where things go wrong only by accident.

The Enigma lesson

The Second World War Enigma machine shows the stakes. The Germans believed their cipher was unbreakable; Allied cryptanalysts, including Alan Turing, broke it anyway. The lesson: you must anticipate and defend against an adversary's innovative strategies. Robustness alone is not enough; you have to think like the attacker.

Distributed system — any system in which components on different networked computers communicate and coordinate by passing messages: client-server, peer-to-peer, cloud, and other decentralised models. This unit uses it as the default setting for everything we secure.
⚠️
Common trap: There is no such thing as a perfectly secure system. Security is multi-faceted and needs cross-disciplinary expertise, from cryptography and hardware tamper-resistance to economics, psychology, organisations and law. System engineering skill is necessary but not sufficient, because it handles error and mischance rather than malice. You also need adversarial thinking, like a chess player who has studied many past attacks from opening to outcome.
🤔
Pause & predict: If you built a system that never crashes and never loses data, have you made it secure? Why or why not?

Security, Safety and Reliability Engineering

All three want dependable systems, but only security engineering assumes a hostile opponent.

Keep security engineering separate from two neighbouring disciplines that use similar tools but answer a different question. The difference is the most examinable idea of the week: neither safety nor reliability engineering considers an adversary.

🛡️ Safety engineering

Prevents accidents and keeps systems from harming people, property, or the environment. Identifies hazards, assesses risks, designs safety features, and investigates accidents to prevent recurrence.

⚙️ Reliability engineering

Ensures systems perform their intended functions without failure over a specified period. Enhances dependability and availability and minimises downtime and maintenance cost.

🔐 Security engineering

Keeps systems dependable under malice, error, or mischance. It alone assumes an intelligent, adaptive opponent who can alter messages and choose when to strike.

ScenarioDisciplineWhy
Chemical plant prevents toxic gas release that could harm workers and residents.SafetyPreventing accidental harm to people.
Sensor network monitors manufacturing equipment to predict breakdowns.ReliabilityPreventing failure and downtime.
Bank adds firewalls, encryption and intrusion detection to protect customer data.SecurityDefence against a hostile opponent.
Airline analyses aircraft components to rectify failure modes before malfunction.ReliabilityComponent failure, not an attacker.
Hospital installs backup power and fail-safe mechanisms for critical equipment.ReliabilityContinuous operation without failure.
Factory adds emergency shutdown procedures and barriers around hazardous machinery.SafetyPreventing accidental worker injury.
Government deploys multi-factor authentication to prevent unauthorised database access.SecurityBlocking a malicious actor.

🧠
Remember: Safety protects people from the system. Reliability protects the system from itself. Security protects the system from someone. The test: "Is an opponent trying to make this fail?" If yes, it is security engineering.

A Framework for Security Engineering

Policy, Mechanisms, Assurance and Incentives are four interconnected lenses; every incident traces back to a failure in one of them.

We need a shared reference for two jobs: designing and assessing a distributed system's security, and investigating incidents afterwards. The framework in this unit comes from Ross Anderson's textbook. Four things must come together: Policy, Mechanisms, Assurance, and Incentives. They are interconnected, not a checklist you finish in order.

Policy→ Mechanisms→ Assurance→ Incentives

Policy — what we mean to achieve

Policy defines what it means, for us, to keep the system secure. Higher management usually sets policies to ensure business continuity and comply with standards. Examples: "Customer data must be stored only within our data centres" and "Only authorised employees must be able to access the personal information of clients". Crucially, policies do not define how to implement themselves. They state the goal, not the machinery.

Mechanisms — the machinery

Mechanisms are the machinery that implements policies: encryption for confidentiality, tamper-resistant hardware for physical security, cryptographic hashes for integrity. Most beginners think security engineering is entirely machinery, but that is only one aspect of the framework. You must still know the mechanisms a policy demands.

Assurance — how much you can rely on it

Assurance is how much reliance you can place on each mechanism and how well the mechanisms work together. It means professional estimates: how long would current computing power need to break RSA encryption or brute-force a ten-character password? Such estimates age. A classical computer would need trillions of years to break RSA-2048, yet a quantum computer would need around ten seconds.

Assurance is a moving target
RSA-2048: classical ≈ 10¹² years · quantum ≈ 10 seconds

Incentives — who wants what

Incentives cover two motives at once: the motive of the people guarding the system to do their job, and the motive of attackers to defeat your policy. Different businesses attract different attackers: state actors, cybercriminals and ransomware groups, script kiddies, and hacktivists. The methodical process of understanding possible threats and prioritising defences is threat modelling. Because security costs money, understanding incentives tells you how much security you need and which attacks to prioritise.

Threat modelling — the methodical process of understanding the possible threats to a system and prioritising defence mechanisms in response.

Applying the Framework: Bank Security

A bank shows all four pillars working together, and shows why insiders are the hardest threat to reason about.

Banks run many security-critical systems at once. A wide range of applications and many people work together, creating a huge attack surface and serious potential for insider attacks. Walking the four pillars is a good rehearsal for the Target case study.

Policy

The overall goal could be simply maintaining correct account balances. Sub-goals refine it: safeguard the integrity, confidentiality and availability of ATMs and online banking, and ensure the accountability of bank-to-bank transfers. The list is too long to enumerate, which is itself a lesson: real policies decompose into many narrower goals.

Mechanisms and assurance

Mechanisms include strong bookkeeping procedures and access control in the backend, anomaly detection systems that flag unusual transactions, the four-eyes principle (large transfers need at least two authorisers), and strong cryptographic protocols for ATM authentication. Assurance is confidence that these measures actually protect operations, and it depends heavily on mechanism effectiveness. The biggest perceived threat is internal: employees committing petty theft, against whom even robust bookkeeping and cryptography have vulnerabilities. Anomaly detection is hard because not all unusual transactions are illegitimate; some are exceptional but genuine customer activity, so sensitivity must be tuned carefully.

Incentives

Attackers want the customer's money without risk of detection, often through a series of transfers that obscure the trail until funds are unrecoverable. Guards and administrators may want to leverage their position for personal advantage, a common attack vector, which is why staff need careful screening and vetting.

Case Study: The 2013 Target Breach

Target is the framework's proof: a breach can succeed even when detection works, because policy, assurance and incentives failed around it.

In late 2013, Target, one of the largest US retail chains, suffered a massive breach. Attackers installed malware in Target's POS (Point of Sale) systems and reached the credit and debit card information of about 40 million customers, plus the personal information of up to 70 million people. We investigate with the four pillars.

PillarWhat failed at Target
PolicyNo policy to vet third-party suppliers. Target granted network access to an HVAC contractor, Fazio Mechanical Services, without verifying its security practices, so attackers exploited the contractor's credentials. The industry responded with vendor security standards, audits and continuous monitoring of third-party access.
MechanismsDetection worked: the FireEye malware tool detected the threats, and credit card fraud detection flagged transactions. But there was no incident response mechanism to act on the alerts, so attackers continued for weeks. Detection without response is not protection.
AssuranceFireEye was reliable at detecting malware. Whether other mechanisms such as IDS and IPS worked is unclear, mainly because no action was taken. The pass-the-hash method exposed the limits of Single Sign-On (SSO) without supplementary mechanisms such as the principle of least privilege.
IncentivesTarget was large and lucrative, and the attack landed in the high-volume holiday shopping season. It is unclear whether the security team had the incentive to act, since it missed FireEye's alarms, and management appears to have prioritised holiday operations over notifying customers.
🧠
Remember: The Target story is often misremembered as "the detection failed". It did not. The system around the detection failed. That is why the framework has four pillars, not one.

Security Goals: Confidentiality and Secrecy

Confidentiality is the effect of mechanisms that limit who can read data in plaintext, and this unit treats it as interchangeable with secrecy.

Security goals define security policies. They are not exhaustive and there are contextual overlaps. The week names nine: confidentiality, data/message integrity, authenticity, authorisation, accountability, non-repudiation, deniability, availability, and privacy. We take them a few at a time, starting with the easiest.

Confidentiality — the effect of mechanisms that limit who can access data in plaintext form. A loss is the unauthorised disclosure of information.

Notice the consequence: access control is a form of confidentiality or secrecy. If a mechanism restricts who can open a file or query a table, it is doing confidentiality work.

Secrecy vs confidentiality

The lecture adds two nuances. Secrecy is an engineering term for the effect of mechanisms, such as cryptography or access controls, that limit the number of principals who can access information. Confidentiality adds an obligation: a duty to protect another person's or organisation's secrets if you know them. The other common use of "confidentiality" is protecting the secret content of a message or data, usually by encryption.

✅
Do this: For this unit we do not distinguish secrecy from confidentiality, and we rarely need the obligation aspect, so use the words interchangeably. Be ready to explain the distinction if asked.

Encryption protects confidentiality, but it is only one mechanism. Access control, permissions and physical protection serve the same goal. A policy saying "customer data must be stored only within our data centres" is a confidentiality policy expressed as a geographic constraint.

Security Goals: Integrity and Authenticity

Integrity lets us detect change; authenticity adds proof of origin and freshness, so a message cannot be forged or replayed.

Data/message integrity

Integrity — the effect of a mechanism that lets a verifier check whether a message was modified in transit, or data altered since the last honest write. A loss is unauthorised modification or destruction of information.

In general we cannot prevent modification of data in transit: an attacker with a useful position in the network can simply flip bits. The important effect of integrity is not prevention but detection. Integrity is therefore very close in meaning to tamper-evident. It is not the same as tamper-resistance, which is the capability to withstand modification attempts and is more commonly a hardware property.

⚠️
Common trap: Integrity is not tamper-resistance. Integrity detects; tamper-resistance endures. Exams like to offer "tamper-resistance" as a distractor when the goal described is detection.

Authenticity

Authenticity — the effect of allowing a verifier to determine the originator of a message or data item. It verifies that users are who they claim to be and that each input came from a trusted source.

Implementing authenticity typically implies integrity, because if you cannot tell a message from a forgery you cannot vouch for its origin. In cryptographic protocols it also implies freshness: proof that the other party is active in the process, not someone replaying a recording. Without integrity you cannot trust the content; without freshness even an unaltered authentic message could be reused harmfully.

The composition of authenticity
Authenticity = Integrity + Freshness

Authenticity is not authentication. Authenticity is the goal or effect: a verifier can determine a message's origin. Authentication is the process of identifying a party we are communicating with. Goal versus process. Confuse these and you will also confuse authorisation later.

🧠
Remember: "Authenticity = Integrity + Freshness." Then remember that authenticity is the goal and authentication is the procedure.
🤔

Security Goals: Authorisation, Accountability and Repudiation

These goals answer "is this entity allowed?", "who did this?", and "can they deny it?", and the three are easy to blur together.

Authorisation — the effect that a verifier can determine whether an entity is allowed to execute some action or access some data.

Authorisation often makes use of authentication but does not imply it. Entering a cinema, you show a ticket (authorisation) rather than prove who you are (authentication). The ticket authorises entry without identifying you. Authorisation is closely linked to access control and accountability. Note that in this unit access control is treated as a mechanism, not a security goal.

Accountability — the effect that a verifier can determine which entity, or category of authorised entities, is responsible for a certain action.

Accountability implies you can trace an entity's actions through the system. It implies authorisation and access control, may imply authentication, and is closely related to non-repudiation. Because truly secure systems are not achievable, we must trace a breach to a responsible party. That means keeping records: accountability needs logging; the logs must be auditable, meaning the verifier can access and rely on them; and the logs must be protected.

  1. Authorisation — the system checks Alice's role and determines she may access financial reports.
  2. Access control — based on that authorisation, the system admits her to view and edit financial data.
  3. Accountability — every view, edit or export is logged with her user ID and a timestamp, so an audit can reconstruct exactly what she did.
Non-repudiation — the entity that caused an action cannot successfully deny responsibility. One cannot deny sending or receiving a transaction.

At its core this is a legal concept. In law we do not need cryptographic proof, only plausible evidence to make a case. Accountability supports non-repudiation. Cryptographically, non-repudiation requires authenticity and integrity plus secure timestamping, which is extremely hard.

Deniability — the capability to successfully reject the notion of being responsible for an action. In many ways the opposite of non-repudiation.

Deniability can be more useful than non-repudiation, especially for highly confidential communication, and can be achieved to a degree by cryptographic means. The security understanding of deniability may differ from a court's interpretation.

GoalQuestion it answersKey relationship
AuthorisationIs this entity allowed to do this?Uses authentication but does not imply it; linked to access control.
AccountabilityWhich entity did this?Implies authorisation and access control; may imply authentication.
Non-repudiationCan they deny it?Legal; supported by accountability; needs authenticity, integrity, timestamping.
DeniabilityCan they plausibly reject responsibility?Opposite of non-repudiation; useful for confidential communication.

Security Goals: Availability, Privacy and Anonymity

Availability keeps services reachable even under attack, while privacy is control over your own information, which is not the same as being anonymous.

Availability

Availability — a system's capability of providing its services when needed, even during an attack, error, or mishap. A loss is disruption of access to or use of information or an information system.

Availability includes the correct functioning of all relevant security mechanisms and network channels. Well-known DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) attacks target availability. It is one of the most important properties, and very hard to achieve for two structural reasons: many systems are asymmetric by design (an exploitable property), and attackers have a huge attack surface to choose from.

💡
Why it matters: Sometimes a lower level of security is deliberately exchanged for higher availability. In warfare, the availability of communication channels may be prioritised over confidentiality, because commanders being able to talk matters more than shielding traffic from eavesdroppers. Encryption might be switched off if needed.

Privacy

Privacy — an entity's right to determine what information relating to themselves they want to release or hide.

There is no single straightforward definition, because privacy relates to society, law and technology at once, so other definitions exist. Privacy is related to anonymity but is not the same, and it may imply secrecy or confidentiality without being identical. Technology can support privacy to a degree. The lecture names three aspects: privacy of static data (released datasets), privacy of dynamic data (database queries and the inferences they permit), and privacy when moving through the network.

Privacy vs anonymity

Anonymity is the state of being unidentified or unidentifiable within a particular context: the identity is unknown and cannot be traced back. Privacy is about control over information that is still tied to you.

Privacy — Alice

  • Joins a health forum with her real name and address.
  • Controls which information is visible to others.
  • The forum protects her data and restricts it to authorised personnel.
  • Her information is identifiable but protected.

Anonymity — Bob

  • Joins the same forum as "HealthSeeker123".
  • Gives no real personal information.
  • The forum logs no IP address or identifiable data.
  • His identity is completely unknown.

Fundamental Security Design Principles, Part I

Decades of research have not eliminated all flaws, so we rely on a shared set of principles to guide protection mechanisms.

It has not been possible to design security so systematically that all flaws are excluded and all unauthorised actions prevented. Instead, we have widely agreed design principles that guide protection mechanisms. There are thirteen, taken in three groups. Each is a habit of thought, not a product you can buy.

1 · Economy of mechanism

Security measures in hardware and software should be as simple and small as possible, so they are easier to test and verify. Complexity offers many more opportunities for an adversary to find subtle weaknesses. UNIX was modular with a small codebase, shaping Linux and macOS; Windows Vista had many complex features and a much larger codebase, increasing the attack surface.

2 · Fail-safe defaults

Base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion: the default is lack of access, and the protection scheme identifies conditions that permit it. Errors then become safe denials rather than unintended access. File permissions deny by default. The POODLE attack shows the opposite: it exploits SSL/TLS falling back to the weaker SSL 3.0 when negotiation fails.

3 · Complete mediation

Every access must be checked against the access control mechanism, with no reliance on cached decisions. In continuously operating systems, remembered decisions need careful propagation of authority changes. File systems appear to comply, but once a file is open there is typically no further check. Zero Trust Networks embody this: every request is validated regardless of previous authorisation, and no trust is granted from network location.

4 · Open design

Design should be open rather than secret. Keys must be secret, but algorithms should face public scrutiny so experts can review them and users can have confidence. This is the NIST standardisation philosophy. The DVD Content Scramble System kept its algorithm secret, received no scrutiny, and was broken in 1999 by Frank A. Stevenson's exploits, leading to the DeCSS bypass. Security through obscurity fails.

Kerkhoff's principle — a cryptographic system should be secure even if everything about it except the key is public knowledge. It aligns with open design: rely on the secrecy of the keys, not the obscurity of the algorithms.
🧠
Memory hook: "Simple, Safe, Checked, Open" — economy of mechanism (simple), fail-safe defaults (safe), complete mediation (checked), open design (open).
🤔

Fundamental Security Design Principles, Part II

The next four principles limit what any single failure can achieve, by splitting privilege, minimising privilege, and keeping security usable.

5 · Separation of privilege

Multiple privilege attributes are required for access to a restricted resource. Multifactor authentication is the example: a password and a smart card, so one compromised factor is not enough. It also applies to dividing a program into parts limited to specific privileges. On Berkeley-based UNIX, reaching root needs both the root password and wheel group (GID 0) membership; either alone is insufficient.

6 · Least privilege

Every process and user operates with the least set of privileges necessary. Role-based access control is the example: roles get only the permissions they need, and absent an explicit grant the resource is unreachable. There is a temporal aspect: administrators hold special privileges only when necessary. Apache's "www-data" account can only read web content and write logs, so a compromise stays confined to the web server context.

7 · Least common mechanism

Design should minimise functions shared between users, providing mutual security. This reduces unintended communication paths and reduces the hardware and software all users depend on, making verification easier. Operating systems provide separate user environments. A concrete rule: never reuse a password across different systems or services, because a shared secret turns one breach into many.

8 · Psychological acceptability

Security mechanisms should not interfere unduly with user work while still meeting the needs of those who authorise access. If mechanisms hinder usability, users may simply turn them off. They should be transparent or minimally obstructive and must reflect the user's mental model of protection, otherwise errors follow. Fingerprint or facial recognition combines high security with ease of use.

Fundamental Security Design Principles, Part III

The final five principles contain, compartmentalise and layer defences so no single bypass leaves the system exposed.

9 · Isolation

Public access systems should be isolated from critical resources to prevent disclosure or tampering. Physical isolation means no connection exists between public-facing resources and critical information; logical isolation places layers of security services between them. Individual users' processes and files are isolated too. Apple's Secure Enclave is a hardware coprocessor that handles encryption, decryption and key management independently of the main processor, so keys stay secure even if the OS is compromised, protecting Face ID and Touch ID data.

10 · Encapsulation

A specific form of isolation based on object-oriented functionality: procedures and data objects are encapsulated in a domain of their own, so internal structure is accessible only to the protected subsystem, and procedures can be called only at designated entry points. In a healthcare database, users cannot query sensitive tables directly; access goes through a stored procedure such as GetPatientRecord and a view such as PublicPatientInfo, enforcing access control, business rules, integrity and auditing.

11 · Modularity

Developing security functions as separate, protected modules and using a modular architecture. Rather than reimplementing cryptography everywhere, one common cryptographic module is invoked by many applications, so effort concentrates on securing that module and protecting it from tampering. Mechanisms should also be upgradeable without redesigning the whole system. AWS Key Management Service (KMS) is the example: S3, EBS, RDS and Lambda all rely on it for consistent, centrally protected encryption.

12 · Layering (defence in depth)

Multiple, overlapping protection approaches across people, technology and operations. If one protection is circumvented, the system is not left unprotected. The corporate example chains MFA, role-based access, patching, antivirus, firewalls, encryption and security training: if phishing steals a password, the attacker still must bypass MFA and the other layers.

13 · Least astonishment

A program or user interface should respond in the way least likely to astonish the user. Authorisation should be transparent enough that the user intuitively understands how security goals map onto the mechanism. JavaScript originally treated strings beginning with "0" as octal (base 8) in parseInt, against the base-10 convention, causing confusion and bugs; this was discouraged in ECMAScript 3 and dropped in ECMAScript 5.

🧠 Memory hook

The lecture offers PILAMES: Policy, Incentives, Layering, Assurance, Mechanisms, Economy of mechanism, Separation of privilege. It mixes framework pillars with design principles, so use it only as a prompt and be ready to name all thirteen principles independently.

Security Attacks: Passive and Active

A passive attack watches without touching; an active attack changes resources, alters the data stream, or creates a false one.

Security attack — any action that compromises the security of information owned by an organisation, or any event that compromises the security goals of its information or information systems.

A useful classification, used in ITU-T Recommendation X.800 (Security Architecture for OSI) and RFC 4949, separates attacks into passive and active. The dividing line is whether the attacker touches system resources or only observes them.

Passive attacks

A passive attack tries to learn or use information from the system but does not affect system resources. It is eavesdropping on, or monitoring of, transmissions, and the goal is to obtain transmitted information. Its two forms are release of message contents, where the attacker reads the communication, and traffic analysis, where the attacker observes encrypted traffic and uses statistical properties of the flows to infer what activity is happening, without decrypting it.

Active attacks

An active attack tries to alter system resources or affect their operation, by modifying the data stream or creating a false one. It subdivides into four categories.

Active attackWhat the attacker does
ReplayCaptures a message and retransmits it later to produce an unauthorised effect.
MasqueradeOne entity pretends to be another. It usually includes one of the other forms; capturing an authentication sequence and replaying it lets a low-privilege entity impersonate a higher-privilege one.
Modification of messagesAlters part of a legitimate message, or delays and reorders messages to produce an unauthorised effect.
Denial of servicePrevents normal use of communications facilities by flooding a service with requests. DDoS is the well-known form.
⚠️
Common trap: A person-in-the-middle (PitM) attack is active, not passive, because the attacker accepts messages, modifies them and forwards them, interfering with the flow rather than merely observing it.

The actors in cryptography

Named characters personify roles and make protocols and attacks easier to describe: Alice (A), the sender or originator; Bob (B), the recipient; Eve (E), an eavesdropper who intercepts and may try to decipher; Mallory (M), a malicious attacker who intercepts, alters or disrupts; and Trent (T), a trusted third party such as a certification authority. Other personas such as Carol, Dave, Charlie, Peggy and Victor exist but are not required for this unit.

Memory hook: PAM — Passive, Active, Masquerade. Remember the boundary: passive observes, active changes.

Attack Vectors, Attack Surfaces and Attack Trees

A vector is one way in, the surface is everything exposed, and the tree is the structured map of how the pieces combine.

Attack vectors

Attack vector — a specific method or pathway through which a malicious actor gains unauthorised access to a computer or network system. It is one means of breaking a system's security goals.

The three headline vectors are phishing (fraudulent emails or messages appearing to come from a trusted source, tricking people into revealing sensitive information or clicking malicious links), malware (software designed to harm or exploit a programmable device, service or network, introduced via email attachments, software downloads or OS vulnerabilities), and person-in-the-middle (interception of communication between two parties to steal or manipulate the data being exchanged).

For a web application, other vectors include compromised credentials (usernames and passwords exposed to unauthorised parties, which without MFA grant access), misconfigurations (setup pages left enabled, or default usernames and passwords), and zero-day vulnerabilities. A zero-day is a vulnerability nobody is aware of until the breach happens; the name reflects the absence of time between the attack and the vulnerability becoming public. If no patch existed beforehand, it is a zero-day attack.

Attack surfaces

Attack surface — the reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities in a system, and effectively the sum of all possible points at which unauthorised users can enter and extract data.

Concrete examples include open ports on outward-facing servers and the code listening on them; services inside a firewall; code processing incoming data such as email, XML, office documents and industry-specific formats; interfaces, SQL and web forms; and an employee with access to sensitive information who is vulnerable to social engineering. Surfaces fall into three categories.

SurfaceWhat it covers
Network attack surfaceVulnerabilities over an enterprise network, WAN or the Internet: protocol vulnerabilities such as those used for denial-of-service, disruption of communication links, and intruder attacks.
Software attack surfaceVulnerabilities in application, utility or OS code, with a particular focus on web server software.
Human attack surfaceVulnerabilities created by personnel or outsiders: social engineering, human error, and trusted insiders.

Attack surface analysis assesses the scale and severity of threats. Systematically finding points of vulnerability makes developers and analysts aware of where mechanisms are needed. Once a surface is defined, designers can shrink it, making the adversary's task harder, and the analysis guides priorities for testing and hardening.

Attack trees

Attack tree — a branching, hierarchical data structure representing a set of potential techniques for exploiting security vulnerabilities.

Attack trees exploit the information already available about attack patterns. Analysts document attacks in a structured form that reveals key vulnerabilities, and the tree guides both system design and the choice and strength of countermeasures. In the internet banking example, the root is the attacker's objective, compromising a user's account, and the leaf nodes are the events that comprise attacks. Branches include credential compromise (malicious software, social engineering, smartcard attacks), injection of commands (active man-in-the-middle), credential guessing (brute force), security policy violation, and use of a known authenticated session (session hijacking).

Understanding the nature and motivation of each attacker category is what makes threat modelling and prioritised defence possible.

Under the Incentives pillar, understanding who attacks and why is critical for effective defences. Different adversaries want different things, so the defences worth prioritising differ too.

Security researchersResearchers and academics who investigate vulnerabilities and report them to improve security.Curiosity, knowledge, recognition and career advancement.
HacktivistsIndividuals or groups using hacking for political ends, social change, or other ideological goals, including hate campaigns.Ideology, politics, attention.
Script kiddiesInexperienced people using existing tools and scripts without deep understanding. They can still cause significant harm.Curiosity, status, mischief.
AI (soon)An emerging threat category flagged by the lecture.Amplifies the other categories; still evolving.
⚠️
Common trap: Do not picture only external criminals. Insiders, whistle-blowers and careless employees sit inside the trust boundary, and the Target breach began with a trusted contractor's credentials. The human attack surface is often the softest.

Security Is a Process, Not a Product

You cannot buy security once and own it, because products have flaws, people matter, and the environment keeps changing.

The week closes with a claim that reframes everything above. Security is a dynamic, continuous process, not a static product you can simply purchase.

reason Products have flaws

History teaches us that all products come with flaws, so security cannot be achieved solely by building or buying one.

reason Many facets

Users, admins and insiders all come into play, and systems must be linked together, so it is never enough to "have security".

reason The environment moves

A change in business value brings a change in attacker incentive, which means updates and collaboration never finish.

reason Complexity

The huge complexity of systems supposedly secured by a "security product" underlines the need for constant vigilance and adaptation.

The one-line version
Security = ongoing process of design, assess, adapt

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else from Week 1, remember these twelve points.

1

Security engineering builds hardware and software that produces only the expected answers despite malice, error, or mischance. It is defined by the presence of a hostile opponent.

2

Safety and reliability engineering assume no adversary. Safety prevents harm from accidents; reliability prevents failure over time. Only security defends against a thinking opponent.

3

The four-pillar framework is Policy (what to achieve), Mechanisms (machinery), Assurance (how much to rely on it), and Incentives (who wants what). The pillars are interconnected.

4

The 2013 Target breach failed on all four pillars: unvetted third-party access, no incident response despite working detection, SSO without least privilege, and misaligned incentives.

5

Security goals define security policies. The nine are confidentiality, integrity, authenticity, authorisation, accountability, non-repudiation, deniability, availability, and privacy.

6

Authenticity = Integrity + Freshness. Integrity is tamper-evidence (detection), not tamper-resistance. Authenticity is a goal; authentication is the process of identifying a party.

7

Authorisation asks "are you allowed?", not "who are you?". Accountability traces actions and needs auditable, protected logging. Non-repudiation is legal; deniability is its opposite.

8

Privacy is not anonymity. Privacy is control over information relating to you; anonymity is being unidentified and untraceable. Availability is hard and is sometimes traded against confidentiality.

9

The thirteen design principles include economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability, isolation, encapsulation, modularity, layering, and least astonishment.

10

Open design beats obscurity. Kerkhoff's principle says a system stays secure even if everything but the key is public. Secrets belong in keys, not in algorithm design.

11

Attacks are passive (eavesdropping, traffic analysis) or active (replay, masquerade, modification, denial of service). A vector is one path in; the surface is the sum of paths; a tree organises them by objective.

12

Security is a process, not a product. Products have flaws, people matter, and the environment changes, so security demands ongoing design, assessment and adaptation.

Glossary

Thirty terms worth being able to define in one sentence each. Filter by category to drill a group.

Security engineering
Conceiving, designing, and implementing hardware and software that produces only the expected answers despite malice, error, or mischance.
Policy
The framework pillar defining what we mean to achieve: what it means to keep the system secure. Policies do not define how they are implemented.
Mechanisms
The machinery that implements policy, such as encryption for confidentiality, tamper-resistant hardware, and cryptographic hashes for integrity.
Assurance
How much reliance can be placed on each mechanism and how well the mechanisms work together. Estimates age as computing power changes.
Incentives
The motives of those guarding the system and of attackers who want to defeat the policy. Understanding them drives threat modelling and prioritisation.
Threat modelling
The methodical process of understanding possible threats and prioritising defence mechanisms in response.
Confidentiality
The effect of mechanisms limiting who can access data in plaintext. Loss is unauthorised disclosure. Treated as interchangeable with secrecy here.
Integrity
The effect of a mechanism letting a verifier check whether a message changed in transit or data changed since the last honest write. Close to tamper-evident.
Authenticity
The effect of letting a verifier determine the originator of a message or data item. Implies integrity and, in cryptographic protocols, freshness.
Freshness
Proof that a message is new and not a replay of an earlier one. The second half of authenticity.
Authorisation
The effect that a verifier can determine whether an entity is allowed to execute an action or access data. Uses authentication but does not imply it.
Accountability
The effect that a verifier can determine which entity is responsible for an action by tracing actions through the system. Requires auditable, protected logging.
Non-repudiation
An entity cannot successfully deny responsibility for an action. At core a legal concept, needing plausible evidence rather than cryptographic proof.
Deniability
The capability to successfully reject responsibility for an action. Opposite of non-repudiation and often useful for confidential communication.
Availability
A system's capability of providing its services when needed, even during attack, error, or mishap. Loss is disruption of access. DDoS targets it.
Privacy
An entity's right to determine what information relating to themselves they release or hide. Has static, dynamic and network aspects.
Anonymity
The state of being unidentified or unidentifiable within a context. Distinct from privacy: the identity cannot be traced at all.
Economy of mechanism
Keep security design simple and small so it is easier to test and verify. Complexity creates more opportunities for subtle weaknesses.
Fail-safe defaults
Base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion: the default is no access, and explicit conditions grant it.
Complete mediation
Every access must be checked against the access control mechanism; do not rely on cached decisions. Zero Trust Networks embody it.
Open design
Security mechanism design should be open to public scrutiny, with only the keys secret. Related to Kerkhoff's principle.
Kerkhoff's principle
A cryptographic system should remain secure even if everything about it except the key is public knowledge. Security rests on key secrecy, not obscurity.
Separation of privilege
Require multiple privilege attributes for access to a restricted resource, such as password plus smart card, or root password plus wheel group membership.
Least privilege
Every process and user operates with the least set of privileges necessary for the task, with privileges withdrawn when not needed.
Layering (defence in depth)
Use multiple overlapping protections across people, technology and operations so circumventing one layer does not leave the system unprotected.
Passive attack
An attempt to learn or use information from a system without affecting its resources. Forms include release of message contents and traffic analysis.
Active attack
An attempt to alter system resources or operation by modifying the data stream or creating a false one: replay, masquerade, modification, denial of service.
Attack vector
A specific method or pathway for gaining unauthorised access, such as phishing, malware, or a person-in-the-middle attack.
Attack surface
The reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities in a system, categorised as network, software and human. It is the sum of all attack vectors.
Attack tree
A branching, hierarchical structure representing potential techniques for exploiting vulnerabilities, with the attacker's objective at the root and events at the leaves.

No terms in this category.

Q1

Define security engineering, and explain how it differs from safety engineering and reliability engineering.

Answer: Security engineering is conceiving, designing, and implementing hardware and software that produces only the expected answers even when confronted with malice, error, or mischance. It goes beyond fault tolerance and assumes a hostile opponent who can alter messages at will, the "Programming Satan's Computer" idea. Safety engineering prevents accidents and harm to people, property, or environment; reliability engineering ensures systems perform their intended functions without failure over a period. Neither considers an adversary, and that is the defining difference.
Q2

Name the four components of the security engineering framework and give one example question each answers.

Answer: Policy asks what we mean to achieve, for example "customer data must be stored only in our data centres". Mechanisms asks what machinery implements that policy, for example encryption, tamper-resistant hardware, or cryptographic hashes. Assurance asks how much reliance we can place on each mechanism and how well they work together, for example how long RSA-2048 resists attack. Incentives asks who has a motive to defeat or defend the system, for example which attackers a given business attracts. The four are interconnected, and incentives drive threat modelling.
Q3

Using the four-pillar framework, explain why the 2013 Target breach happened.

Answer: Policy failed because Target had no policy requiring third-party suppliers to have a proper security posture; it granted network access to the HVAC contractor Fazio Mechanical without verifying its practices. Mechanisms partly worked (FireEye detected the malware) but there was no incident response mechanism, so attackers continued for weeks. Assurance failed because SSO without least privilege enabled pass-the-hash, and IDS/IPS effectiveness is unknown since nothing was done. Incentives failed because Target was lucrative during the holiday season, the security team did not act on FireEye alarms, and management prioritised operations over customer notification. About 40 million cards and up to 70 million personal records were exposed.
Q4

What does "authenticity = integrity + freshness" mean, and how does authenticity differ from authentication?

Answer: Authenticity lets a verifier determine the originator of a message or data item. That needs integrity, so the message was not altered in transit, and freshness, so the message is new rather than a replay. Without integrity you cannot trust the content; without freshness even an unaltered authentic message could be reused harmfully. Authenticity is the goal or effect; authentication is the process of identifying the party you are communicating with. Implementing authenticity as a mechanism usually implies integrity and, in cryptographic protocols, freshness.
Q5

Explain confidentiality and secrecy, and why this unit treats them as the same thing.

Answer: Confidentiality is the effect of mechanisms that limit who can access data in plaintext; a loss is unauthorised disclosure. Secrecy is an engineering term for the effect of mechanisms, such as cryptography or access controls, that limit the number of principals who can access information. Confidentiality adds an obligation to protect another party's secrets, but this unit rarely needs the obligation aspect, so the terms are used interchangeably. Access control counts as a form of confidentiality or secrecy, and encryption is the other common mechanism for protecting message or data content.
Q6

Distinguish authorisation from authentication, using the lecture's example, and say what authorisation is closely linked to.

Answer: Authorisation is the effect that a verifier can determine whether an entity is allowed to execute an action or access data. Authentication is the process of identifying a party. Entering a cinema, you show a ticket, which is authorisation, rather than proving who you are, which would be authentication. Authorisation often uses authentication but does not imply it, and it can be granted by a token without identity proof. It is closely linked to access control and accountability, and in this unit access control is a mechanism, not a security goal.
Q7

What is accountability, and what does implementing it require beyond logging?

Answer: Accountability is the effect that a verifier can determine which entity, or category of authorised entities, is responsible for an action, by tracing actions through the system. It implies authorisation and access control, may imply authentication, and is closely related to non-repudiation. It needs some form of logging, but the logs must also be auditable, meaning the verifier can access and rely on them, and the logs must be protected. The Alice example shows this: her role is checked, access control admits her, and every view, edit or export is logged with her user ID and a timestamp.
Q8

Contrast non-repudiation and deniability, and explain why non-repudiation is described as a legal concept.

Answer: Non-repudiation means the entity that caused an action cannot successfully deny responsibility, so it cannot deny sending or receiving a transaction. Deniability is the capability to successfully reject responsibility, in many ways the opposite, and it can be more useful, for example in highly confidential communication. Non-repudiation is legal at its core because in law you do not need cryptographic proof; plausible evidence is enough to make a case. It is supported by accountability and cryptographically requires authenticity, integrity and secure timestamping, which is extremely hard. Deniability can be partially achieved by cryptographic means and may differ from a court's interpretation.
Q9

Explain the difference between privacy and anonymity, using the health forum example.

Answer: Privacy is an entity's right to determine what information relating to themselves they release or hide. Anonymity is the state of being unidentified or unidentifiable within a particular context, so the identity cannot be traced back. Alice joins a health forum with her real name and address but controls what is visible; the forum protects her data and restricts it to authorised personnel, so she has privacy. Bob joins as "HealthSeeker123" with no real information, and the forum logs no IP address or identifiable data, so his identity is completely unknown and he has anonymity. Privacy can also have static, dynamic and network aspects.
Q10

State fail-safe defaults, complete mediation and open design, give an example of each, and relate Kerkhoff's principle to open design.

Answer: Fail-safe defaults base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion, so the default is no access; file permissions that deny by default are the example, while the POODLE attack exploited SSL/TLS downgrading to SSL 3.0 when negotiation failed, the opposite of failing safe. Complete mediation checks every access against the access control mechanism without relying on caches; Zero Trust Networks validate every request regardless of previous authorisation. Open design makes the mechanism open to public scrutiny; DVD's secret CSS algorithm got no scrutiny and was broken, leading to DeCSS, whereas NIST standardisation embodies open design. Kerkhoff's principle says a system should stay secure even if everything except the key is public, so security rests on key secrecy, not obscurity, exactly as open design implies.
Q11

Explain separation of privilege, least privilege, and least common mechanism, with one example of each.

Answer: Separation of privilege requires multiple privilege attributes to access a restricted resource, such as multifactor authentication with a password and a smart card, or on Berkeley UNIX needing both the root password and wheel group (GID 0) membership. Least privilege says every process and user operates with the least privileges necessary, as with role-based access control and Apache's "www-data" account, which can only read web content and write logs, so a compromise stays confined. Least common mechanism minimises functions shared between users to reduce unintended communication paths and ease verification, for example never reusing a password across systems or services. All three reduce what a single failure can reach.
Q12

Distinguish an attack vector, an attack surface, and an attack tree.

Answer: An attack vector is a specific method or pathway for gaining unauthorised access, such as phishing, malware, a person-in-the-middle attack, or password brute force. An attack surface is the sum of the reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities in a system, categorised as network, software and human, and effectively the sum of all possible vectors. An attack tree is a branching, hierarchical structure representing potential techniques for exploiting vulnerabilities, with the attacker's objective at the root and events that comprise attacks at the leaves, as in the internet banking example where the root is compromising a user's account. Vectors are single paths, the surface is the whole set of entry points, and the tree organises them by objective.
Q13

Distinguish passive from active attacks, give an example of each, and classify a person-in-the-middle attack. Name the four active sub-categories.

Answer: A passive attack tries to learn or use information from the system but does not affect its resources; it is eavesdropping or monitoring. Its two forms are release of message contents and traffic analysis, where encrypted traffic is observed statistically without decryption. An active attack tries to alter system resources or operation by modifying the data stream or creating a false one. A person-in-the-middle attack is active, because the attacker acts as a proxy, accepting, modifying and forwarding messages, interfering with the flow. The four active sub-categories are replay, masquerade, modification of messages, and denial of service. Side channels such as recording keyboard sounds or monitoring CPU power to infer keys are passive, because the attacker does not interfere.
Q14

Why is security a process and not a product? Include the categories of opponent in your answer.

Answer: Security is a dynamic, continuous process because history teaches that all products come with flaws, so building or buying one is not enough; because users, admins and insiders matter and systems must be linked together; and because the environment is dynamic, so a change in business value changes attacker incentives and forces updates and collaboration. The huge complexity of systems supposedly secured by a "security product" reinforces that constant vigilance and adaptation are required. The opponents are criminals seeking financial gain, malicious insiders, state actors, whistle-blowers, security researchers, hacktivists and hate campaigns, script kiddies using existing tools, and increasingly AI. Because each has different incentives, defences must be prioritised and continuously revised, which is threat modelling in practice.
