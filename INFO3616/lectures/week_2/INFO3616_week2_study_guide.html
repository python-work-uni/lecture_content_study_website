Security Is a Human Problem: Usable Security, Biases and Passwords

This week asks why strong security so often fails in the real world, and answers: because people, not machines, are the target. We tour the psychology of security — the biases attackers exploit, how deception works in practice, and the long, tangled story of passwords. You finish able to design security that people can actually use.

Read this first: Hold one mental model for the whole week — a security mechanism is only as strong as the behaviour it produces in real people. "Theoretically secure" is not the same as "effectively secure". Every idea this week (biases, nudges, passphrases, passkeys) is a way of closing the gap between the two.

Why Usable Security Matters

Attackers do not defeat machines, they defeat people — so a security system that ignores human behaviour is already broken.

Three slogans set the tone for the whole week:

  • "Only amateurs attack machines; professionals target people."
  • "People are the weakest link in the cybersecurity chain."
  • "80%–90% of breaches are caused by human error."

A concrete case is the 2020 Twitter bitcoin scam. Attackers breached Twitter's systems by using the credentials of an administrator account that had been shared on a Twitter Slack channel. No clever cryptanalysis was needed — a human shortcut did the work.

Why does this happen? Users behave in ways engineers do not expect. They are often not as tech-savvy as the developers, and their attention is on the task their role demands, not on the security of the system. So developers must find solutions that seamlessly help users while keeping the system secure. The field that studies this is called usable security (or the usability of security).

Usable security — the study of how to design security mechanisms that people can and will use correctly, with minimal friction, in real-world conditions. It is a wide field, developing fast, and surprisingly late to be recognised as a fundamental factor in security.

Usable security is tightly connected to usability engineering and human–computer interaction (HCI). In software engineering, usability simply means the degree of ease and intuitiveness with which users can interact with an application — for example good graphical user interface (GUI) design. Many of those same principles translate directly to security. Two things we will lean on constantly are user psychology and mental models (how users picture how a system works).

Theoretical vs effective security

For a long time the security community built theoretically secure solutions that were often not user-friendly. Many technologies promise high security yet turn out to be unacceptable to real users. Security mechanisms must be usable and operationally sustainable, not just technically secure.

Smart cards

  • Store high-entropy cryptographic keys.
  • Hardware-backed protection.
  • Resistant to guessing attacks.
  • Clear "possession" mental model — like a door key.
  • But: what if I forget it at home? What if the reader malfunctions? What if not all devices or operating systems support it? What if I sit on it, or leave it in a hot car?

Passwords

  • Cryptographically weaker.
  • No hardware dependency.
  • Low deployment friction.
  • Fewer ecosystem constraints.
  • Users find them "easy to use"; the conceptual model matches intuition.

Both give the user a clear mental model — unlock a door with a key, or unlock with a secret. But the smart card's real-world pain points are severe enough that the weaker-in-theory password usually wins in practice. That gap between theory and practice is the subject of this unit.

💡
Why this matters: a mechanism that is mathematically strong but that people cannot use will be worked around, shared, or disabled — and a worked-around mechanism offers no security at all. Effectiveness, not elegance, is the goal.

The Principle of Psychological Acceptability

The oldest idea in usable security: a protection mechanism must not make a resource harder to reach than it would be without the mechanism.

Recall the fundamental security design principles from last week: economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, psychological acceptability, isolation, encapsulation, modularity, layering, least astonishment, and least common mechanism. This week we zoom in on psychological acceptability.

The principle was first defined by J. Saltzer and M. Schroeder in their 1975 paper "The protection of information in computer systems" (Proc. IEEE 63:9). Their original definition says:

Psychological acceptability (original) — it is essential that the human interface be designed for ease of use, so that users routinely and automatically apply the protection mechanisms correctly. To the extent that the user's mental image of their protection goals matches the mechanisms they must use, mistakes are minimised. If users must translate their image of their needs into a radically different specification language, they will make errors.

A simpler summary, from Bishop (2003), is:

Principle of psychological acceptability
A security mechanism should not make a resource more difficult to access than if the mechanism were not present.

Example: think about setting file permissions. Programmers find it easy to set file permissions correctly, because they understand the model. A secretary may find it much harder. The same mechanism is acceptable to one user and unacceptable to another.

An alternative definition sharpens the point: security mechanisms cannot be applied in a vacuum — they must take into account the abilities, knowledge, and mental models of the people using them. Unfortunately, developers often design a mechanism based on their own expectations of users, which can be far from reality.

Acceptability of passwords

Psychological acceptability demands a low threshold for users to use the mechanism correctly, and this can conflict with the goal of higher security. Administrators understand the need for strong passwords because they live in a world "under attack". Ordinary users generally do not. Instead they use names, places, or dictionary words — all predictable — sometimes in a different language, forgetting that attackers have dictionaries for many languages.

Passwords are the best-known mechanism (though this may change), and users understand them intuitively. But because the principle requires only minimal overhead, users under-invest in strength, and poor choices fall quickly to:

  • Social engineering — if the password relates to the user's environment, an attacker can just check their social media.
  • Dictionary attack — try dictionary words ("security") and algorithm-based variations ("s3cu4itY").
  • Brute-force — try all character combinations.

Two properties defend against these: unpredictability defeats dictionary attacks and guesses, and length defeats brute-force. But here is the core problem: unpredictability collides with the human capacity to memorise.

Acceptability of patching

A patch is an update to a system (usually software) that enhances functionality or removes a problem — often a security problem. Ideally, maintaining security should be invisible, but that immediately collides with acceptability. Real patching pain points include:

  • Disruption — users must halt work or exit applications, and often restart the system.
  • Complexity — some procedures need technical knowledge.
  • Notification fatigue — too many alerts, including for minor updates.
  • Compatibility issues — a patch can break existing software or configurations.

A good compromise: Apple iPhones may show "An update is available. It will be installed tonight while your device is charging and connected to Wi-Fi." This avoids disrupting the user, and avoids draining the battery or using mobile data.

Acceptability of security configurations

Building a secure system is only the beginning: true security is achieved when it is configured correctly for its operating environment. Developers know the available settings; users often find them intricate and time-consuming. Failing to configure properly creates real vulnerabilities. Many people never configure the privacy features on their social media, and inadvertently share personal information.

⚠️
Real breach — Iraq, 2009: insurgents gained unauthorised access to video footage from US drones. The investigation found the communication channels were not encrypted — the encryption technology was available, but it was never updated or enabled. A configuration failure, not a missing feature, caused the breach.

The standard fix is for developers to establish default settings that already provide the desired level of security, so users get protection without doing the work.

Categories of Human Errors

Human error is not random — it comes in three predictable kinds, and each kind suggests a different defence.

The safety research community spent decades analysing errors in equipment operation. These predictable errors stem from the nature of cognition. Our mental models are excellent at recognising people, sounds and concepts (better than computers), but they also make us vulnerable when a wrong model is triggered. Human errors fall into three broad categories.

1️⃣ Manual skill fails

Actions performed often become a matter of skill, and a failure of skill causes an accident. These are slips and lapses at the level of skill. Example: a fatigued worker slips while operating machinery; a user makes a typing error in a URL.

2️⃣ Following the wrong rule

Actions taken by following rules go wrong when the rule itself is wrong. These are mistakes at the level of rules. Example: "always trust HTTPS because it is secure" — but attackers can also acquire certificates.

3️⃣ Cognitive reasons

People do not understand the problem, pretend they do, or ignore advice to get work done. These are limitations at the level of cognition. Example: an employee finds updates disruptive and postpones them.

Manual skill failure: typosquatting

When a user types a familiar address by habit and slips on one character, they may land on a typosquatted domain — a look-alike registered by an attacker.

A second example of a wrong rule from safety: "Always assume a machine is safe to operate if the safety light is green" — dangerous if the light system is malfunctioning or has not been updated for a new hazard. The security analogue is trusting the padlock (HTTPS) blindly.

And a cognitive example from the workplace: an operator trained on new protocols finds them cumbersome, believes the old methods are sufficient, and ignores the advice. These safety-derived ideas have already been used with early success in security engineering — affordance, which we meet later, is one. To design truly usable security, we next need to understand human decision-making and its biases.

From Rational Choice to Prospect Theory

Economics assumed we maximise utility; psychology showed we fear losses more than we enjoy gains — and attackers frame offers accordingly.

Early theories of decision-making assumed we are rational. Rational Choice Theory says individuals make decisions by selecting the option that maximises their utility (satisfaction). Expected Utility Theory is a more specific model within that framework, dealing with decisions under uncertainty and risk where outcomes are not deterministic.

🛒 Rational choice example

A shopper choosing a cereal brand weighs price, taste and nutrition to maximise satisfaction (utility).

📈 Expected utility example

An investor chooses between investments by weighing potential returns (utilities) against risks (probabilities), seeking the highest expected utility.

But our decision-making is often neither rational nor perfect. According to rational choice theory people should always choose healthy food at the supermarket — yet they do not. So security engineering turns to Prospect Theory and Risk Misperception, and to behavioural economics.

Prospect theory

Kahneman and Tversky challenged expected utility theory by showing that people do not always act rationally under risk. Prospect theory models our risk appetite: we prioritise avoiding losses over making choices based on probability and expected utility. It is the successor to expected utility theory and says people dislike losing and use all sorts of heuristics to decide.

Its two headline concepts:

  • Loss aversion — losses are felt more intensely than equivalent gains. The psychological value (say, sadness) of losing $100 is greater than the happiness of winning $100.
  • Framing effects — decisions are influenced by how choices are presented. Framing an action as avoiding a loss rather than gaining a profit makes people more likely to take it.

Gain framing (weaker)

"Get it now, on sale!"

Loss framing (stronger)

"Don't miss out, sale ends soon!" — and "Your PayPal account has been frozen, and you need to click here to unlock it."

The second example is a classic phishing lure precisely because loss framing triggers a strong reaction. People are also generally bad at calculating probabilities, so they fall back on heuristics.

🧠
Memory hook: prospect theory = losses loom larger than gains. Attackers exploit this by threatening loss (account frozen, prize expiring) rather than offering gain.

Risk Misperception, Anchoring and Availability

We misjudge risk by how we feel, anchor on the first number we see, and treat whatever comes to mind as the most likely.

Risk misperception

If we like an activity, we tend to judge its benefits high and its risks low. If we dislike it, we judge it low-benefit and high-risk. A person afraid of flying perceives flying as riskier than driving, despite statistical evidence that flying is much safer, and may choose a long road trip instead.

Anchoring effect

The anchoring effect means we base a judgment on an initial guess and then adjust if necessary — but the anchor drags the estimate with it. Examples:

  • A car advertised at $30K feels expensive, until the buyer is told it was originally $60K and discounted to $30K. The buyer anchors on the discount and is tempted.
  • Fake antivirus pop-ups emphasise a huge original price and then a reduction ("It is $1000 per year, we are giving it away for $500"), so victims anchor on the high price and click.
  • In lottery scams, a large jackpot anchors the victim, making a small "processing fee" seem negligible — so they pay it to claim a prize that does not exist. The same trick appears in Facebook Marketplace PayID scams.

Availability heuristic

The availability heuristic is the tendency to think that whatever is easiest to recall is the best basis for predicting the future. If you watched a documentary on shark attacks last night, vivid memories may make you hesitant to visit the beach this morning. "Spray and pray" SMS scams at tax time pretend to be from the Australian Taxation Office (ATO), prompting recipients to recall that they recently submitted a tax return and to click without verifying the message.

✅
Quick discriminator: anchoring = the first number you saw still pulls your judgment. Availability = the easiest memory to recall feels the most probable. Risk misperception = your feelings about the activity decide the risk.

Present Bias, Clustering, Confirmation and Zero-Risk

Four more biases explain why we postpone updates, see patterns in noise, defend our beliefs, and chase impossible certainty.

Behavioural economics integrates psychology with economic theory to describe how people actually decide, rather than how they would if perfectly rational. It examines cognitive biases, emotions and heuristics. Here are the four biases it highlights for security.

Present bias and hyperbolic discounting

Present bias is the tendency to give stronger weight to payoffs closer to the present when trading off two future moments. Consequences:

  • People decline or postpone security updates, giving more weight to finishing today's task uninterrupted than to a future vulnerability.
  • People click "Accept" on cookie pop-ups to clear the overlay rather than reviewing privacy settings.
  • People neglect reading privacy policies or adjusting settings, prioritising immediate access over long-term data exposure.

Hyperbolic discounting is the model decision scientists use to quantify present bias: people discount the future so deeply that immediate gratification looks best, even when it is not. It helps explain the privacy paradox — the discrepancy between what people say about privacy and what they do. Despite expressing concern, they ignore security warnings, cookie notifications and privacy policies.

Clustering illusion

The clustering illusion is the tendency to perceive patterns or clusters in random data. Our brains are wired to find patterns — an evolutionary advantage — but that wiring misleads us with genuinely random data. Famous cases:

  • The "hot hands" belief: a basketball player who just scored is "on a streak" and more likely to score again. Data shows no evidence; spectators and coaches insisted the data was wrong.
  • The gambler's fallacy: if a coin has landed heads often, tails is "due". In reality, past independent random events do not influence the future.

Attackers exploit this in phishing: fake URLs, text formatting, logos and graphics that resemble the legitimate organisation, using personal data about the victim, so the email looks like a familiar pattern.

Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek, interpret and remember information in ways that confirm existing beliefs, while ignoring contradictory evidence. Humans find it easier to accept something as true if it fits their preconceptions, and once a belief is established it is hard to dislodge — inconsistencies get ignored. Flat Earth believers focus on horizons that seem flat and dismiss satellite images. In a phishing email posing as PayPal, once the recipient trusts the link, that confidence spreads to the logos, graphics and URL, and minor discrepancies are overlooked.

Zero-risk bias

Zero-risk bias is the preference for options that completely eliminate a specific risk (achieving 0%), driven by a desire for absolute certainty — even when another option would reduce overall risk by more. It is common in government, crypto, and technical communities, and shows up as a preference for absolute, information-theoretic security:

  • Highly secure but imperfect solutions are dismissed.
  • In a classic study, participants ranked cleanup approaches for hazard sites and preferred approaches that cleaned one site perfectly but did poorly elsewhere over those that reduced overall risk to a minimum.
  • Developers insist on extremely complex encryption for all channels, even low-risk internal ones, adding usability problems without proportional security benefit.
🧠
Memory hook: the four B's of behavioural economics — Bias to the present, Believing patterns in noise (clustering), Believing what we already think (confirmation), and Buying zero risk at any cost.

Defaults, Nudges and the Control Paradox

If people take the easiest path, then designing the easiest path well is one of the most powerful security tools available.

Because human decision-making is systematically biased, and because people often choose the easiest option (present bias), defaults matter. A default is a pre-selected system setting applied unless the user actively changes it. People frequently opt for the path of least resistance, assuming the default "should suffice". Security engineers can therefore configure systems to a secure baseline by default:

  • Automatic updates for the system and installed software enabled by default.
  • Encryption for sensitive data and communication channels enabled by default.
  • Privacy settings that restrict data collection and sharing by default.
  • Minimal privileges assigned by default, with extra permissions granted only as needed (least privilege).

Nudges are subtle, non-coercive prompts or design features that guide users towards better decisions while preserving their choice. They encourage desired security behaviours without disrupting the user's primary task:

  • Friendly notifications encouraging users to install the latest updates.
  • Gentle reminders to enable two-factor authentication (2FA).
  • Prompts suggesting users review and adjust privacy settings.

Effective security design combines good defaults + well-designed nudges. But defaults can be misused too. On most social networks, default settings are relatively open, and when many users tighten their privacy the platform may periodically change its settings so users must opt out again — hazardous defaults that exploit inertia.

This connects to the control paradox: providing the illusion of control causes people to share more information. Too many configuration choices can be counter-productive — and excessive nudges can backfire through user conditioning (coming up soon).

💡
Why it matters: a good default protects every user who never opens the settings page — which is most of them. A nudge catches the ones who do. Together they improve security without demanding expertise.

Cialdini's Six Techniques to Influence People

Six everyday social instincts — and six ready-made attack playbooks built on top of them.

Biases in the human psyche can be methodically exploited. Psychology professor Robert Cialdini ("Influence", 2006) describes six primary categories of techniques used to influence decisions. They come from a different origin than the cognitive biases above, but for this unit you can treat them as biases too.

TechniqueHuman tendencyHow it is exploited
1. Reciprocity (reciprocation bias) We feel the need to return favours. A "helpful" IT caller walks a new employee through software, then asks them to install "a new tool IT is rolling out" — even though it is against policy, the employee may install the malware.
2. Commitment and consistency We suffer cognitive dissonance if we feel inconsistent. An attacker advises an employee of security policies and gets an explicit commitment, then asks for the password to "verify compliance" — then persuades them to choose a "better" password the attacker can guess.
3. Social proof / validation We want others' approval and copy what others do. A caller claims to run a survey and names colleagues who "already took part"; the victim participates and reveals sensitive data. In the 2020 Twitter scam, verified accounts of Elon Musk, Bill Gates and Barack Obama were hijacked to lend false legitimacy.
4. Like bias We comply with requests from people we like. An attacker feigns shared interests (a favourite singer) or compliments the victim, builds rapport, then asks them to install software they claim to be developing.
5. Respect to authority We defer to authority figures, often without scrutiny. In a 2006 experiment by R. Cialdini, nurses received a call from a person claiming to be a hospital physician asking them to administer medication. 95% complied (the experiment was stopped before they reached the patient). An attacker can similarly demand a bank transfer.
6. Scarcity We fear missing out; scarce items seem more valuable. A scam SMS advertises a "limited-edition" product, falsely claiming only a few remain, and prompts a click to buy.
R · C · S · L · A · S
"Really Clever Scammers Leverage Authority and Scarcity"
Reciprocity · Commitment and consistency · Social proof · Like bias · Authority · Scarcity.

User Conditioning and User Education

Warnings we see too often stop being warnings, and telling people about security rarely changes what they do.

User conditioning

Decision-making is also affected by automatisms introduced by conditioning. User conditioning is getting users habituated to react to situations in a specific way — a "click-whirr reaction" — through repeated exposure, reinforcement or manipulation of stimuli. The effect is that users do not stop to think when a security decision arises; they react automatically.

  • Warning dialogues: nagging, unnecessary dialogues that must be "cancelled" train a "click-away" response — dismissing the warning without reading it.
  • Delete prompts: repeated "are you sure?" prompts when permanently deleting files produce a habit of confirming without thinking, so crucial files get deleted accidentally.
  • Certificate errors: most are benign and have practically never been an attack, so users habitually dismiss them — and are then conditioned to ignore even a genuinely harmful one. Users are completely right to ignore these in the common case; the design question is what to report, and when, to avoid conditioning.

User education

Attempts to educate users have met with mixed success:

  • Just telling people (for example, distributing a company policy) usually has no effect.
  • Training — incorporating feedback and iteration — works better, which makes sense given what we know about biases and the mind.
  • A complication is that security language is fundamentally different from user language, and users have no intrinsic motivation to learn security: they have jobs to get done.

User education improves security, but not as much as we often assume. Human behaviour is variable and easily swayed by context, pressure and sophisticated attacks, so training should complement, not replace, strong technical and systemic controls. The real goal is to design intuitive systems where the ideal security is so well integrated that users barely notice it.

⚠️
Common trap: "we'll just train the users" is not a security strategy. If a mechanism depends on users behaving perfectly under pressure, it is fragile. Design the system so the secure path is the easy path.

Deception in Practice: Social Engineering and Phishing

Two real-world techniques that target the human link — and the surprising numbers behind how well they work.

🎭 Social engineering

Hacking systems through people — targeting the most vulnerable link in the chain to disclose confidential information, grant unauthorised access, or perform actions that compromise security. It is especially useful for stealing credentials and identities.

🎣 Phishing

Deceptive emails, messages or websites that appear to come from reputable sources, tricking recipients into providing confidential information for fraud, unauthorised access or identity theft. Phone-based social engineering dominated the 20th century; phishing dominates the 21st.

The old ATM scam

A classic 20th-century scam: steal someone's ATM card, then phone them posing as a bank representative. Ask whether their card was stolen; when they say yes, imply the PIN is needed to cancel the card — and the victim hands over the PIN.

The numbers that should worry you

  • IRS audit, 2007: 102 Internal Revenue Service employees at all levels were contacted and asked for their user IDs and to change their passwords to a pre-determined value. 62 complied.
  • University of Sydney, 1996: 336 computer science students were emailed asking for their password to "validate" the password database after a suspected break-in. 138 returned a valid password, 30 returned an invalid one, and 200 changed their passwords — yet very few reported it to the authorities.

Modern bank phishing often reuses genuine bank emails, changing only the URLs, or builds fake login windows such as www.paypals.com/login (note the extra "s") that mimic the real one.

💡
Why these work: social engineering and phishing do not break cryptography — they break trust. Every bias from earlier this week (authority, reciprocity, scarcity, confirmation, clustering) is a tool in the attacker's kit.

The Three Broad Concerns of Passwords

Passwords sit at the crossroads of usability, applied psychology and security — and they fail in three predictable places.

Human memory has real limits: it is hard to remember infrequently-used or frequently-changed items, we cannot forget on demand, recall is harder than recognition, and non-meaningful words are harder still. That makes passwords a poor fit for authentication — yet they persist. Three broad concerns define the problem.

1️⃣ Entry

Will the user enter the password correctly with a high enough probability?

2️⃣ Memory

Will the user remember it, or must they write it down or choose one easy to guess?

3️⃣ Disclosure

Will the user break security by disclosing it — accidentally, on purpose, or through deception?

Concern 1 — password entry

If a password is too long or complex, users struggle to enter it correctly. Since 2010, common rules demanded at least one lowercase letter, one uppercase letter, a number and a special character — annoying and fiddly, especially on mobile phones. Users prefer simple passwords such as three- or four-word passphrases that they can type without many errors. A striking counter-example: the firing codes for US nuclear weapons were only 12 decimal digits, because experiments showed 12 was the maximum that could be conveyed reliably under extreme stress, possibly using improvised or obsolete channels.

Concern 2 — remembering the password

Standard advice can be summed up as: "Choose a password you can't remember, and don't write it down." But is writing passwords down really a bad idea? It depends on context. In an open office or public place — definitely no. In a private space such as your own bedroom, it may be advisable to choose a complex password and keep it securely written down. Users otherwise choose easily guessable values, write them down, or do both.

Concern 3 — disclosing the password

An attacker can extract passwords during entry or from storage, or simply persuade the user to hand them over (social engineering and phishing).

  • Password entry: interface design matters — at EFTPOS terminals, some customers fail to cover their PIN in public, letting an observer capture it. Attackers can also exploit technical defeats of password retry counters, for example a timing attack that infers information from how long the system takes to respond.
  • Password storage: if attackers access a stored password list (typically hashed and salted), they can use brute-force, dictionary attacks or cracking tools to reveal the passwords. Password managers are a partial answer — but can we trust them?
ℹ️
Key insight: all three concerns are usability problems, not just cryptography problems. Entry, memory and disclosure are about people; that is exactly why they belong in this unit.

Outdated Advice and Better Complexity Checks

Two pieces of password folklore — forced expiry and complexity rules — make users weaker, not stronger.

Outdated advice 1: change passwords every 30/60/90/180 days

In general this is bad advice:

  • Stored correctly, strong passwords remain strong — there is no reason to change them.
  • Humans can remember only a handful of strong passwords at best.
  • When forced, they choose ever-weaker passwords, and passwords for different logins become more similar over time as they run out of ideas.

Better advice: do not expire passwords unless you suspect they have been compromised. UK policies recommended this from 2015; Australia followed in 2017.

Outdated advice 2: blind complexity rules

A rule like "your password must be 6–12 characters, with at least one special character and two numbers" does not guarantee strength. Consider:

Accepted by the rule

Password123! — weak, predictable, easily cracked.

Rejected by the rule

geyps5aykj0q71c637n9gf4ycg — long and genuinely strong.

So complexity rules alone are a poor test. Passwords still need to be unpredictable (which implies complexity and length), but we should test unpredictability directly with cheap checks:

  • Check whether the password is from a dictionary (in any language).
  • Check whether it is on the list of passwords tried by attackers (a breach list).
  • Check for common substitutions (4 for A or R, 3 for e, 7 for L) and for number combinations that are really dates.

Rationale: attackers try dictionaries first because it is far more cost-efficient; brute-force is a last resort. Checking the same way closes the gap that matters most.

✅
The tension in one line: passwords must be unpredictable and memorable. Unpredictability wants complexity and length; memorability fights back. The next section is about bridging that gap.

Password Managers and Memorable Password Methods

Three tested ways to bridge the gap between unpredictable and memorable — plus the tool that removes the memory problem entirely.

Password managers

A password manager is a software application or service that securely stores and manages passwords and other sensitive login information. Popular examples include Apple Keychain, Google Chrome Password Manager, Firefox Lockwise, Bitwarden and LastPass. Its main features:

  • A database of passwords and logins protected by a strong master password.
  • Can generate highly complex, long passwords.
  • Often integrated with browsers and other applications.
  • Can copy and paste passwords into an application.
  • Available for some mobile operating systems, and passwords synchronise across devices.

Drawbacks:

  • Losing the master password means losing all passwords.
  • Portability remains an issue — not widespread on mobile devices, though things are improving.
  • A longer workflow (also getting better).
  • Some sites prevent copy and paste — which is the wrong call, because it pushes users towards weaker, typable passwords.

Several studies have examined the risks and usability of password managers, but there is a lack of recent research evaluating modern ones specifically.

Method 1 — passphrases via the dice method

Concept: randomly choose 7–12 words from a dictionary. Words are easier to memorise, especially when linked with a story, and the phrase is easily extended by substituting characters. The dice method (Diceware) uses a standard dictionary and dice for random picks, giving reasonably high security. Drawback: a lot of typing.

  1. Get a word list that maps each unique word to a five-digit number — for example the EFF's Long Wordlist.
  2. Roll five six-sided dice to produce a five-digit number (each digit 1–6).
  3. Match the number to the corresponding word on the list.
  4. Repeat for as many words as you want.
  5. Combine the words into your passphrase.
ℹ️
Worked example: rolling 30 times might give 54452 61644 63545 53161 42142. Looking these up in the EFF long wordlist yields sizable tapioca undoing scalded nemesis. To remember it, build a story: "The sizable bowl of tapioca pudding was undoing my patience as I scalded my tongue, cursing my nemesis." People remember sentences far better than random numbers. The list must contain 65 = 7,776 words — the number of outcomes of rolling a die five times.

Method 2 — letters from a sentence

Choose a memorable sentence and pick one or two (usually first) letters from each word. Example: "Wow! 62 students, all in this memorable class of 2026!" becomes W!62s,aitmc02o26!. Ideally the sentence contains upper- and lower-case words, digits (dates, amounts) and special characters. It extends easily to W!62s,aitmc02o26!.

Method 3 — Person-Action-Object (PAO)

PAO uses visual cues and unusual imagery to jog memory. Choose a random place and person, then imagine a random action on a random object involving them, and apply a rule to turn the scene into a password.

🧠
PAO example: person Darth Vader, place Mt Everest, action riding, object pony → "Darth Vader riding a pony on Mt Everest." One rule gives DVrapoMtE! (first letters), easily extended to DVr4poMt3! (4 for A, 3 for e).
✅
Which method? Use a password manager where you can, use the dice method when you need to recall a master passphrase yourself, and treat the sentence and PAO methods as fallbacks — the lecture flags them "but careful", because a rule you can remember is a rule an attacker may guess.

Measuring Password Strength: Combinatorics and Entropy

Two numbers tell you how hard a password is to crack: how many passwords exist, and how many bits of uncertainty that represents.

Combinatorics: counting possible passwords

Password complexity can be measured by calculating the total number of possible passwords. If every position can hold any character from a set, the count is the size of the character set raised to the power of the password length:

Number of possible passwords
N = C^L

where C is the number of characters available and L is the password length.

ℹ️
Example 1 — simple: L = 6 characters, C = 26 lowercase letters, so N = 266 = 308,915,776 possible passwords.
Example 2 — complex: L = 8, C = uppercase + lowercase + digits + special = 26 + 26 + 10 + 10 = 72, so N = 728 ≈ 720 trillion possible passwords.

Entropy: measuring strength in bits

Entropy measures the uncertainty or randomness in a password, usually in bits. Higher entropy means a more complex, secure password. It is the base-2 logarithm of the number of possible passwords:

Entropy
H = log₂(N) bits

where H is entropy in bits and N is the total number of possible passwords. Applying it to the examples above gives H = log₂(266) ≈ 28.2 bits for the 6-lowercase case, and H = log₂(728) ≈ 49.3 bits for the 8-character, 72-symbol case.

Three worked comparisons

PasswordLengthCharacter setCombinationsEntropy
abcdef626 lowercase266 = 308,915,776≈ 28.2 bits
a1B!9xYz872728 ≈ 7.2 × 1014≈ 49.3 bits
a1B!9xYzA1B!12727212 ≈ 1.9 × 1022≈ 73.9 bits

Notice how length compounds the effect: adding four characters took entropy from about 49 bits to about 74 bits.

Dice-method complexity

Modern advice recommends six dice words. Assume the attacker knows the wordlist and knows how many words were used. With 65 = 7,776 word possibilities, the total six-word possibilities are 7,7766 ≈ 2.2107392 × 1023, and the entropy is:

Dice-method entropy
H = log₂(2.2107392 × 1023) ≈ 77.55 bits

That is strong even though the attacker knows the whole scheme — because the randomness, not the secrecy of the method, supplies the security.

🧠
Two formulas to memorise: N = CL (count the passwords) and H = log₂(N) (measure the uncertainty in bits). Everything else this section is just plugging numbers in.
🤔

Password Practicalities, Alternatives and Passkeys

In theory we use a different strong password everywhere; in practice we compromise — and passkeys may finally end the compromise.

Different passwords for every site?

In theory, yes. It is not transparent how sites protect your password, and a breach on one site can expose both the username (often an email address) and the password. If those credentials are reused elsewhere, attackers gain access to those accounts too. In practice, most of us have dozens of accounts and cannot memorise them all. A password manager is a great choice if you do not need all passwords with you at all times (mobility) and if you understand how it works well enough.

Writing passwords down

Weigh the risk of someone evil getting the password against the risk of you forgetting it and choosing an easy one. Writing down can be OK at times — for websites you only log in to from home, for Wi-Fi passwords, and so on — unless your family is your attacker or you have many unknown visitors. It is not suitable for office situations or admin duties. Hybrid approaches may be best: a password manager where it works for you, plus a handful of strong passwords you memorise for sites that must always be accessible.

Will passwords ever be outdated?

Alternatives exist, each with trade-offs:

AlternativeHow it worksLimitation
SMSMostly a secondary authentication factor.Legacy technology; can be spoofed or intercepted easily.
Physical authentication tokensChip Authentication Program (CAP) readers that log on to online banking with a bank card; tokens used by companies such as Google and Microsoft to authenticate staff.Requires carrying and managing hardware.
BiometricsReliable, easy to use, minimal effort — face or fingerprint.Can be spoofed, can change over time or with health conditions, and if compromised it is gone for good.

Because biometrics alone have limits, the most viable approach is to use them as one factor among several. For example, you usually unlock your phone with Face ID, but periodically the phone prompts for your PIN; laptops likewise ask for a password after a reboot. Passwords will be with us for a few more years and will remain a usability challenge for users and a security challenge for engineers — but passwordless technologies are emerging and are more usable and secure because they build on advances in usable security.

Passkeys

Many web services now integrate passkeys. Instead of entering a password, users authenticate their identity through biometric verification on their own device. The user does almost nothing — but a great deal happens in the background.

Registration process:

  1. User initiates registration on the relying party's (the website's) service.
  2. Challenge issued: the relying party generates a random piece of data — a challenge — and sends it to the user's device.
  3. Biometric verification: the user verifies identity with a fingerprint, face or local PIN.
  4. Key pair generation: the authenticator (the device platform) generates a unique public–private key pair. The private key is stored securely on the device, in a secure enclave or TPM (Trusted Platform Module).
  5. Public key registration: the public key plus the signed challenge is sent to the relying party, which stores the public key for future logins.

Verification (login) process:

  1. User initiates login at the relying party.
  2. New challenge issued to the device; the user verifies identity again with biometrics or a local PIN.
  3. Challenge signing: the authenticator signs the challenge with the stored private key, and the signed response goes back.
  4. Verification: the relying party uses the stored public key to verify the signature. If it checks out, the user is authenticated.
💡
Why passkeys are more usable and more secure: there is no shared secret to phish, no password to memorise or type, and the private key never leaves the device. Apple, Microsoft, Google and many others are promoting them.

UX/UI Design for Security: Mental Models

Design to the user's picture of the world, and security becomes something they do naturally rather than something they endure.

Mental models are the internal representations or frameworks people build to understand and interact with systems. They are based on experience, beliefs and interpretation, and they shape how users perceive and navigate a system. They borrow from related situations in users' own lives. When designers know these models, they can use them to steer users towards secure decisions. Several companies — Apple and Google especially — have become very proficient at exploiting mental models.

Three concepts from UX/UI design

🔘 Affordance

Elements that guide a user towards correct use. Seeing a grey Sign in button, users perceive they must complete the form before it can be pressed; once completed, it turns green and is clearly pressable. Affordances leverage mental models to indicate possible actions.

🚧 Constraints

The user cannot easily make a wrong choice. Examples include password length requirements, restrictions on allowed characters, or offering only a limited set of options. Constraints shape and refine mental models by limiting possible interactions.

🎛️ Conventions

Recognisable elements users expect from prior experience — for example the standard pause symbol on a DVR. Conventions align with mental models through familiar patterns, making interfaces predictable and easier to use.

Takeaway messages

  • If your security feature did not work, do not blame the user.
  • Do not assume users have developers' experience, or that they feel as little stress when making decisions.
  • The design of a new security feature must take psychological factors into account.
  • Use a design process that integrates stakeholders' perspectives.
  • Relying on user education instead of the above is not recommended.
💡
Why it matters: good UX is not decoration — it is a security control. Affordances, constraints and conventions are how you make the secure action the obvious action.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else from Week 2, remember this.

1

People are the weakest link. "Only amateurs attack machines; professionals target people," and 80–90% of breaches come from human error.

2

Usable security asks that a mechanism not make a resource harder to reach than it would be without it. Theoretical strength is not effective security.

3

Psychological acceptability has two definitions: Saltzer and Schroeder's original (design the interface for ease of use) and the simpler summary (do not make access harder).

4

Three categories of human error: manual-skill failures (slips, typosquatting), following the wrong rule, and cognitive reasons.

5

Prospect theory says losses loom larger than gains; framing a message as avoiding loss makes it more persuasive.

6

Heuristics and biases — risk misperception, anchoring, availability, present bias, clustering illusion, confirmation bias, zero-risk bias — all create exploitable vulnerabilities.

7

Defaults and nudges are powerful because people take the easiest path; the control paradox warns that too much choice can backfire.

8

Cialdini's six techniques — reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, like bias, respect to authority, scarcity — are the playbook of social engineers.

9

User conditioning turns warnings into reflex clicks, and user education alone cannot replace good technical and systemic design.

10

Passwords fail in three places: entry, memory and disclosure. Expiry and blind complexity rules are outdated; check dictionaries, breach lists and substitutions instead.

11

Bridge unpredictable and memorable with password managers, dice-method passphrases, letters from a sentence, or PAO — and measure strength with N = CL and H = log₂(N).

12

Passkeys replace passwords with a device-held key pair unlocked by biometrics — and good UX uses affordance, constraints and conventions to make secure choices natural.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Usable security
The study of designing security mechanisms that people can and will use correctly, with minimal friction, in real-world conditions. Also called the usability of security.
Psychological acceptability
The design principle that a security mechanism should not make a resource harder to access than if it were not present, and must account for users' abilities, knowledge and mental models.
Prospect theory
Kahneman and Tversky's model of decision-making under risk. It says people prioritise avoiding losses over maximising expected utility, and are influenced by how choices are framed.
Risk misperception
Judging an activity we like as high-benefit and low-risk, and one we dislike as low-benefit and high-risk, regardless of the evidence.
Anchoring effect
Basing a judgment on an initial value and adjusting from it, so the first number seen drags the final estimate — exploited in discount, antivirus and lottery scams.
Availability heuristic
The tendency to treat whatever is easiest to recall as the best basis for predicting the future, such as a vivid memory of shark attacks or a recent tax return.
Present bias
Giving stronger weight to immediate payoffs than to larger but delayed ones — which is why people postpone updates and dismiss cookie banners.
Clustering illusion
Perceiving patterns or clusters in random data, such as the "hot hands" belief in basketball or the gambler's fallacy. Attackers exploit it with convincing fake logos and URLs.
Confirmation bias
Seeking, interpreting and remembering information that confirms existing beliefs while ignoring contradictory evidence — making it hard to dislodge a false belief once formed.
Zero-risk bias
Preferring an option that eliminates one risk completely over one that reduces overall risk more, driven by a desire for absolute certainty.
Default
A pre-selected system setting applied unless the user actively changes it. Because people take the easiest path, secure defaults protect everyone who never opens the settings.
Nudge
A subtle, non-coercive prompt or design feature that guides users towards better decisions while preserving their freedom to choose.
User conditioning
Habituating users to react to a situation automatically — a "click-whirr reaction" — so that warnings are dismissed without thought. Excess nudges and warnings can cause it.
Manual skill failure (slip)
An error at the level of skill: a routine action goes wrong through distraction or fatigue, such as mistyping a URL.
Following the wrong rule
An error at the level of rules: applying a rule that is itself wrong, such as "always trust HTTPS because it is secure".
Cognitive reasons (error)
Errors at the level of cognition: users do not understand the problem, pretend they do, or ignore advice to get their work done.
Typosquatting
Registering a domain name that closely resembles a popular one (for example glthub.com for github.com) to catch users who mistype the address.
Social engineering
Hacking systems through people — manipulating users into disclosing confidential information, granting access, or compromising security. Especially useful for stealing credentials and identities.
Phishing
Deceptive emails, messages or websites that impersonate reputable sources to trick recipients into giving up confidential information. The dominant human-hacking tactic of the 21st century.
Dictionary attack
Trying dictionary words and algorithm-based variations (such as "s3cu4itY") to guess a password. Attackers try it first because it is cost-efficient.
Brute-force attack
Trying all possible character combinations to find a match. Effective against short passwords or when part of the password is known; a last resort for attackers.
Password manager
Software that securely stores and manages passwords, protected by a strong master password, and can generate long complex passwords. Losing the master password means losing everything.
Dice method (Diceware)
Generating a passphrase by rolling dice to pick random words from a numbered wordlist. With 65 = 7,776 words, six words give about 77.55 bits of entropy.
PAO method
Person-Action-Object: building a vivid mental scene (person, action, object, place) and applying a rule to turn it into a password, such as DVrapoMtE! from Darth Vader riding a pony on Mt Everest.
Entropy (password)
A measure of the uncertainty or randomness in a password, in bits, calculated as H = log₂(N) where N is the number of possible passwords. Higher is stronger.
Passkey
A passwordless authentication method in which a device generates a public–private key pair, unlocked by biometrics. The private key stays on the device and the public key is stored by the website.
Mental model
The internal framework a user builds to understand and interact with a system, based on experience and beliefs. Designers can use mental models to steer users towards secure decisions.
Affordance
A design aspect that guides the user towards correct behaviour — for example a grey "Sign in" button signalling that the form must be completed before it can be pressed.
Constraint (design)
A limitation that guides interaction and makes wrong choices hard — for example password length requirements or a restricted character set.
Convention (design)
An established norm or standard symbol users expect from prior experience, such as the pause symbol on a DVR, making interfaces predictable.

No terms in this category.

Practice Questions

Look away from the page first — retrieval is what builds memory.

Answer, reveal, then tick “Mastered”. Your progress is saved.
Q1

A friend receives a call from an unknown number. The caller says they are from the Australian Taxation Office and has an issue with the friend's tax return, then asks for their legal name, address, tax file number and job information to "verify their identity". Months later the friend is a victim of identity theft. Which bias was the attacker exploiting?

Answer: Respect to authority. The caller claims to be from the ATO and asks official-sounding questions, so the friend is likely to comply out of deference to authority. Confirmation bias can play a secondary role once the target is convinced the call is genuine, but authority is the direct answer. Reciprocation and like bias describe returning favours and complying with people we like, neither of which is the main driver here.
Q2

A password rule allows 5- or 6-character passwords drawn from lowercase letters [a-z], digits [0-9], and the two special characters * and @, with no repetition. How many possible passwords are there?

Answer: None of the above (2,047,923,360). The character set is 26 + 10 + 2 = 38. Without repetition we use permutations: 38P5 = 60,233,040 for five characters and 38P6 = 1,987,690,320 for six characters. The total is 60,233,040 + 1,987,690,320 = 2,047,923,360, which is not one of the listed options.
Q3

Which of the following are true about the usability and psychology principles from this week? (a) In most current environments it is rational for users to ignore security warnings. (b) Affordance means designing systems where users are constrained from making errors. (c) The principle of psychological acceptability says the human interface should be designed for ease of use so users automatically apply protections correctly. (d) The principle of psychological acceptability says a mechanism should not make a resource more difficult to access than if it were not present.

Answer: (a), (c) and (d). The class gave two definitions of psychological acceptability: (c) is the original Saltzer and Schroeder definition, and (d) is the simpler summary. (a) is correct because users often ignore warnings to get work done or because of user conditioning. (b) is wrong: affordance is about guiding the user towards correct behaviour; it is constraints, not affordances, that stop wrong choices.
Q4

What techniques do attackers use to find a target user's login password?

Answer: All of them. (a) Checking social media profiles for birthdays, pets' names and similar details, especially in targeted attacks. (b) Dictionary attacks, trying the most commonly used passwords such as "password", "123456", "123456789", "guest" and "qwerty". (c) Brute-force attacks, trying all character combinations, which work well for short passwords or when part of the password is known. (d) Social engineering, interacting by email, in person or by phone to trick the user into revealing the password or parts of it.
Q5

You generate a password with the Diceware method using the EFF large wordlist and roll 32345 21345 25662 12456 11234 23456. Which password results: (a) gloomily creative everglade attempt acts dispatch, (b) active bonus extent relocate dispense fax, (c) gloomily fantasize idiom polar simmering specimen, or (d) none of these?

Answer: (a) gloomily creative everglade attempt acts dispatch. Diceware produces random yet memorable passwords: you look each five-digit number up in the wordlist to get its word. Humans remember word sequences better than numbers, and a sequence can be memorised by linking it to a story.
Q6

Using the PAO method, you imagine "Harry Potter is eating Sushi at Broadway". Which passwords could you generate: (a) HaPoiseaSuatBr, (b) ry3r1sngh14t4y, (c) HPieSaB, or (d) all of these?

Answer: (d) All of these. In PAO you imagine a person, action, object and place, then apply your own rule. (a) takes the first two letters of each word. (b) takes the last two letters with substitutions (e→3, i→1, a→4). (c) takes the first letter of each word. Any consistent rule works.
Q7

You work in a government institution handling classified information and catch the train to work. A person who often shares your train buys you coffee and you get along well. They subtly ask about your team, your bosses and the office layout. Which biases are being exploited?

Answer: Reciprocation bias and like bias. Reciprocation: they buy you coffee, so you feel obliged to return the favour. Like bias: the text says the two of you like each other, and we respond positively to requests from people we like. Social validation (doing something because colleagues already did) and confirmation bias (interpreting evidence to fit existing beliefs) are not the drivers here.
Q8

A list of domains registered on the web looks very close to the popular domain "google.com". Which category of human error is the attacker exploiting: (a) failure of a manual skill, (b) following the wrong rule, (c) cognitive reasons, or (d) all of them?

Answer: (a) Failure of a manual skill. A user typing google.com out of habit may slip on one character and land on a look-alike typosquatted domain. Following the wrong rule would be something like trusting the padlock icon to mean a site is safe, but malicious sites can obtain SSL certificates. Cognitive reasons (not understanding the problem) are possible but less direct than the typing slip.
Q9

Is writing down passwords always a bad idea? True or false — and why?

Answer: False. There are situations where writing a password down poses little risk — for example on your home study desk, provided family members are trustworthy, since most attacks on webmail and social media are remote. Writing down in a public place such as an office desk is not acceptable. It depends on the context: weigh the risk of someone obtaining it against the risk of forgetting it and choosing an easy password.
Q10

It is good practice to change passwords every three months. True or false — and why?

Answer: False. If a password is strong and stored correctly, there is no need to change it when there is no evidence of compromise. Frequent changes burden the user, who then makes predictable changes or reuses similar passwords across logins. Better advice is not to expire passwords unless you suspect they have been compromised — recommended in the UK since 2015 and in Australia since 2017.
Q11

Describe how passkeys work, covering both registration and verification.

Answer: Registration: the user starts on the relying party's site; the relying party sends a random challenge to the device; the user verifies with biometrics or a local PIN; the authenticator generates a public–private key pair and stores the private key securely on the device (secure enclave or TPM); the public key and the signed challenge go back, and the relying party stores the public key. Verification: the user initiates login; a new challenge is sent; the user verifies with biometrics; the authenticator signs the challenge with the private key; the relying party verifies the signature with the stored public key and, if successful, authenticates the user.
Q12

Explain user conditioning using the certificate-error example, and say why user education alone is not enough.

Answer: User conditioning is habituation to react automatically — a "click-whirr" response — built up through repeated exposure. Most certificate warnings are benign and have practically never been an attack, so users habitually dismiss them; that conditioning then makes them ignore even a genuinely harmful error. Users are right to ignore the common case, so designers must decide what to report and when to avoid conditioning. Education alone is insufficient: merely distributing policy rarely works, training with feedback helps but is limited, security language differs from user language, and users have no intrinsic motivation because they have jobs to get done. Training should complement, not replace, strong technical and systemic controls.
