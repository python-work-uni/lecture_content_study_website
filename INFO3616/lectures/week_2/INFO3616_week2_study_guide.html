Security Only Works When People Can Actually Use It

Machines rarely defeat themselves; people make the mistakes. This week reframes security as a human problem: why 80 to 90 percent of breaches begin with a person, how the mind shortcuts under uncertainty, how attackers turn those shortcuts into phishing and weak passwords, and the design response of psychological acceptability, secure defaults, nudges and usable authentication.

Read this first: a security mechanism protects nothing unless real users, under real time pressure, will actually use it correctly. Every topic this week is a variation on that single idea.

Usability of Security: Why People Are the Weakest Link

Security is a human problem before it is a technical one, so the systems we build have to fit the people who use them.

Start with the statistic that frames the whole week: 80 to 90 percent of security breaches are caused by human error. The security world puts it bluntly too: amateurs attack machines, professionals target people. A machine does exactly what it is told; a person is tired, hurried, and focused on a real job rather than on security.

The 2020 Twitter bitcoin scam is the lecture's flagship example. An attacker broke into Twitter's internal systems using administrator credentials sitting in a company Slack channel. No cryptography was defeated; a person's handling of a secret was, and the fallout spread across the platform.

Why do users behave in ways engineers do not expect? Two reasons:

🎯 Different skill levels

Users may not be as tech-savvy as the developers. What looks obvious to an engineer can genuinely confuse everyone else.

⏱️ Different priorities

A user's focus is the task their role demands, not the security of the system. Security is overhead between them and finished work.

Usability of security — the field exploring solutions that seamlessly help users while ensuring the security of the system. Also called usable security.

The field is wide and fast-moving, and surprisingly late to be recognised as fundamental. It connects to usability engineering in human-computer interaction, where usability means how easily and intuitively a person can interact with an application, and many of those principles translate straight to security.

💡
Why it matters: treat users as careless and you design controls they route around; treat them as busy people with limited patience and you can design controls that fit.

Theoretical vs. Effective Security

A mechanism that is mathematically strong but nobody uses is not secure in the real world; it is just unused.

The security community long optimised for theoretical security: designs that were provably strong but often painful for humans. The lecture's comparison is smart cards versus passwords. Both give a clear mental model, unlocking a door with a key or with a secret, but they behave differently once deployed.

Smart cards

  • Store high-entropy cryptographic keys.
  • Hardware-backed protection.
  • Resistant to guessing attacks.
  • Clear "possession" mental model, like a door key.
  • But: what if I forget it, the reader breaks, my device does not support it, or I sit on it?

Passwords

  • Cryptographically weaker.
  • No hardware dependency.
  • Low deployment friction.
  • Fewer ecosystem constraints.
  • Users find them "easy to use"; the model matches intuition.

The lesson is not that passwords are better engineering, but that security mechanisms must be usable and operationally sustainable, not just technically secure. A control users cannot operate correctly fails, however elegant its mathematics.

ℹ️
Context: psychological acceptability was one of the classic design principles from Week 1, alongside fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, least privilege and others. Week 2 drills into that one principle.
⚠️
Common trap: never read "theoretically secure" as "actually secure". A strong mechanism that is abandoned, bypassed or misconfigured has an effective security of near zero.

The Principle of Psychological Acceptability

A security mechanism should not make a resource harder to reach than it would be with no mechanism at all.

The principle was defined by J. Saltzer and M. Schroeder in their 1975 paper "The protection of information in computer systems". The lecture gives two definitions; know both.

Simple definition — a security mechanism should not make a resource more difficult to access than if the mechanism were not present.
Original definition — design the human interface for ease of use, so users routinely and automatically apply the protection mechanisms correctly. To the extent that the user's mental image of their protection goals matches the mechanism, mistakes are minimised. If users must translate their needs into a radically different specification language, they will make errors.

The alternative definition says the same thing from the designer's side: mechanisms cannot be applied in a vacuum. They must account for the abilities, knowledge and mental models of the people using them.

Why developers get this wrong

Developers often design from their own expectations, which can be far from reality. The lecture's example is file permissions: a programmer sets them easily, while a secretary may struggle without a security background. The mechanism is identical; the usability is not.

🧠
Remember: psychological acceptability has an interface half (make protection easy to apply correctly and automatically) and a matching half (align the user's mental model with the mechanism, or expect mistakes).
🤔

Acceptability in Practice: Passwords, Patching and Configurations

The principle becomes concrete in three everyday places, and the fix is usually a better default rather than a better lecture.

1. Acceptability of passwords

Administrators understand the need for strong passwords because they live in a world "under attack". Users generally do not, even though they understand passwords intuitively. So users pick predictable values: names, locations and dictionary words. Poor choices are then easy to crack by:

🕵️ Social engineering

If the password relates to the user's environment, check their social media and guess.

📖 Dictionary attack

Try dictionary words (security) and algorithm-based variations (s3cu4itY).

🔨 Brute-force

Try every character combination until something matches.

A strong password must be unpredictable and long, but unpredictability collides with the human capacity to memorise.

2. Acceptability of patching

A patch is an update to a system, usually software, that improves functionality or fixes a problem, very often a security vulnerability. Security maintenance should ideally be invisible, and patching is anything but. Four complications recur:

  1. Disruptions — patching makes users halt work or exit applications, and often needs a restart to take full effect.
  2. Complexity — some procedures demand technical knowledge less experienced users lack.
  3. Notification fatigue — frequent alerts for minor or non-critical patches overwhelm users.
  4. Compatibility issues — patches can conflict with existing software and cause unexpected errors.

The showcase fix is the iPhone's overnight update. When the device is plugged in at night, a notice appears: an update is available, and it will install tonight while charging on Wi-Fi. It lands without interrupting the user, without draining the battery and without using mobile data. Streamlined background patching is psychological acceptability in action.

3. Acceptability of security configurations

Building a secure system is only the beginning; true security requires it to be configured correctly for its operating environment. Developers know the options; users often lack that expertise and find settings intricate and time-consuming. In 2009, insurgents in Iraq gained unauthorised access to US drone video footage because the available encryption had not been enabled or updated. Many people also leave social media privacy settings untouched, inadvertently sharing personal information.

✅
Do this: ship default settings that provide the desired level of security. A secure default protects the users who never open the settings page, which is most of them.

Three Categories of Human Error

Human error is not random; safety research shows it comes in predictable forms that follow from how cognition works.

To build security that survives human weakness, we must understand how users' mental models differ from developers' models. The safety research community did this first for equipment operation. Our mental models excel at recognising people, sounds and concepts, but that same power makes us vulnerable when an erroneous model is triggered. Mistakes fall into three broad categories.

S · R · K
"Skill slips, Rule slips, Knowledge gaps"
Skill fails · Rule is wrong · Knowledge is missing. Each fails at a different level of cognition.

1. Manual skill fails

Actions performed often become a matter of skill, executed automatically. A slip causes accidents in safety engineering and vulnerabilities in security. Two examples: a distracted or fatigued worker mishandles heavy machinery, and a user typing a URL in a hurry makes a character error. That second one is industrialised by typosquatters, who register lookalike domains cheaply and catch everyone who mistypes.

2. Following the wrong rule

Actions taken by following a rule go wrong when the rule itself is wrong. In industrial safety, "assume the machine is safe if the light is green" is dangerous when the light malfunctions or has not been updated for a new hazard. In security, the flawed rule is "always trust HTTPS because it is secure". Attackers can acquire certificates too, so the padlock alone does not prove trustworthiness.

3. Cognitive reasons

People may simply not understand the problem, pretend they do, or ignore advice to get their work done. Examples: an operator finds new protocols cumbersome and sticks to old methods, or an employee who finds updates disruptive postpones them and increases breach risk.

🧠
Memory hook: skill errors are slips at the level of action, rule errors are mistakes at the level of rules, cognitive errors are limitations at the level of knowledge. These early ideas were later used by attackers to craft strategies and by security engineers to design defences.

Prospect Theory, Risk Misperception and Heuristics

People are not the rational calculators of classical economics, and the gap between the two is where attacks live.

Starting point: rational choice and expected utility

Rational Choice Theory says individuals choose the option that maximises their utility or satisfaction, for example a shopper weighing price, taste and nutrition. Expected Utility Theory is a more specific model for decisions under uncertainty and risk, where outcomes are not deterministic, for example an investor weighing returns against probabilities.

Decision-making is often neither rational nor perfect, so security engineering borrows prospect theory and risk misperception plus behavioural economics.

Prospect theory: losses loom larger

Prospect theory, from Kahneman and Tversky, challenged expected utility theory by showing people do not always act rationally under risk. Two ideas are central:

📉 Loss aversion

Losses are felt more intensely than equivalent gains: the pain of losing $100 exceeds the pleasure of winning $100, so framing an action as avoiding a loss makes people likelier to take it.

🖼️ Framing effects

Decisions are influenced by presentation. "Don't miss out, sale ends soon!" persuades more than "Get it now on sale!"

So a message like "Your PayPal account has been frozen, click here to unlock it" converts a request into an urgent loss to be avoided.

Risk misperception

When we like an activity, we judge its benefits high and risks low; when we dislike it, low-benefit and high-risk. Someone afraid of flying sees flying as riskier than driving despite the statistics.

Anchoring and the availability heuristic

People are generally bad at calculating probabilities and rely on heuristics instead.

Anchoring effect — basing a judgment on an initial value and adjusting from it. A car at $30K feels expensive, but "originally $60K, now $30K" feels like a bargain. Fake antivirus pop-ups quote $1000 per year then "give it away" for $500. Lottery scams anchor on a huge jackpot so a small processing fee looks negligible.
Availability heuristic — whatever is easiest to recall feels like the best basis for prediction. After a shark-attack documentary, someone may avoid the beach. "Spray and pray" SMS scams at tax time pretend to be from the ATO because a recently submitted return is fresh in memory.
⚠️
Common trap: framing is mostly the attacker's tool, since security messages already sound like losses. Defensively, make the legitimate secure action the easy one instead of competing on fear.

Present Bias, Hyperbolic Discounting and the Privacy Paradox

We overvalue now and deeply discount later, which is why security chores keep getting postponed.

Present bias is the tendency to give more weight to immediate rewards than to larger delayed benefits. The delayed cost of a vulnerability is abstract, the immediate cost of a restart is real, and security is almost always the delayed benefit, so it loses.

🔄 Updates postponed

Users ignore updates and patches because the immediate interruption outweighs the distant chance of exploitation.

🍪 Cookie pop-ups

Users click "Accept" to clear the overlay and reach content rather than review privacy settings.

📜 Policies unread

Users skip privacy policies and settings, prioritising feature access over long-term data exposure.

Hyperbolic discounting is the decision-science model that quantifies present bias: we discount the future so steeply that immediate gratification looks best even when it is not.

Privacy paradox — the discrepancy between people's expressed concern about privacy and their actual online behaviour. People say they care, yet ignore security warnings, cookie notices and privacy policies, and share personal information anyway.
💡
Why it matters: the privacy paradox is not hypocrisy to scold. Present bias and hyperbolic discounting explain it as predictable behaviour, so the fix is to shorten the distance between the secure action and its reward.
🤔
Pause & predict: would a scary warning or a one-tap prompt with an immediate visible benefit work better for enabling two-factor authentication? Predict, then read the defaults and nudges section.

Clustering Illusion, Confirmation Bias and Zero-Risk Bias

Our brains find patterns, protect beliefs and chase absolute certainty, and each habit has a security cost.

Clustering illusion

The clustering illusion is the tendency to perceive patterns in random or unrelated data. Our brains are wired to find patterns, an evolutionary advantage that misfires on genuinely random data. Examples: the "hot hands" belief that a basketball player who just scored will score again, and the "gambler's fallacy" that tails is "due" after a run of heads.

Attackers exploit it directly. Phishing emails use fake URLs, formatting, logos and graphics resembling the legitimate ones, so the victim's pattern-matching says "this is my bank" and the small differences are missed.

Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to favour, interpret or recall information that confirms existing beliefs, and to discount what contradicts them. Once a belief is set, inconsistencies are easier to ignore. Flat-Earth believers interpret observations to confirm their view and dismiss satellite evidence.

In a phishing email posing as PayPal, once the recipient trusts the link, confidence spreads to the other elements. The false sense of security is reinforced by the clustering illusion, and small URL, logo and graphic discrepancies slip past unnoticed.

Zero-risk bias

Zero-risk bias is the preference for options that eliminate one specific risk entirely, even when another option reduces overall risk by more. A classic study asked participants to rank cleanup approaches for hazard sites: approaches that cleaned one site perfectly but did poorly elsewhere were preferred over those reducing overall risk to a minimum.

Technical communities show it too, chasing absolute, information-theoretically secure solutions that are nearly impossible to deploy and dismissing "very good but not perfect" options.

🧠
Remember: clustering invents a pattern, confirmation defends a belief, zero-risk prefers a perfect fix for one risk over a bigger cut to total risk. All three let a bad decision feel correct.

Defaults and Nudges: Designing with Bias, Not Against It

Because decisions are systematically biased, the most powerful design levers are the options already chosen and the gentle prompts around them.

Since the mind is reliably biased, security engineers use defaults and nudges to make secure choices the path of least resistance.

Defaults — pre-established settings applied automatically unless a user or administrator makes an explicit change. People often keep the default assuming it suffices, and present bias reinforces this.

⬆️ Automatic updates

System and software updates enabled by default.

🔒 Encryption on

Encryption for sensitive data and channels enabled by default.

🙈 Privacy restrictions

Data collection and sharing restricted by default.

🔑 Least privilege

Minimal privileges by default, extra permissions granted on request.

Defaults can be misused. Most social networks are relatively open by default, and when many users tighten privacy the platform may alter its settings and require them to opt out again, exploiting hazardous defaults and exposing the control paradox.

Control paradox — giving users more control over privacy settings can lead to less privacy-conscious behaviour, because the illusion of control makes people comfortable sharing more.
Nudges — subtle, non-coercive prompts that shape behaviour by presenting options in a way that encourages a desired response while preserving free choice. Security examples include friendly update reminders, 2FA prompts and privacy-setting suggestions.

Effective design combines good defaults plus well-designed nudges. But too many nudges backfire through user conditioning, which the next section explains.

✅
Do this: prefer a secure default over a nag. A default protects everyone, including the majority who never change a setting, without imposing a decision cost.

Attacks on people are not improvised; they systematically exploit six well-studied levers of influence.

User Conditioning and User Education

Repeated warnings train users to click through them, and telling people about security is far weaker than designing for them.

User conditioning

Decision-making is also affected by automatisms from conditioning. User conditioning is getting users habituated, through repeated exposure or reinforcement, to react to certain situations in a fixed way. The lecture calls it the "click-whirr" reaction: faced with a security decision, the user no longer stops to think.

🚨 Warning dialogues

Bombarded with nagging alerts, users develop a conditioned "click-away" response without reading.

🗑️ File deletion

Repeated "are you sure?" prompts make users habitually confirm permanent deletion, risking crucial files.

🔓 Certificate errors

Most are benign and never cause harm, so users learn to dismiss all of them.

The certificate case is sharpest: users are right to ignore most such warnings, and that conditioning then makes them ignore the rare warning that matters. The lecture asks the design question: what should we report to avoid conditioning users?

💡
Why it matters: a warning that fires constantly carries almost no information. Reserve high-impact warnings for situations that genuinely matter, and make the safe option the default elsewhere.

User education

Attempts to educate users have met with mixed success. Merely distributing company policy usually has no effect, while training with feedback and iteration works better, which fits what we know about the mind.

Two limitations always apply. Security language differs from user language, a significant learning cost, and users have no intrinsic motivation because they have jobs to get done. Education improves security but less than we assume, since behaviour varies with context, pressure and attack sophistication. Training should complement, not replace strong technical and systemic controls, and we should design intuitive systems where users barely notice the security.

Deception in Practice: Social Engineering and Phishing

Social engineering and phishing are the biases of this week turned into working attacks on real people.

Social engineering — hacking a system through its people. It targets the most vulnerable link, humans, to make them disclose confidential information, grant unauthorised access, or act against security. It is especially useful for stealing credentials and identities.
Pick a bias→ Craft the pretext→ Make contact→ Request the secret→ Harvest credentials

Classic social engineering cases

  • ATM PIN scam. Steal someone's ATM card, then phone them posing as a bank representative. The caller asks whether the card was stolen; on hearing yes, they claim the PIN is needed to cancel it. The victim hands it over.
  • The 2007 IRS audit. Staff contacted 102 IRS employees at various levels, requested their user IDs and told them to change passwords to a known value. 62 of the 102 complied.
Phishing — deceptive emails, messages or websites appearing to come from reputable sources, tricking recipients into providing confidential information for fraud, unauthorised access or identity theft. Phone-based social engineering dominated the 20th century; online phishing is the predominant 21st-century tactic.
  • 1996, University of Sydney. 336 computer science students were emailed asking for their password to "validate" the database after a suspected break-in. 138 returned a valid password, 30 an invalid one, and 200 changed their passwords. Very few reported it.
  • Bank phishing. Attacks often reuse a genuine bank email with only the URLs changed, or present a fake login window such as www.paypals.com/login.
ℹ️
Context: authority, commitment and availability explain why educated people fall for these attacks. The target is not gullibility; it is normal cognition under pressure.

Passwords: Three Broad Concerns and Outdated Advice

Password management sits exactly where usability, psychology and security collide, which is why the obvious advice is often wrong.

Passwords sit at the intersection of usability, applied psychology and security. Human memory struggles with infrequently used or changed items, cannot forget on demand, prefers recognition over recall, and is worse with non-meaningful words, so passwords may not be optimal. The lecture frames three broad concerns.

⌨️ Concern 1: Entry

Will the user enter the password correctly with high enough probability?

🧠 Concern 2: Memory

Will the user remember it, or write it down or choose a guessable one?

📢 Concern 3: Disclosure

Will the user break security by revealing it, accidentally, on purpose, or through deception?

Concern 1: password entry

Long or complex passwords are hard to enter correctly. Since 2010, common rules have demanded at least one lowercase letter, uppercase letter, number and special character, which is fiddly, especially on a phone. The US nuclear firing codes are only 12 decimal digits, because 12 was judged the maximum conveyable reliably under extreme stress. Users prefer simple passwords to make entry easy and errors rare.

Concern 2: remembering the password

Standard advice is a paradox: "Choose a password you can't remember, and don't write it down." Context decides whether writing down is acceptable. In an open office or public space it is strongly discouraged; in a private space such as your own bedroom, a complex password written down securely may be advisable.

Concern 3: disclosing the password

Attackers extract passwords via social engineering and phishing, or capture them during entry or from storage. At entry, interface design leaks secrets: some EFTPOS customers fail to cover their PIN, and attackers defeat retry counters with timing attacks. At storage, if attackers reach a hashed and salted password list, they apply brute-force, dictionary attacks and cracking tools. Password managers store secrets too, with their own limitations.

Outdated advice 1: forced expiry

"Change your password every 30/60/90/180 days" is generally bad advice. A correctly stored strong password remains strong, and humans remember only a handful of strong passwords, so when forced they choose ever-weaker ones that grow more similar over time. Better: do not expire passwords unless you suspect compromise. The UK recommended this from 2015; Australia followed in 2017.

Outdated advice 2: blind complexity rules

A rule like "6 to 12 characters, at least one special character and two numbers" does not guarantee strength. It accepts a weak password such as Password123! while rejecting a strong one such as geyps5aykj0q71c637n9gf4ycg.

✅
Do this: use better, cheaper checks. Reject passwords found in a dictionary (any language), passwords on attacker-tried lists, common substitutions (4 for A or R, 3 for e, 7 for L), and number combinations that are really dates.
💡
Why it matters: attackers try dictionaries first because it is far more cost-efficient and treat brute-force as a last resort. Blocking the cheap attacks is what raises the bar.

Measuring Password Strength: Combinatorics and Entropy

Password strength is a counting problem: how many candidates would an attacker try, and how many bits of uncertainty does that represent?

Combinatorics: the size of the search space

Measure the total number of possible passwords by raising the size of the character set to the power of the length.

Search space
N = CL

Here N is the number of possible passwords, C is the character-set size, and L is the length.

Entropy measures the uncertainty in a password, in bits. Higher entropy means a more complex and secure password. It is the base-2 logarithm of the number of possibilities.

Length and a bigger character set pull ahead quickly, which is why length is such a strong lever against brute-force: each extra character multiplies the search space.

Entropy of the dice method

The dice method draws words from a list built for five dice rolls, so each word has 65 = 7,776 possibilities. A six-word passphrase therefore has:

Its entropy
log2(2.2107392 × 1023) ≈ 77.55 bits

A six-word diceware passphrase clears 77 bits while remaining ordinary words you can picture: strong against brute-force and dictionary attacks, yet friendly to human memory.

🧠
Remember: combinatorics counts the candidate set (CL); entropy converts that count into bits (log2N). Managers generate high-entropy strings; passphrases reach high entropy with memorable words.

Creating Secure and Memorable Passwords

A strong password must be complex, long and memorable, and four tested methods try to deliver all three at once.

Contemporary recommendations favour passphrases of three or more randomly chosen dictionary words. Empirically, users pick multi-word passphrases with lower entropy than truly random choices, usually common noun pairs, with diminishing returns beyond three or four words. Here are the lecture's methods.

1. Passphrases via the dice method

Randomly choose 7 to 12 words from a dictionary. Words are easier to memorise than random characters, especially linked to a story, and the phrase can be extended by substituting characters. The process:

  1. Get a word list — a list where each unique five-dice combination maps to one word, for example the EFF's Long Wordlist.
  2. Roll the dice — roll five standard six-sided dice and record the five-digit number.
  3. Match the number to a word — look up the number to get one word.
  4. Repeat — repeat steps 2 and 3 for as many words as you want.
  5. Combine the words — join them to form the passphrase.

Rolling 30 times produced:

Dice rolls
54452 61644 63545 53161 42142

Looking these up in the EFF Long Wordlist gives sizable tapioca undoing scalded nemesis. You can remember the words, or build a mnemonic: "The sizable bowl of tapioca pudding was undoing my patience as I scalded my tongue, cursing my nemesis." People remember sentences far better than number sequences. The list must contain 65 = 7,776 words, one per five-dice number.

⚠️
Common trap: the dice method's clear drawback is the amount of typing. Strong and memorable, but not always convenient, which is why password managers exist.

2. Letters from a sentence

Choose a memorable sentence and take one or two letters from each word. "Wow! 62 students, all in this memorable class of 2024!" becomes W!62s,aitmco2025!. Ideally the result mixes upper and lower case, digits from dates or amounts, and special characters, and it extends easily to W!62s,aitmc02o25!. The lecture cautions: use this carefully, because a sentence you love may be guessable from your public life.

3. Person-Action-Object (PAO)

PAO uses visual cues and unusual imagery to stimulate recall. Choose a random place and person, imagine a random action and object involving them, then set a rule to turn the scene into a password. "Darth Vader riding a pony on Mt Everest" gives DVrapoMtE!, easily extended with substitutions to DVr4poMt3!. Again: use it carefully.

D · S · P
"Dice, Sentence, Picture"
Dice method for random memorable words · Sentence initials for structured strings · Person-Action-Object for vivid imagery.

Password Managers, Practicalities and the Future of Authentication

Real life has dozens of accounts, so the honest answer is a hybrid: tools where they fit, a few strong passwords where they must, and passkeys on the horizon.

Password managers

A password manager is software or a service that securely stores and manages passwords and login information. Examples include Apple Keychain, Google Chrome's manager, Firefox Lockwise, Bitwarden and LastPass.

What they do well

  • A strong master password protects the encrypted database.
  • They generate highly complex, long passwords.
  • They integrate with browsers and applications.
  • They let you copy and paste credentials.
  • Some run on mobile operating systems.
  • Passwords sync across devices.

Drawbacks

  • Losing the master password loses all passwords.
  • Portability remains an issue on mobile.
  • The workflow is longer.
  • Some sites block copy and paste, which the lecture calls a bad idea.

Practicalities: unique passwords and writing them down

In theory you should use a different password for every site, because a breach can expose the username and password reused elsewhere. In practice most of us have dozens of accounts and cannot memorise them all. Password managers fit when you do not need every password with you at all times, and when you understand how they work.

What about writing passwords down? Weigh the risk of someone malicious getting it against the risk of forgetting it and choosing an easy one. Writing down can be acceptable at home, for websites you only access there or for Wi-Fi passwords, unless your family is the attacker or you have many unknown visitors. It is normally not suitable for office situations or admin duties. A hybrid approach may be best: managers where they work for you, plus a handful of strong passwords for sites that must always be accessible.

Because biometrics change and cannot be reset, the most viable approach is to use them as one factor among several. Most of the time you unlock a phone with Face ID, but periodically the phone makes you enter the PIN; laptops do the same after a reboot.

How passkeys work

With a passkey you verify identity through biometric verification on your device rather than a password. Registration and login are a public-key handshake:

Challenge issued→ Biometric check→ Key pair generated→ Public key stored→ Signed challenge verified
  1. Registration starts — you begin on the relying party's site, giving an email and authenticating locally with biometrics.
  2. Challenge issued — the relying party generates a random challenge and sends it to your device.
  3. Biometric verification — you confirm identity with a fingerprint, face or local PIN.
  4. Key pair generated — the authenticator creates a public and private key pair; the private key stays on your device, in a secure enclave or TPM.
  5. Public key registered — the public key and signed challenge response go to the relying party, which stores the public key.
  6. Login — a new challenge is issued, you approve with biometrics, the private key signs it, and the server verifies with the stored public key.

Passwords will remain for a few more years, but passwordless technologies are emerging, and the services adopting them inherit the advances in usable security, so they tend to be more usable while remaining secure.

🧠
Remember: the passkey trade is that the secret never leaves your device. There is no password to phish, reuse or write down: usable security applied to authentication itself.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else from Week 2, remember these twelve points.

1

People are the weakest link. 80 to 90 percent of breaches are caused by human error, so usable security is a core engineering field, not an afterthought.

2

Theoretical strength is not effective security. Smart cards are stronger on paper, but passwords won because they impose no hardware dependency and match user intuition.

3

Psychological acceptability (Saltzer and Schroeder) says a mechanism must not make a resource harder to reach than if it did not exist, and must fit users' abilities, knowledge and mental models.

4

Secure defaults are the highest-leverage fix. Users cannot be expected to configure intricate settings, so auto-updates, default encryption, privacy restrictions and least privilege do the work for them.

5

Human error is predictable. It is a skill failure, a wrong rule or a cognitive gap, each best countered with a different design response.

6

Losses loom larger than gains. Prospect theory, loss aversion and framing explain why "your account has been frozen" beats a neutral request every time.

7

Present bias explains the privacy paradox. Immediate convenience beats delayed risk, so secure actions must be made immediate and effortless.

8

Defaults and nudges design with bias. Nudges preserve choice, but overuse causes conditioning and makes real warnings invisible.

9

Cialdini's six levers are the social engineer's toolkit: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, like bias, respect to authority and scarcity.

10

Deception targets normal cognition. The IRS and USYD cases show that authority, commitment and availability defeat even trained people under pressure.

11

Forced expiry and blind complexity rules are outdated. Do not expire passwords without evidence of compromise, and check dictionaries, attacker lists, substitutions and dates instead.

12

Strength is countable and memorability is solvable. Use CL and log2N, then bridge the gap with managers, dice passphrases, sentence initials, PAO and passkeys.

✅
Final rule: when a security feature fails, do not blame the user. Examine the design. Assume nothing about your users' technical experience or stress tolerance that you would not assume about yourself on a bad day. Always reconcile with the official lecture material.

Q1

What does "people are the weakest link" mean, and what does usable security do about it?

Answer: Human error causes 80 to 90 percent of breaches; users are less tech-savvy and focused on their own tasks. Usable security designs solutions that help users while keeping the system secure, drawing on HCI usability engineering. Twitter 2020, where admin credentials leaked via Slack, is the example; redesign systems, do not blame users.
Q2

Why did passwords beat smart cards, even though smart cards are stronger in theory?

Answer: Effective security needs usability and sustainability, not just theoretical strength. Smart cards store high-entropy keys but add hardware dependency and pain points (forgetting them, broken readers, unsupported devices, damage). Passwords have no hardware dependency, low friction and a model matching intuition, so users actually use them.
Q3

State the principle of psychological acceptability in its two definitions, with the lecture's example.

Answer: Simple: a mechanism must not make a resource harder to access than if it were absent. Original (Saltzer and Schroeder): design the interface for ease of use so users apply protection correctly and automatically, matching their mental image to the mechanism. Example: programmers set file permissions easily; a secretary may struggle.
Q4

Explain the acceptability problem with patching and the design fix the lecture highlights.

Answer: Security should be invisible, but patching brings disruptions (halting work, restarts), complexity, notification fatigue from too many minor alerts, and compatibility issues causing errors. The fix is streamlined background patching, like the iPhone: an overnight notice installs the update while charging on Wi-Fi without interrupting the user.
Q5

Name and describe the three categories of human error, with one security example each.

Answer: (1) Manual skill fails: automatic actions slip, like mistyping a URL, exploited by typosquatters such as gougle.com. (2) Following the wrong rule: a flawed rule causes error, like trusting HTTPS when attackers can also get certificates. (3) Cognitive reasons: users do not understand, pretend to, or ignore advice, like dismissing disruptive updates.
Q6

What is loss aversion, and how does framing make a phishing message more persuasive?

Answer: Loss aversion, from Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, is that losses are felt more intensely than equivalent gains, so people prioritise avoiding a loss over rational expected value. Framing effects mean presentation changes the decision, so "your account has been frozen, click here" converts a request into an urgent loss to avoid.
Q7

Define risk misperception, the anchoring effect and the availability heuristic, with examples.

Answer: Risk misperception: liked activities are judged high-benefit, low-risk; disliked ones low-benefit, high-risk, so a fearful flyer sees flying as riskier than driving. Anchoring: judging from an initial value, so a car "$60K discounted to $30K" feels cheap. Availability: what is easiest to recall feels predictive, like avoiding the beach after a shark documentary.
Q8

Explain present bias, hyperbolic discounting and the privacy paradox, and how they relate.

Answer: Present bias is giving more weight to immediate rewards over larger delayed benefits, so users postpone updates and accept cookie pop-ups. Hyperbolic discounting quantifies it: we discount the future so steeply that immediate gratification seems best even when it is not. Together they explain the privacy paradox, the gap between expressed concern and actual sharing.
Q9

How do the clustering illusion and confirmation bias work together in a phishing email?

Answer: Clustering illusion is seeing patterns in random data; scammers use fake URLs, formatting, logos and graphics resembling the real brand, so pattern-matching accepts the message. Confirmation bias then favours information confirming existing beliefs: once the victim trusts the link, confidence extends to the other elements and small discrepancies are overlooked.
Q10

Distinguish defaults from nudges, give an example of each, and explain the control paradox and conditioning risk.

Answer: Defaults are pre-selected settings applied automatically unless changed (automatic updates, default encryption). Nudges are subtle, non-coercive prompts encouraging a choice while preserving freedom (update reminders, 2FA prompts). The control paradox is that more privacy controls can lead to less privacy-conscious behaviour. Excessive nudges cause conditioning, where users dismiss warnings without thinking.
Q11

A caller claims to be from the ATO and later the victim suffers identity theft. Which Cialdini techniques are at work?

Answer: The strongest is respect to authority: people defer to authority without critical evaluation, as in the 2006 hospital experiment where 95 percent of nurses complied. Reciprocity and like bias can appear if the attacker builds rapport. Commitment and consistency follow once the victim agrees to verify details. Social proof can appear when colleagues are cited.
Q12

Why are forced expiry and blind complexity rules bad advice, and what should replace them?

Answer: Forced expiry is bad because a correctly stored strong password stays strong, and when forced to change, people pick ever-weaker passwords. Better: do not expire unless you suspect compromise (UK 2015, Australia 2017). Blind complexity rules accept Password123! and reject geyps5aykj0q71c637n9gf4ycg. Replace them with dictionary, attacker-list, substitution and date checks, since attackers try dictionaries first.
Q13

How many passwords exist for 8 characters over 72 symbols, what is the entropy, and how does a six-word diceware passphrase compare?

Answer: Search space is CL, so 728 ≈ 720 trillion, and entropy log2(N) is ≈ 49.4 bits. Six diceware words draw from 65 = 7,776 possibilities, giving 7,7766 ≈ 2.2107392 × 1023 and ≈ 77.55 bits. Far stronger in entropy while remaining ordinary words.
