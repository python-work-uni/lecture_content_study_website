Getting the Keys to the Kingdom: Authentication and Key Distribution

Every cipher you have met so far quietly assumed you already had the right key. This week answers the question we kept postponing: how do two parties who have never spoken before end up holding the same secret key, or the correct public key for each other? We meet trusted third parties (KDCs and CAs), certificates and the X.509 standard, the machinery of authentication (passwords, biometrics, one-time passwords), and the protocol that ties it all together — Kerberos.

Read this first: Hold one mental model for the whole week — you cannot conjure trust out of nothing; you must inherit it from someone both sides already trust. That is Boyd's theorem in a sentence. Symmetric keys are introduced by a Key Distribution Centre, public keys are vouched for by a Certificate Authority, and remote logins are brokered by an Authentication Server. Almost every mechanism this week is a different way of routing trust through that one trusted third party, then using fresh, short-lived keys so that a single future compromise cannot unlock the past.

The Key Distribution Problem

Cryptography gives us confidentiality and integrity, but it never tells us where the keys come from — and that gap is the whole lecture.

For six weeks we have been building a toolkit. Symmetric cryptography (for example AES) keeps data confidential once two parties share a secret key. Asymmetric cryptography (for example RSA) lets us encrypt to a public key or sign with a private key. But all of them rest on an assumption we never justified: that the right key is already in the right hands.

  • With symmetric encryption, we assumed both parties already share a key.
  • With public-key encryption, we assumed the sender reliably knows the receiver's public key.

In practice, neither assumption is free. The key distribution problem is the challenge of securely establishing shared secret keys (or authentic public keys) between parties who wish to communicate securely, without relying on a pre-existing secure channel to do so.

Bootstrapping problem — the circular difficulty that every protection mechanism needs a key before it can protect anything, including the distribution of the next key.

Look at how the circle closes on itself. No matter which primitive we pick, we still need something we do not yet have:

PrimitiveWhat it needs first
Diffie-Hellman key exchangeTo know the other party's public key in a reliable way (to stop an active attacker).
Hybrid cryptographyTo know the other party's public key in a reliable way.
MACs / AESA pre-shared symmetric key.

So the honest summary is: the bootstrapping problem remains. This lecture is about the two broad families of answers.

Two families of answers

Without a third party

  • Secure physical shipment of keys.
  • Only done for very high-value communication — for example keys carried to embassies, or device pairing done face-to-face with a QR code.
  • Expensive and does not scale to internet size.

With a third party

  • Symmetric case: a Key Distribution Centre (KDC).
  • Asymmetric case: a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI).
  • Scales, but introduces a party you must trust.
💡
Why this matters: a perfect cipher with a badly distributed key is worthless. Historically, breaking the key management has been far easier than breaking the mathematics, so this is where real systems live or die.
🤔
Pause & predict: you want to message a stranger securely tomorrow. Could you solve it by simply mailing them an AES key today? What assumption about the postal service does that quietly rely on, and what would it cost at a million users?

Attacker Model: Dolev-Yao

Before designing any protocol, decide who you are fighting — and for this lecture, the enemy owns the network.

In earlier weeks we defined several attackers by what they could see: one who only has ciphertext, one who can obtain some plaintext, and so on. Formally defining an attacker's capability is called threat modelling. From now on we upgrade to a much stronger and more realistic adversary: the Dolev-Yao model.

The slogan to remember is: "The attacker carries the message." In the Dolev-Yao model the attacker has full control over the network and can:

  • Eavesdrop on any message in transit.
  • Delay, delete, modify, replay and inject messages.
  • Impersonate any legitimate party.

That sounds hopeless, but there is one crucial limit. The Dolev-Yao attacker is fundamentally constrained by cryptography:

  • It cannot modify an integrity-protected message without the receiver noticing.
  • It cannot decrypt a message encrypted with a key it does not hold.

Intuitively, this is exactly the attacker we picture for secure electronic communication: a hostile router, a malicious Wi-Fi hotspot, or an internet service provider that reads and rewrites traffic. The design goal of every protocol this week is to achieve security despite such an attacker.

ℹ️
Contrast with earlier models: the ciphertext-only or known-plaintext attackers only passively observe data. Dolev-Yao is active — it changes, removes and fabricates messages at will. A protocol that is safe against a passive eavesdropper can still collapse against Dolev-Yao, as we are about to see.
🤔
Pause & predict: if the attacker controls the medium but cannot break cryptography, what is the only kind of weakness it can still exploit? (Hint: think about the protocol, not the mathematics.)

Boyd's Theorem and the Limits of Diffie-Hellman

You cannot build trust from nothing: without a secure channel, a trusted mediator is not a convenience — it is a necessity.

Boyd's theorem governs the whole field of key distribution. It says:

Boyd's theorem
Assuming the absence of a secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without the existence of an entity that can mediate between the two and which both parties trust and have a secure channel with.

Rephrased in Alice-and-Bob language: Alice and Bob cannot securely establish keys between them if they do not already have existing, established keys. The only way is an 'introduction' via a third party, with which both Alice and Bob already have established keys.

🧠
Memory hook: think of a school playground. Two children who do not know each other can still be introduced by a teacher both of them trust. Without the teacher, a stranger can claim to be anyone. Boyd's theorem says: find the teacher.

Why Diffie-Hellman is not enough

Diffie-Hellman key exchange is secure if the attacker is passive. It lets two parties compute the same secret over a public channel without ever sending the secret. But under our new Dolev-Yao attacker, passivity is no longer guaranteed.

With an active attacker, we must protect the Diffie-Hellman exchange with some form of origin authentication — proof of who the values really came from. Each available tool has a catch:

  • A MAC requires a previously exchanged symmetric key — which is the very thing we are trying to establish.
  • A digital signature requires that the public key be known, and that the mapping between the entity (origin) and that public key be correct.

That last point is the heart of it: knowing a public key is useless if you cannot be sure whose it is. This is precisely the gap that certificates and PKI (later in this lecture) fill.

We need that protection only to secure the Diffie-Hellman exchange itself. Once the exchange has completed securely once, we do have a secure channel — and from then on we can use it to bootstrap everything else. Diffie-Hellman also gives us forward secrecy: the session keys used to encrypt and decrypt information change frequently and automatically, so even if the most recent key or the long-term key is breached, only a minimal amount of sensitive data is exposed.

🤔
Pause & predict: Alice signs her Diffie-Hellman value and sends it to Bob. The attacker swaps in its own signed value. Which of the two catches above (MAC or signature) failed to stop it — and why?

Symmetric Key Distribution with a KDC

The symmetric answer to Boyd's theorem: a mutually trusted third party that everyone already shares a secret with, brokering short-lived keys on demand.

Two parties who wish to communicate securely face the bootstrapping problem: how do they share a key without already having one? The solution is to introduce a Key Distribution Centre (KDC) — a mutually trusted third party.

  • Each party shares a long-term master key with the KDC, established out-of-band (that is, outside the network — for example by physical delivery).
  • The KDC uses those master keys to broker session keys for individual communications.

A KDC is always online and shares a long-term key with every member. In practice those keys are pre-configured by administrators, or derived from a strong password. KDCs are among the most important technologies we use — even newer systems such as OpenID and OAuth fall into this category. The KDC is the basis for the famous Kerberos protocol, which is used across Windows, Linux, UNIX, macOS, AIX and Solaris. Microsoft's Active Directory relies on Kerberos.

The two-level key hierarchy

KDC-based distribution relies on a key hierarchy. At a minimum, two levels of keys are used:

KeyWho shares itHow it is distributedLifetimePurpose
Master keyKDC and each end system or userNon-cryptographically — physical delivery, or bundled with managed software (e.g. copied by IT when they set up your laptop)Long-livedUsed only to protect session keys
Session keyTwo end systems, for one logical connectionObtained from the KDC, transmitted encrypted under the master keyShort-lived — one per logical connection, then discardedUsed for the actual communication

Why the hierarchy? Frequent key changes are desirable, because they limit how much data is compromised if an attacker learns a key. Session keys are cheap and disposable; master keys are precious and rarely used. Encrypting the many short-lived keys with the few long-lived ones keeps the expensive secret out of harm's way.

ℹ️
How a KDC works, in one sentence: A asks the KDC for a session key to use with B; the KDC generates one, encrypts it under the master key it shares with A, and either sends B's copy directly to B or gives it to A to forward — encrypted under the master key the KDC shares with B.
🤔
Pause & predict: the KDC is online all the time and holds a key for every user. If it were compromised, what would an attacker gain? Keep this in mind — it is the price of centralised trust.

The key distribution scenario

The concrete five-step dance — also called the Needham-Schroeder protocol — that hands Alice and Bob a fresh session key and proves both are really present.

This scenario assumes that each user shares a unique master key with the KDC. User A wishes to establish a logical connection with B and requires a one-time session key to protect the data over that connection. A holds master key Ka, known only to A and the KDC; B shares Kb with the KDC.

First, the notation you will see throughout the week:

SymbolMeaning
Ka, KbMaster keys shared by the KDC with A and with B
KsThe one-time session key
N1, N2Nonces — unique identifiers used once, to prevent masquerade and replay (a timestamp, a counter, or a random number)
IDA, IDBIdentifiers of A and B (for example, a network address)
E(K, m)The message m encrypted under key K
fA generic function that transforms a nonce (for example, adding one)
  1. A requests a session key. A sends the KDC the identities of A and B and a unique identifier N1 for this transaction (the nonce). The nonce may be a timestamp, a counter or a random number; the minimum requirement is that it differs with each request, and it should be hard for an opponent to guess — so a random number is a good choice.
  2. The KDC responds, encrypted under Ka. Because only A can read this, A knows it originated at the KDC. The message contains two items for A: the one-time session key Ks, and the original request including the nonce, so A can match the response to the request and confirm freshness (not a replay). It also contains two items for B: the session key Ks and an identifier of A, IDA — both encrypted under Kb.
  3. A forwards the ticket to B. A stores Ks and forwards E(Kb, [Ks ‖ IDA]). Because it is encrypted under Kb, it is protected from eavesdropping. B now knows the session key, knows the other party is A, and knows the information originated at the KDC.
  4. B challenges A with a fresh nonce. Using the new session key, B sends N2 to A.
  5. A answers with f(N2). Also under Ks, A returns a transformed version of N2 (for example, N2 + 1).

Steps 4 and 5 assure B that the message it received in step 3 was not a replay — because A proved she could read the fresh challenge and react to it. Note that the actual key distribution happens in steps 1–3, but steps 3–5 together perform an authentication function. This is the Needham-Schroeder protocol, and we will revisit it in detail under Kerberos.

✅
Exam tip: be ready to write the five steps from memory and to say which steps distribute the key (1–3) versus which authenticate (3–5). The role of N1 (freshness of the KDC reply) and N2 (freshness for B) is a classic short-answer question.

Distributing Keys with Asymmetric Encryption

Public-key cryptography is far too slow for bulk data — so we use it for its real strength: wrapping and delivering secret keys. But naive sharing invites the person in the middle.

Because public-key cryptosystems are inefficient, they are almost never used to encrypt large blocks of data; they are limited to relatively small blocks. One of their most important uses is to encrypt secret keys for distribution. We already know hybrid cryptography works if we reliably know the receiver's public key — but simply advertising the public key does not work.

The person-in-the-middle (PITM) attack

Any attempt to distribute public keys naively can result in a person-in-the-middle attack. Let the attacker be D. The classic five-step failure goes like this:

  1. A generates a public/private key pair {PUa, PRa} and transmits PUa plus an identifier of A, IDA, intended for B.
  2. D intercepts the message, creates its own key pair {PUd, PRd}, and sends PUd ‖ IDA to B — so B believes D's key is A's.
  3. B generates a secret key Ks and transmits E(PUd, Ks).
  4. D intercepts it and learns Ks by computing D(PRd, E(PUd, Ks)).
  5. D forwards E(PUa, Ks) to A.

The result is that both A and B know Ks and are unaware that D also knows it. They happily exchange messages with Ks, while D quietly decrypts everything. D no longer needs to interfere actively — it simply eavesdrops. This simple protocol is therefore only useful where the sole threat is passive eavesdropping.

⚠️
Common trap: the attack works because the binding between the identifier IDA and the key PUa was never verified. Encryption itself was perfect; the protocol was broken. This is exactly why certificates exist.

Adding confidentiality and authentication

The fix is to protect against both active and passive attacks, using nonces. Assume now that A and B already reliably know each other's public keys (PUa, PUb) — we will see shortly how that is achieved. PRa is A's private key; N1 and N2 are A's and B's nonces respectively.

  1. A → B: A encrypts, under B's public key, a message containing IDA and the nonce N1, which identifies this transaction uniquely.
  2. B → A: B replies encrypted under PUa, containing A's nonce N1 and B's new nonce N2. Because only B could have decrypted message 1, the presence of N1 assures A that the correspondent really is B.
  3. A → B: A returns N2 encrypted under B's public key, assuring B that its correspondent is A.
  4. A → B: A selects a secret key Ks and sends M = E(PUb, E(PRa, Ks)). Encryption with B's public key ensures only B can read it; encryption with A's private key ensures only A could have sent it.
  5. B: B computes D(PUa, D(PRb, M)) to recover the secret key.

The result is a scheme that ensures both confidentiality and authentication in the exchange of a secret key.

ℹ️
A hybrid scheme: another way to use public-key encryption is a hybrid approach once used on IBM mainframes. It keeps a KDC that shares a secret master key with each user and distributes session keys under that master key, but uses a public-key scheme to distribute the master keys. The public-key layer gives a secure, efficient way to distribute master keys — an advantage when a single KDC serves widely distributed users.

Four Ways to Distribute Public Keys

Four proposals have been made over the years; each fixes the weaknesses of the one before, until certificates win.

Publicly available directoryA trusted entity maintains a dynamic directory of name-and-public-key entries for each participant.If the directory authority's private key is compromised, the adversary can pass out counterfeit keys, impersonate anyone and eavesdrop on messages to any participant. Certificates and X.509

A certificate is a signed marriage of an identity and a public key — and X.509 is the universally accepted format for that marriage.

In essence, a certificate consists of three things:

  • A public key PU.
  • An identifier ID of the key owner.
  • The whole block signed by a trusted third party — typically a Certificate Authority (CA), such as a government agency or financial institution trusted by the user community.

A user presents their public key to the authority in a secure manner and obtains a certificate, which they can then publish. Anyone needing that user's public key can obtain the certificate and verify it via the attached trusted signature. Formally, for participant A the authority issues:

Certificate creation and verification
CA = E(PRauth, [T ‖ IDA ‖ PUa])    D(PUauth, CA) = (T ‖ IDA ‖ PUa)

Here PRauth is the authority's private key and PUauth its public key; T is a timestamp. Because the certificate is readable only with the authority's public key, a successful decryption proves it came from the CA. The elements IDA and PUa give the recipient the holder's name and public key.

Why the timestamp matters

The timestamp T counters a subtle attack. Suppose A's private key is learned by an adversary. A generates a new key pair and applies for a new certificate. Meanwhile the adversary replays the old certificate to B. If B encrypts messages using the compromised old public key, the adversary can read them. In this context the compromise of a private key is like the loss of a credit card: the owner cancels the number but is at risk until everyone knows the old card is obsolete. The timestamp acts as an expiration date — if a certificate is sufficiently old, it is assumed expired.

Digital certificate — a cryptographic binding between an identifier and a public key that is to be associated with that identifier. An issuer creates the binding and takes the role of a trusted third party (TTP), in accordance with Boyd's theorem.

X.509

One scheme has become universally accepted for formatting public-key certificates: the X.509 standard. X.509 certificates are used in most network security applications, including IP security (IPsec), transport layer security (TLS) and S/MIME. X.509 is based on public-key cryptography and digital signatures. The standard does not dictate a specific digital signature algorithm or hash function.

The certificate for Bob's public key contains unique identifying information for Bob, Bob's public key, and identifying information about the CA, plus other fields. This information is signed by hashing it and generating a digital signature using the hash value and the CA's private key — X.509 indicates that the signature is formed by encrypting the hash value.

ℹ️
Real-world anchor: the lecturer showed an example X.509 version 3 certificate taken from Google Chrome for ChatGPT. If you ever click the padlock in your browser and inspect the certificate, you are reading exactly this structure.
🤔
Pause & predict: a certificate proves that a key was bound to an identity at some moment. What does it not prove about the present? (This question sets up revocation.)

PKI, the Chain of Trust and Root Stores

Certificates only help if you can trace them back to a source you already trust. That tracing is the chain of trust, and its endpoint is a root CA pre-installed on your machine.

The problem in one line: how does Bob know a given public key is actually Alice's? The solution is a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI). The trust anchor is achieved through certificates issued by a trusted third party, the CA.

Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) — a system of hardware, software, people, policies and procedures needed to create, manage, distribute, use, store, verify and revoke digital certificates, and to manage public-key encryption. Its principal objective is to enable secure, convenient and efficient acquisition of public keys.

In practice, Bob may not have a certificate that Alice can verify directly. Instead Bob presents a certificate chain that lets Alice trace the trust back to a common trusted entity. For example, a CA I1 issues a certificate to a subordinate CA I2, and I2 issues a certificate to Bob, represented as X. The chain of trust is I1 → I2 → X. Alice verifies Bob's certificate X by following the chain from X, through I2, up to I1, which she trusts. This process is called certificate chaining.

Root CA (I1)→ Intermediate CA (I2)→ End entity (X)

Trust is transitive: if I1 signs I2, and I2 signs X, then I1 vouches for I2 vouches for X. To verify X, you follow the arrows back to a source you already trust.

The self-signed root and the trust gap

The Root CA sits at the top and signs its own certificate. This creates a trust gap: you cannot verify a Root CA through a chain, because there is no higher authority to vouch for it. The solution is that root certificates are pre-installed in your browser or operating system. To trust Alice, Bob's computer checks every signature in the chain until it hits a pre-installed root.

Hierarchical PKI: from one CA to many

The naive idea is a single global issuer trusted by everyone. This is not practical, for several reasons:

  • It would be overwhelmed by too many requests.
  • Deciding which global authority is trustworthy is itself a huge problem.
  • The verification steps would have to be universally agreed.
  • Names are not globally unique — distinguishing between people both called "John Smith" would need unique global identifiers akin to passports.

The pragmatic solution is to allow many global CAs and accept them all as equals. But then how do users come to trust them? The answer is that the public keys of 'trusted' CAs are shipped with your OS and/or browser. A consequence is that users do not actually get to choose which CAs they trust — the nature of the verification process is defined between vendors and CAs. Identity verification in different jurisdictions can differ, and there is a concern of too much centralisation.

Root certificate stores

  • The repository: root stores hold the certificates of trusted CAs.
  • Trust anchors: they specifically contain the self-signed root certificates that serve as the mathematical starting point for all validation chains.
  • Application use: every application that uses X.509 (for example browsers) or the operating system itself must maintain a root store. Windows, Apple, Linux and the browsers each have their own.
  • Recall: root certificates are self-signed because they are the apex of the hierarchy; they cannot be verified mathematically via a higher authority and must be trusted by decree through pre-installation.

If a certificate's chain ends at a root that is not in your store, verification fails. That is the source of the familiar browser warning: the site's certificate chain ends with a CA your browser does not recognise.

🧠
Memory hook: a root store is a guest list printed on your machine at the factory. Anyone on the list can sign for anyone else, and you were never asked who to invite.

Identity Verification and Intermediate Certificates

Before a CA signs anything, it must check that it is signing for the right person — and intermediate certificates let the crown jewels stay locked in a safe.

Verifying identity before issuing

The issuance process embeds a critical step: there are industry-agreed steps that must be carried out. Before issuing a certificate, the issuer must be sure that it is issuing to the correct identity and that the identity really holds that particular key. The required rigour varies with the purpose and value of the certificate.

Baseline verification

  • Commonly used for domains (e.g. suranga.me).
  • Proof of ownership: the CA asks the domain operator to place a specific file on the web server, or to be able to receive email under that domain name.
  • Note: this relies on technologies that might themselves be insecure for identity verification.

Extended verification

  • High-assurance certificates requiring legal documentation and background checks.
  • More expensive and rigorous, providing a higher level of "human" trust.

It is easier said than done: which verification process is acceptable to everyone? That is the perennial hard question of PKI.

Why intermediate certificates?

Intermediate certificates are "middle-link" certificates that are neither the trusted root nor the final end-entity. Certificates signed by the root certificate are called intermediate certificates; most websites hold certificates signed by an intermediate, not directly by the root. They form a bridge in the chain of trust, and they exist for two main reasons:

  1. Delegated authority (sub-CAs): a root CA can delegate signing powers to different organisations, departments or geographical regions. This enables a hierarchical web of trust without every user contacting the main root CA directly.
  2. Risk mitigation and root protection: the root CA's private key can be kept offline in a highly secure, air-gapped location (often a physical safe), while daily signing operations are handled by the intermediate CA. If an intermediate CA is compromised, it can be revoked without needing to replace the root CA in every user's root store — a process that would otherwise take years.
💡
Why it matters: the root key is the single most valuable secret in the whole system. Intermediates mean it almost never has to be used, so the risk of its exposure is tiny — and a mistake costs only an intermediate, not the entire internet's trust.

Certificate Revocation, CRLs, OCSP and PKIX

A certificate is a promise made at a moment in time — so we need a way to ask, right now, whether that promise still holds.

A certificate is merely a signed statement that a binding was valid at the moment of issuance. We must be able to invalidate it if the trust foundation collapses before the expiration date. There are three classic reasons to revoke:

  • Private key compromise: the key is stolen, leaked, or suspected of being accessed by an unauthorised party (for example, a server breach).
  • Identity fraud or change: identity verification was flawed, domain ownership changed, or the entity (company or employee) no longer exists.
  • CA compromise: the CA that issued the certificate is itself compromised, making all its promises untrustworthy.

To be sure the binding is valid at the time of interest — the moment the receiver receives the message — the receiver must be able to perform a real-time revocation check.

Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs)

A CRL is a list of certificates considered revoked. It should be issued, updated and maintained by every CA. Certificates are identified by serial number, and every CRL must be time-stamped and signed. Technically, a browser should download the CRL (and update it after the given time) and look up the host certificate every time it connects to a server. This is too slow and is not done in practice anymore.

Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) and stapling

Because CRLs can be massive and stale, we use OCSP for live, per-certificate status checks. It is a query-response model: the CRL is hosted server-side rather than on the local host, the query is a lookup of a certificate in a server-side CRL-like structure, and the response contains a certificate status that must be signed.

ModeMechanismDrawbacks / benefits
Standard OCSP (client-side check)The client (Bob) queries the CA's "OCSP responder" with a certificate serial number. The CA returns a signed status: Good, Revoked or Unknown.Drawbacks: it slows the initial connection (latency) and leaks Bob's browsing history to the CA (privacy).
OCSP stapling (server-side solution)The server (Alice) fetches the signed OCSP response from the CA periodically and "staples" it to the certificate during the handshake. Bob receives both the certificate and the proof of validity in one go.Benefits: zero extra latency for Bob and total privacy from the CA.

The PKIX model

While X.509 defines the "data structure" of a certificate, the PKIX model defines how that data moves across the global internet. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) developed the PKIX profile, RFC 5280, so that different vendors (Microsoft, Google, Cisco) can all interoperate using the same rules. PKIX standardises the entire lifecycle, not just the "ID card": it defines Certificate Revocation Lists (the "blacklist" of certificates compromised before expiry), OCSP (a real-time way for Bob to ask the CA whether Alice's key is still valid right now), and the trust models — formalising the hierarchical PKI structure as the technical "rules of the road" for Certificate Authorities.

ℹ️
Serial numbers: a serial number is an integer value unique within the issuing CA and unambiguously associated with a certificate. Both CRLs and OCSP refer to certificates by serial number, which is why the exam may ask you to identify it.

Problems of X.509

Certificates are only as trustworthy as the weakest CA on the guest list — and that list is enormous.

In modern browsers, all pre-installed Root CAs are equally trusted. This means a compromise of a single, obscure CA in a distant jurisdiction can be used to issue a fake certificate for any global domain.

Verification failures (identity fraud):

  • The "Trickster" attack: adversaries have successfully convinced CAs of forged identities, obtaining valid certificates for domains they do not own — for example the Comodo and DigiNotar incidents.
  • Hacked CAs: direct attacks on CA infrastructure have allowed hackers to issue fraudulent certificates at will.

Systemic weaknesses revealed by research:

  • Lax certification practices: many CAs prioritise speed and profit over rigorous identity verification.
  • Operator mismanagement: many website administrators deploy certificates with errors, such as mismatched domain names or expired chains.
  • High-value bias: effective X.509 deployment is often seen only in top-tier, high-value websites; the "long tail" of the internet remains poorly secured.
⚠️
Common trap: do not assume "it has a valid certificate" means "it is trustworthy". A valid certificate only proves that some trusted CA signed a binding — and any one of thousands of CAs can sign for any domain.
🤔
Pause & predict: if one obscure CA is compromised, why can it forge a certificate for a bank it has nothing to do with? (Answer: browsers trust all roots equally, so any root's signature is accepted for any name.)

What Authentication Is and Corroborative Evidence

Authentication is not just "checking a password" — it is a process that must prove a second party actually took part.

We met authentication briefly under access control: before you can use the filesystem, you must log in, and the login is a form of authentication. After authentication, you have authorisation for certain things. But what is authentication?

Authentication (Menezes et al.) — the process whereby one party is assured (through the acquisition of corroborative evidence) of the identity of a second party involved in a protocol, and that the second has actually participated.

Notice the three elements needed to ascertain identity:

  1. Corroborative evidence — the proof itself.
  2. A process between (at least) two parties.
  3. Involvement and participation of the second party.

The last element is the one people forget: it is not enough that someone knows the secret — they must demonstrate they are present and acting right now.

Corroborative evidence: the three classic factors

We look for factors that are unique to an entity. The classic categorisation is:

🔑 Possession

Something you have. A physical key, or a phone that receives SMS codes.

🖐️ Inherence

Something you are. Biometrics: fingerprints, iris scans, face and voice recognition.

🧠 Knowledge

Something you know. Passwords, security questions (mother's maiden name, and so on).

These can be used stand-alone or in combination.

The problems with each factor

Possession. The obvious problem is loss of the physical device. Others are subtler: how is the phone identified — by phone number, or SIM? SMS messages are sent to a number, and numbers can be rerouted. SS7, the global signalling network that also carries SMS, has a bunch of known vulnerabilities.

Inherence. In general, you cannot change biometrics. Some can be easily inferred: fingerprints are everywhere, and even standard cameras can capture them (a demonstrated attack). They are also more susceptible to forgery via recordings, generative AI and 3D printing — including the emerging threat of deepfake fingerprints.

Knowledge. We have already discussed the usability and strength of passwords, and note that databases of cracked passwords exist. Security questions are often very standard, with predictable answers and limited possibilities: "Mother's maiden name?" — depending on culture, try Smith, Chang, Kim, Schmidt; "First car?" — try Golf, Yaris, Corolla. Social networks help attackers collect the additional personal information needed to answer these questions.

⚠️
Common trap: biometrics feel secure because they are unique and unchangeable — but unchangeable is exactly the problem. If your fingerprint leaks, you cannot reset it like a password.

Multi-Factor Authentication and One-Time Passwords

Combining factors from different categories raises the bar — but same-category factors add little, and social engineering can still walk straight through.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) increases security by requiring multiple factors for authentication, in general from different categories. A special form is two-factor authentication (2FA). The canonical example is a password plus a code sent as a text to your phone. This needs a very motivated attacker and assumes a targeted attack, not a broad sweep.

Some factors are stronger than others, so choose factors according to the problem and the setting. For example, possession is strong, but loss and forgetfulness are real problems.

  • 2FA is standard today for high-value sites, such as webmail providers and social networks.
  • MFA often comes with mechanisms that check plausibility: a login from an unusual country the user has never logged in from; many failed login attempts; an IP from a range known to be the origin of other attacks. Then MFA is switched on, or additional factors are required — for example password, plus phone code, plus an email access token.
  • Implementation must follow the principle of psychological acceptability: if authentication frustrates users, they will find ways around it, and security suffers.

The limits of MFA

  • MFA places a bigger onus on the user. Remember psychological acceptability. Generally, MFA is more readily accepted in high-value, high-risk scenarios.
  • MFA with factors from the same category is not guaranteed stronger. Asking for two passwords, both of which may be weak, adds no real security.
  • Social engineering can still easily overcome multiple factors. A phone call that tricks the user into disclosing both the password and the phone code defeats the whole scheme.

One-Time Passwords (OTPs)

Authentication can be strengthened with one-time passwords, which are valid for one login only. This defeats the attacker who records a password and tries to reuse it. There are two categories:

TypeHow it works
Static OTPAn indexed list of OTPs is given to the user in advance.
Dynamic OTPFollows a challenge-response principle, so the OTP generated depends on the specific challenge presented.

Examples include Google Authenticator, Microsoft OTP and Yubikey. Note that OTP can be done as MFA: for instance, the OTP is computed by a device the user must possess, or the website sends a challenge as a code which the user types into the device to derive the OTP. This allows context-specific OTPs. A bank might require an OTP before processing a high-value payment, generated by a device the user possesses or in response to a challenge code from the website.

ℹ️
Which factor is an OTP? An OTP is a form of possession — a token or device you must have. Contrast: a fingerprint is inherence; a PIN or a secret-question answer is knowledge. This distinction is a favourite exam question.

Console vs Network Login, and Authentication Protocols

Sitting at a keyboard proves participation; logging in over a network does not — and that difference forces us to design protocols.

Console login

With a console login, participation is guaranteed by physical keyboard input: the password must be typed locally by someone present. This is a clean example of the "process between at least two parties" element. Even USB attack devices that try to guess passwords face rate-limiting delays — UNIX systems introduce a slight delay between password attempts to defend against rapid automated guessing.

Network login: the participation problem

Over a network there is no physical presence, so it is difficult to prove active participation. The key issues are:

  • Credentials transmitted over the network can be captured.
  • Replay attacks: intercepted credentials can be reused later.
  • You cannot distinguish a legitimate user actively logging in, malware sending stored credentials, or an attacker replaying captured data.
💡
Implication: network authentication requires additional mechanisms — challenge-response, nonces and time-based tokens — to cryptographically prove fresh, active participation, beyond merely "knowing" the password.

Authentication protocols

So far we applied cryptography mostly to single messages. For authentication over a network we must do better: a sequence of steps. A sequence of well-defined steps to achieve a goal is a protocol; if we add cryptographic protection, it is a cryptographic protocol.

Warning: composing cryptographic operations into secure protocols is hard. Even if the cryptography is perfect, we can leave loopholes in the protocol — just like the replay of an encrypted password.

The person-in-the-middle replay

Alice tries to send password p to Bob, encrypted with key k. The attacker can simply record c = Enck(p) and replay it later. Bob does not actually know whether Alice has participated. Neither MACs nor signatures help — they can be replayed as well. We must somehow bind the transmission of the password to the current communication.

Authentication and Key Establishment (AKE)

Freshness defeats replay: challenge-response with nonces, signed Diffie-Hellman, forward secrecy, and APIs that stop developers from making dangerous choices.

Constructing an authentication protocol

Setup: Alice (A) wants to authenticate to Bob (B). The goal is that A proves possession or knowledge of something only A has. Assumptions: Alice and Bob share a secret key kA,B, and Bob has a way to verify Alice's password.

Naive attempt #1: send the encrypted password

A → B: c = Enck(p)

Problem: replay attack. Attacker C intercepts c and replays it; Bob accepts C as Alice. C never needed to know p or k — it just replays c.

Naive attempt #2: add a MAC

A → B: c, MACkA,B(c)

Still broken: the MAC prevents tampering but not replay. C captures both and replays c, MACkA,B(c); Bob verifies the MAC successfully and accepts C as Alice.

Key insight: we need a way to ensure freshness and prove active participation, not just knowledge of the key.

Challenge-response with nonces

Let {m} denote an encrypted and integrity-protected message. A nonce is a "random number used once". The protocol plays out as:

Challenge-response
A → B : NA    B → A : {NA, NB}    A → B : {NA, NB + 1}

B now has assurance that A participated and reacted to precisely the second message, because A was able to read NB (the challenge) and increment it (the response). Nonces can be replaced with timestamps in some cases, but we do not show that here.

AKE: combining authentication and key establishment

Most protocols combine authentication and key establishment. There are multiple approaches:

  • Password-based methods (with challenge-response to prevent replay): use nonces or timestamps to ensure freshness, and derive session keys from the password exchange. Examples: encrypted key exchange protocols.
  • Diffie-Hellman based methods: establish a shared secret without a pre-shared password, combined with authentication (certificates, signatures). Examples: TLS and SSH key exchange.

There are important rules for session key management:

  1. Always generate session keys from your actual, master keys.
  2. Use session keys for one communication session, then delete them.
  3. Never reuse session keys.
  4. Both parties should be involved in the key creation.

A simple AKE uses long-term asymmetric keys to protect short-term symmetric keys. In the simplest hybrid approach, Alice generates a symmetric key k. A better scheme has both sides contribute: Alice and Bob each choose half, so even if one chooses a poor key half by accident, the scheme remains secure. Variants of this are in use, but are being phased out.

Diffie-Hellman recap

Let p be prime and g a generator for ℤp* (the multiplicative group modulo p).

Diffie-Hellman key exchange
Alice: choose random a < p, compute X = g^a mod p, send X to Bob.
Bob: choose random b < p, compute Y = g^b mod p, send Y to Alice.
Alice computes k = Y^a mod p.  Bob computes k = X^b mod p.
Y^a = (g^b)^a = g^(ab) = (g^a)^b = X^b mod p — both sides obtain the same k.

Diffie-Hellman allows key establishment over an insecure channel, under a passive attacker. In practice we sign the Diffie-Hellman values: Alice sends X, SigA(X) and Bob sends Y, SigB(Y). This protects against an active attacker trying to modify them. Alice and Bob use their long-term secret private keys for that, for example RSA keys.

Forward secrecy

Using Diffie-Hellman every time we run a protocol gives us fresh session keys every time — 'ephemeral' keys. But we must choose fresh a, b every time and delete the old ones. This is forward secrecy:

  • If the attacker learns the long-term signing keys, all messages protected with session keys remain secure.
  • If the attacker learns one pair (ai, bi), all messages protected with (aj, bj) where i ≠ j are still secure.
Forward secrecy (Perfect Forward Secrecy, PFS) — a property of key-agreement protocols ensuring that a session key will not be compromised even if the long-term private keys (the "master keys") are compromised in the future. The compromise of long-term keys does not compromise the confidentiality of past session keys.

This is achieved by generating unique session keys for each session that are independent of the long-term keys. Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (DHE) and Ephemeral Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDHE) refer to using Diffie-Hellman to establish a new, temporary key for each session, used exclusively for that session and then discarded. Forward secrecy and perfect forward secrecy are used interchangeably and mean the same thing.

Modern cryptographic APIs

The problem: there are too many ways to make mistakes. Common developer errors include:

  • Poor key lengths or weak random values (predictable seeds).
  • Wrong or no padding (leading to padding oracle attacks).
  • Insecure block cipher modes (ECB mode reveals patterns).
  • Mixing encryption and authentication incorrectly.
  • Reusing nonces or IVs with stream ciphers.

The design philosophy: don't give developers dangerous choices — make safe defaults the only option. Real-world examples include libsodium / NaCl, the AWS Encryption SDK and Google Tink. TLS 1.3 went further and removed weak cipher suites entirely (no RSA key exchange, no CBC mode). What modern APIs cannot prevent are semantic flaws in the protocol — which is why we use standardised, thoroughly vetted protocols such as Kerberos.

🧠
Memory hook for forward secrecy: change the locks after every visitor, and burn the old key. Even if a thief later steals your master keyring, the rooms from last week stay shut.

Needham-Schroeder and Kerberos

The theory becomes a deployed reality: an authentication server that hands out tickets, and the protocol that runs logins on Windows, Linux and macOS.

Needham-Schroeder: the basic idea

The Needham-Schroeder protocol introduces an intermediary 'Authentication Server' (AS) that helps Alice and Bob. It establishes a session key between two users over an insecure network; several variants exist, and we focus on the symmetric-key one. In short, Needham-Schroeder is a way to build a Key Distribution Centre (KDC).

The symmetric-key version proceeds as follows. First, the initiator sends a request; a nonce identifies the session:

Needham-Schroeder protocol
1. A → AS : IDA, IDB, N1
2. AS → A : E(Ka, [Ks ‖ IDA ‖ IDB ‖ N1]) ‖ E(Kb, [Ks ‖ IDA])
3. A → B : E(Kb, [Ks ‖ IDA])
4. B → A : E(Ks, N2)
5. A → B : E(Ks, f(N2))

Step by step:

  • Initiate: the nonce identifies the session.
  • The AS creates the session key Ks and encrypts it with the key shared with Bob — this encrypted block is called the 'ticket'. It sends A both A's copy (encrypted under Ka) and Bob's ticket (encrypted under Kb).
  • Forward the ticket: A passes E(Kb, [Ks ‖ IDA]) to B.
  • Prove knowledge of the session key (freshness!): B sends E(Ks, N2) to A.
  • Prove knowledge of the session key and nonce: A returns E(Ks, f(N2)), where f is a generic function that modifies the value of the nonce.

Kerberos

Kerberos is a widespread protocol for authentication and access control, much deployed as a backend protocol. Built on Needham-Schroeder, it has remarkable features:

  • A ticket concept that allows users to access services.
  • Users authenticate with a password and then request access to a server.
  • Timestamps give tickets a lifetime, after which reauthentication is necessary.
  • It can be used to 'federate' several administrative domains.

Instead of implementing complex authentication protocols on each server, Kerberos centralises the process with an Authentication Server (AS) whose primary function is to authenticate users to servers and vice versa — much like Needham-Schroeder. To stop ticket-granting tickets being captured and misused before they expire, the AS gives both the client and the Ticket Granting Server (TGS) a secret piece of information securely. Even if a ticket is stolen, the thief cannot use it without also having that secret. The client proves its identity to the TGS by revealing the secret securely — efficiently implemented as an encryption key called a session key, which is both the secret and the means of secure communication.

The Kerberos exchanges run in six messages. Here Kc is encryption based on the user's password, and TS values are timestamps:

#ExchangeWhat it means
1C → AS : IDc ‖ IDtgs ‖ TS1The client requests a ticket-granting ticket. IDtgs tells the AS the user wants access to the TGS; TS1 lets the AS verify the client's clock is synchronised with its own.
2AS → C : E(Kc, [Kc,tgs ‖ IDtgs ‖ TS2 ‖ Lifetime2 ‖ Tickettgs])The AS returns the ticket-granting ticket. Kc (from the user's password) lets AS and client verify the password and protects the contents; Kc,tgs is a session key for secure exchange between client and TGS without a permanent shared key; TS2 is the issue time; Lifetime2 the ticket's lifetime.
3C → TGS : IDv ‖ Tickettgs ‖ AuthenticatorcThe client requests a service-granting ticket. IDv names the desired server; Tickettgs proves the user was authenticated by the AS; the authenticator is generated by the client to validate the ticket.
4TGS → C : service-granting ticketThe TGS returns a ticket for server V. Kc,tgs protects the message; Kc,v is the session key for client and server V; Ticketv is the ticket to access server V.
5C → V : TicketvThe client requests the service. Ticketv assures the server that the user has been authenticated by the AS.
6V → C : optional server authentication, service grantedOptional authentication of the server to the client. Kc,v assures the client the message is from V; returning TS5 + 1 assures C this is not a replay of an old reply.
✅
Exam tip: remember the two servers — the Authentication Server (AS) issues the ticket-granting ticket, and the Ticket Granting Server (TGS) issues service-granting tickets. Kerberos is based on Needham-Schroeder (TRUE), and its main target is the person-in-the-middle attack.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else, remember this.

1

The key distribution problem is how to establish shared secret keys or authentic public keys without a pre-existing secure channel. Every primitive hits this bootstrapping wall.

2

Dolev-Yao is the attacker model for the week: it controls the network entirely (eavesdrop, delay, delete, modify, replay, inject, impersonate) but is limited by cryptography.

3

Boyd's theorem: without a secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without a mutually trusted mediator they both already have a secure channel with.

4

Diffie-Hellman is safe only against a passive attacker; an active attacker needs origin authentication, which itself needs a key — hence certificates or a KDC.

5

Symmetric distribution uses a KDC and a two-level hierarchy: long-lived master keys (shared out-of-band) protect short-lived session keys (one per logical connection).

6

Needham-Schroeder is the five-step KDC scenario; steps 1–3 distribute the key, and the nonce challenge in steps 3–5 authenticates and prevents replay.

7

Naive public-key sharing invites the person-in-the-middle attack. Certificates fix this by having a trusted CA sign the binding of identity to public key.

8

X.509 is the universal certificate format. A certificate is a public key plus an identifier, signed by a CA; its timestamp acts as an expiry date.

9

PKI is a chain of trust ending at a self-signed root CA pre-installed in your OS or browser. Intermediate CAs let the root key stay offline and be revocable.

10

Revocation matters: CRLs are too slow, so we use OCSP; OCSP stapling removes latency and protects the client's privacy. PKIX (RFC 5280) standardises all of this.

11

Authentication requires corroborative evidence and proof of active participation, using factors of possession, inherence or knowledge — each with its own weaknesses.

12

MFA and OTPs raise the bar, but same-category factors add little and social engineering still works. Freshness (nonces, timestamps, challenge-response) is what defeats replay.

13

Forward secrecy uses ephemeral Diffie-Hellman keys so past sessions stay secure even if long-term keys leak. Modern APIs enforce safe defaults.

14

Kerberos is the deployed child of Needham-Schroeder: an AS and a TGS issue time-limited tickets so users authenticate once and access many services.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Key distribution problem
The challenge of securely establishing shared secret keys (or authentic public keys) between parties who wish to communicate securely, without relying on a pre-existing secure channel.
Bootstrapping problem
The circular difficulty that every protection mechanism needs a key before it can protect anything, including the distribution of the next key.
Dolev-Yao model
An attacker model in which the adversary has full control of the network — eavesdropping, delaying, deleting, modifying, replaying, injecting and impersonating — but cannot break cryptography (cannot forge integrity-protected messages or decrypt without the key).
Boyd's Theorem
Assuming no secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without a mutually trusted mediator with which both already have a secure channel. In short: you need an introduction from a trusted third party.
Forward secrecy (PFS)
A property of key-agreement protocols ensuring a session key is not compromised even if long-term private keys are later compromised. Past sessions stay secure. Also called perfect forward secrecy.
Ephemeral key
A temporary key generated fresh for a single session and discarded afterwards. DHE and ECDHE establish ephemeral keys and are what give forward secrecy.
Key Distribution Center (KDC)
A mutually trusted third party, always online, that shares a long-term master key with every member and brokers session keys between them. The basis of Kerberos.
Master key
A long-lived key shared between the KDC and an end system or user, distributed non-cryptographically (for example by physical delivery). Used only to protect session keys.
Session key
A short-lived key used for the actual communication between two end systems, one per logical connection, obtained from the KDC encrypted under the master key and then discarded.
Public-key certificate
A public key plus an identifier of the owner, signed by a trusted third party (a CA). It is a cryptographic binding between an identifier and a public key.
Certificate Authority (CA)
A trusted third party (such as a government agency or financial institution) that verifies identities and issues signed certificates, in accordance with Boyd's theorem.
X.509
The universally accepted standard defining the format of public-key certificates. Based on public-key cryptography and digital signatures; used in IPsec, TLS and S/MIME.
Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)
The hardware, software, people, policies and procedures needed to create, manage, distribute, use, store, verify and revoke digital certificates, and to manage public-key encryption.
Chain of trust
The transitive chain of certificates (for example I1 → I2 → X) by which a verifier follows signatures back to a root they already trust. Also called certificate chaining.
Root CA / trust anchor
The CA at the apex of the hierarchy. It signs its own (self-signed) certificate and cannot be verified through a chain, so it is trusted by pre-installation.
Root certificate store
A repository of trusted CA certificates, specifically the self-signed root certificates that form the starting point for all validation chains. Maintained by operating systems and browsers.
Intermediate certificate
A middle-link certificate signed by the root but not the end-entity. It delegates signing authority (sub-CA) and protects the root key, which can stay offline.
Certificate Revocation List (CRL)
A time-stamped, signed list of certificates a CA has revoked, identified by serial number. Technically correct but too slow to download and check on every connection.
Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP)
A live, per-certificate revocation check in which the client queries the CA's OCSP responder with a serial number and receives a signed status: Good, Revoked or Unknown. OCSP stapling has the server fetch and attach the response, removing latency and protecting privacy.
PKIX (RFC 5280)
The IETF profile that defines how X.509 data moves across the internet, standardising the certificate lifecycle including CRLs, OCSP and trust models so different vendors interoperate.
Authentication
The process whereby one party is assured, through corroborative evidence, of the identity of a second party involved in a protocol, and that the second has actually participated.
Corroborative evidence
The factors unique to an entity that are used to establish identity. The classic categories are possession, inherence and knowledge.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Requiring multiple authentication factors, in general from different categories, to raise security. Two-factor authentication (2FA) is the special case of two factors.
One-time password (OTP)
A password valid for a single login only. Static OTPs come from an indexed list; dynamic OTPs follow a challenge-response principle. OTPs act as a possession factor and can be part of MFA.
Replay attack
An attack in which a recorded message (even an encrypted password, MAC or signature) is captured and sent again later to impersonate a legitimate party. Defeated by freshness — nonces, timestamps and challenge-response.
Person-in-the-middle (PITM) attack
An active attack in which the adversary interposes between two parties, substituting its own public key so that both believe they share a secret key that the attacker also knows.
Nonce
A "random number used once", such as a timestamp, counter or random value, used to ensure freshness and prevent masquerade and replay.
Needham-Schroeder protocol
The classic five-step protocol that builds a Key Distribution Centre: an authentication server issues a session key and a ticket, and a nonce challenge proves freshness.
Kerberos
A widely deployed authentication and access-control protocol built on Needham-Schroeder. It uses tickets with a timestamp-based lifetime and can federate several administrative domains.
Ticket Granting Server (TGS)
The Kerberos component that issues service-granting tickets. The client first obtains a ticket-granting ticket from the Authentication Server, then presents it (with an authenticator) to the TGS.
Challenge-response
An authentication technique in which one party sends a fresh challenge (a nonce) and the other must transform and return it, proving they participated in the current exchange rather than replaying an old one.

No terms in this category.

Practice Questions

Answer, reveal, then tick “Mastered”. Your progress is saved.
Q1

State the key distribution problem in your own words, then explain the bootstrapping problem using all three primitives (Diffie-Hellman, hybrid cryptography, MACs/AES).

Answer: The key distribution problem is how to securely establish shared secret keys or authentic public keys between parties without relying on a pre-existing secure channel. The bootstrapping problem is that each primitive still needs something it does not yet have: Diffie-Hellman and hybrid cryptography both need to reliably know the other party's public key, while MACs and AES need a pre-shared symmetric key. Whichever primitive you choose, the same circular dependency remains.
Q2

Describe the Dolev-Yao attacker. What can it do, and what is its one fundamental limitation?

Answer: Dolev-Yao has full control of the network — "the attacker carries the message". It can eavesdrop on any message, delay, delete, modify, replay and inject messages, and impersonate any legitimate party. Its limitation is cryptography: it cannot modify an integrity-protected message without the receiver noticing, and it cannot decrypt a message encrypted with a key it does not hold.
Q3

State Boyd's theorem and explain why Diffie-Hellman key exchange alone is not enough under Dolev-Yao.

Answer: Boyd's theorem: assuming the absence of a secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without an entity that can mediate between them and which both parties trust and have a secure channel with. Diffie-Hellman is secure only against a passive attacker; an active attacker can substitute its own values. Protecting it requires origin authentication, but a MAC needs a prior symmetric key and a signature needs a known public key with a correct entity-to-key mapping — so we are back to needing a trusted third party.
Q4

Explain the two-level key hierarchy used by a KDC. Why is each level distributed differently and used for different things?

Answer: Level one is the master key, shared between the KDC and each end system or user and distributed non-cryptographically (physical delivery or bundled with managed software). It is long-lived and used only to protect session keys. Level two is the session key, used for the actual communication, obtained from the KDC encrypted under the master key, short-lived and one per logical connection. This limits exposure: frequent session-key changes reduce how much data is compromised if a key leaks, while the precious master key is rarely exposed.
Q5

Walk through the Needham-Schroeder key distribution scenario. Which steps distribute the key, and which authenticate?

Answer: (1) A → KDC: IDA, IDB, N1 (nonce). (2) KDC → A: encrypted under Ka, containing Ks and the original request with N1 for freshness, plus Ks and IDA encrypted under Kb for B. (3) A → B: E(Kb, [Ks || IDA]) — the ticket. (4) B → A: E(Ks, N2). (5) A → B: E(Ks, f(N2)). The actual key distribution happens in steps 1–3; steps 3–5 (the nonce challenge) perform authentication and prove the message was not a replay.
Q6

Describe the person-in-the-middle attack against naive public-key distribution and explain how the authenticated scheme with nonces fixes it.

Answer: A sends PUa to B; D intercepts and substitutes its own PUd; B encrypts a secret Ks under PUd; D decrypts it with PRd and re-encrypts it under PUa for A. Both A and B know Ks, but so does D, who can now eavesdrop passively. The fix is to protect against active and passive attacks with nonces: A sends IDA and N1 under PUb; B returns N1 plus a fresh N2 under PUa (proving B decrypted message 1); A returns N2 under PUb (proving A is the correspondent); then A sends M = E(PUb, E(PRa, Ks)) so only B can read it and only A could have sent it, and B recovers Ks via D(PUa, D(PRb, M)).
Q7

List the four schemes for public-key distribution and give the weakness of each (or note why one wins).

Answer: (1) Public announcement — anyone can forge a public announcement. (2) Publicly available directory — if the directory authority's private key is compromised, the adversary can pass out counterfeit keys, impersonate anyone and eavesdrop; records can also be tampered with. (3) Public-key authority — based on Needham-Schroeder, but the authority becomes a bottleneck for every contact, and the directory is still tamper-vulnerable. (4) Public-key certificate — the best option and what we use today, because participants can exchange keys without contacting an authority yet with the same reliability.
Q8

What exactly does a certificate contain, how is it created and verified, and what is the role of the timestamp?

Answer: A certificate contains a public key PU, an identifier ID of the owner, and the whole block signed by a trusted third party (the CA). It is created as CA = E(PRauth, [T || IDA || PUa]) and verified as D(PUauth, CA) = (T || IDA || PUa). Because only the authority's public key can read it, a successful decryption proves it came from the CA. The timestamp T acts as an expiry date: if a private key is compromised and the owner gets a new certificate, an attacker replaying the old certificate can only do so until the old one is considered expired, much like a cancelled credit card.
Q9

Explain the chain of trust and the trust gap. How is the trust gap solved, and what are intermediate certificates for?

Answer: Trust is transitive: if I1 signs I2 and I2 signs X, then I1 vouches for I2 vouches for X, and a verifier follows the chain from X back to a source they already trust (certificate chaining). The Root CA sits at the top and signs its own certificate, creating a trust gap: it cannot be verified through a chain because there is no higher authority. The gap is solved by pre-installing root certificates in the browser or OS. Intermediate certificates bridge root and end-entity: they let a root delegate signing authority to sub-CAs, and they let the root's private key stay offline in an air-gapped safe while daily signing is done by the intermediate — so if an intermediate is compromised it can be revoked without replacing the root everywhere.
Q10

Why must certificates be revocable, and compare CRLs with OCSP and OCSP stapling.

Answer: A certificate is only a signed statement that a binding was valid at issuance, so it must be invalidated early if the trust foundation collapses — private key compromise, identity fraud or change, or CA compromise. A CRL is a time-stamped, signed list of revoked certificates (by serial number) maintained by every CA, but downloading and checking it on every connection is too slow, so it is no longer done in practice. OCSP is a live query-response check: the client asks the CA's responder with a serial number and gets a signed status (Good, Revoked, Unknown), but this adds latency and leaks browsing history to the CA. OCSP stapling fixes both: the server periodically fetches the signed response and staples it to the certificate during the handshake, giving zero extra latency and total privacy.
Q11

Define authentication as Menezes et al. do, name its three elements, and evaluate the three corroborative-evidence factors with one weakness each.

Answer: Authentication is the process whereby one party is assured, through corroborative evidence, of the identity of a second party involved in a protocol, and that the second has actually participated. Its three elements are corroborative evidence, a process between at least two parties, and the involvement and participation of the second party. Factors: possession (something you have — weakness: loss of the device, and SMS rerouting via SS7 vulnerabilities); inherence (something you are — weakness: biometrics cannot be changed and can be captured, forged by recordings, generative AI or 3D printing); knowledge (something you know — weakness: cracked-password databases exist and security questions have predictable answers that social networks help attackers guess).
Q12

Explain challenge-response with nonces, and how signed Diffie-Hellman with ephemeral keys delivers forward secrecy.

Answer: In challenge-response, {m} is an encrypted and integrity-protected message and a nonce is a random number used once. A sends NA; B replies {NA, NB}; A replies {NA, NB + 1}. Because A could read the challenge NB and increment it, B knows A participated in this exact exchange, not a replay. Nonces can be replaced by timestamps in some cases. For forward secrecy, Alice and Bob run Diffie-Hellman and sign their values (X, SigA(X) and Y, SigB(Y)) with long-term private keys, protecting against an active attacker. Using fresh ephemeral a and b every session, and deleting the old ones, means that even if the long-term signing keys leak, past session-key-protected messages stay secure; learning one pair (ai, bi) does not expose messages protected with any other pair.
