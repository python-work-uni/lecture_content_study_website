Who Gets the Keys? Proving Identity and Handing Out Secrets

Every cipher we have studied assumes the parties already hold the right keys. This week closes that gap. You will learn how a trusted third party hands out session keys (Key Distribution Centres and Kerberos), how certificates bind a name to a public key (X.509, PKI and revocation), and how a party proves they are genuinely present now rather than replaying an old message. Along the way you meet the Dolev-Yao attacker and the theorem that says you cannot bootstrap trust out of nothing.

Read this first: cryptography cannot create trust from thin air. By Boyd's theorem, two strangers can only build a secure session if a third party both already trust introduces them. This week is about who that introducer is and what they hand out. Symmetric world: a Key Distribution Centre (KDC). Asymmetric world: a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) of certificates. In both, freshness (nonces and timestamps) stops an attacker replaying yesterday's valid message.

The Key Distribution Problem

Every cipher needs a key, and the hard part is not using the key but getting the right key to the right party without an eavesdropper seeing it.

We have used encryption to keep data confidential, meeting symmetric key cryptography (AES) and asymmetric public-key cryptography (RSA). All require a key, and we quietly assumed the keys were already in place. One factor was missing: how do entities actually obtain the keys they need? In symmetric encryption we assumed a shared key existed; in public-key encryption we assumed the sender knew the receiver's genuine public key. Making those assumptions realistic is the key distribution problem.

Key distribution problem — the challenge of securely establishing shared secret keys (or authentic public keys) between parties who wish to communicate securely, without relying on a pre-existing secure channel to do so.

The caveat "without a pre-existing secure channel" makes it hard: if you had a secure channel you could just send the key over it. The problem is circular, and that circularity is the bootstrapping problem.

🤝 Diffie-Hellman

Needs the other party's public key in a reliable way, or an active attacker substitutes their own.

🔑 MACs / AES

Needs a pre-shared symmetric key. Where does that first key come from?

🧬 Hybrid cryptography

Needs the other party's public key in a reliable way to protect the symmetric key being sent.

This lecture addresses the bootstrapping problem. We also assume a specific attacker, which we meet in the next section.

Without a third party

  • Secure physical shipment of keys is real but expensive and not scalable to internet scale.
  • Used only for high-value communication, e.g. embassies or pairing devices with a QR code.

With a third party

  • Symmetric case: a Key Distribution Centre (KDC).
  • Asymmetric case: a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI).
  • Scales; this is what the internet actually uses.
💡
Why it matters: broken key distribution undoes perfect cryptography. A flawless AES implementation is useless if an attacker hands each side their own key and reads everything in the middle.

The Dolev-Yao Attacker and Boyd's Theorem

We assume the worst network attacker, who controls every message, and then accept a hard limit: without a trusted introducer, no secure session is possible.

In the cryptography overview we defined four attacker capabilities (for example, ciphertext-only, or being able to ask for some ciphertext to be decrypted). For network security we upgrade to a stronger, more realistic adversary: the Dolev-Yao model, reflecting real-world network conditions where the attacker controls the medium entirely. "The attacker carries the message."

The Dolev-Yao attacker has full control over the network: they can eavesdrop on any message, delay, delete, modify, replay and inject messages, and impersonate any legitimate party. But they are fundamentally constrained by cryptography: they cannot modify an integrity-protected message without the receiver noticing, and cannot decrypt a message encrypted with a key they do not hold. Intuitively, this is the attacker we picture for secure electronic communication: all the power of the network, none of the power of the keys.

Boyd's theorem
Assuming the absence of a secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without an entity that can mediate between them, which both parties trust and have a secure channel with.

Rephrased: Alice and Bob cannot securely establish keys between themselves if they do not already have existing, established keys. The only way out is introduction via a third party with whom both already have established keys. This is why KDCs, CAs and trust anchors exist: each plays the role of the mutually trusted mediator.

🧠
Remember: whenever you see a KDC, a CA or a trust anchor, it is satisfying Boyd's theorem. Cryptography alone cannot be the only tool protecting the protocol that establishes the cryptography.

Why Diffie-Hellman Alone Is Not Enough: Forward Secrecy

Diffie-Hellman defeats a passive eavesdropper but not an active attacker; authenticating it buys the property called forward secrecy.

Diffie-Hellman key exchange is secure if the attacker is passive. With an active attacker we need to protect the exchange with some form of origin authentication, or the attacker substitutes their own values. Two primitives can supply it, each with a prerequisite:

PrimitiveWhat it needs first
Message Authentication Code (MAC)A previously exchanged symmetric key.
Digital signatureA known public key plus a correct mapping between entity and key.

Both loop back to key distribution. Consolation: we need this protection only to protect the Diffie-Hellman exchange itself, and once it has completed securely once, we do have a secure channel.

Forward Secrecy (FS), also Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS) — compromise of long-term keys does not compromise the confidentiality of past session keys. FS and PFS mean the same thing.

Even if an attacker gains a server's private key or other long-term credentials, they should not be able to decrypt past communications. This is achieved by generating a unique session key per session, independent of the long-term keys. Learn one session key ki and every other session kj (j ≠ i) stays secret.

Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (DHE) and Ephemeral Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDHE) generate a new temporary key per session, used exclusively for that session and then discarded. Because each session uses a unique, independent key, even if the long-term private key used to authenticate the session is later compromised, the attacker cannot recover the session keys or decrypt past communications.

⚠️
Common trap: forward secrecy only works if the ephemeral values are genuinely fresh each time and the old ones are deleted. Reusing a Diffie-Hellman secret a across sessions destroys the property.

Key Distribution Centres and the Key Hierarchy

A KDC is a mutually trusted third party holding one long-lived master key with every member, used to hand out short-lived session keys on demand.

For symmetric encryption the two parties must share a key, protected from everyone else, and frequent key changes are desirable so less data is exposed if a key leaks. By Boyd's theorem the answer is a trusted third party: the Key Distribution Centre (KDC). Each party shares a long-term master key with the KDC (established out-of-band); the KDC uses these to broker session keys for individual communications. This is a two-level key hierarchy.

long-lived Master key

Shared between the KDC and each end system or user, distributed non-cryptographically (physical delivery, or bundled with managed software, e.g. copied by IT when your laptop was configured). Long-lived, used only to protect session keys.

short-lived Session key

Used for the actual communication between end systems. Obtained from the KDC, transmitted encrypted under the master key, used for one logical connection (frame relay or transport layer) then discarded.

KDCs are among the most important technologies we use. Even newer technologies like OpenID and OAuth fall into this family. They are the basis for Kerberos, used across Windows, Linux, UNIX, macOS, AIX and Solaris; Active Directory relies on Kerberos. A KDC is always online, with long-term keys pre-configured by admins or, in practice, derived from a strong password.

How it works: A asks the KDC for a session key for B. The KDC generates it, encrypts it under the master key it shares with A and sends it to A, and does the same under the master key it shares with B. Alternatively it sends both encrypted bundles to A, who forwards the one for B. Only A and B can read the key.

A requests Ks→ KDC generates Ks→ Wrap for A under Ka→ Wrap for B under Kb
✅
Key idea: the master keys are the crown jewels, distributed out-of-band because no secure channel exists to send them online. Everything else travels encrypted under a master key.

The Key Distribution Scenario

Walk the classic five-step exchange and you see every idea of the week at once: master keys, a nonce for freshness, a session key, a ticket, and a final challenge proving liveness.

Assume each user shares a unique master key with the KDC. User A wants a logical connection with B and needs a one-time session key. A holds Ka, known only to A and the KDC; B shares Kb with the KDC.

Nonce — a "number used once", here a unique identifier N1 for the transaction. It may be a timestamp, a counter or a random number; the hard requirement is that it differs with each request, and it should be hard for an opponent to guess, which favours a random number.
  1. A requests a session key from the KDC, sending IDA, IDB, N1.
  2. The KDC responds under Ka, so only A can decrypt and A knows it came from the KDC. For A: Ks and N1 (freshness). For B: Ks and IDA, both under Kb.
  3. A forwards the ticket E(Kb, [Ks ∥ IDA]) to B, who learns the key, A's identity and the KDC origin.
  4. B challenges A with a nonce N2. (Optional but desirable.)
  5. A proves liveness by replying f(N2) under Ks, assuring B the ticket was not replayed.
The exchange
1. A → KDC : IDA, IDB, N1
2. KDC → A : E(Ka, [Ks ∥ IDA ∥ IDB ∥ N1]) ∥ E(Kb, [Ks ∥ IDA])
3. A → B : E(Kb, [Ks ∥ IDA])
4. B → A : E(Ks, N2)
5. A → B : E(Ks, f(N2))
🧠
Remember: key distribution happens in steps 1–3; steps 4 and 5, and the ticket in step 3, perform authentication. This exact protocol is the Needham-Schroeder protocol, revisited at the end.

Distributing Keys with Asymmetric Encryption

Public-key cryptography is too slow for bulk data, so its most important job is encrypting secret keys; but simply advertising your public key invites a person-in-the-middle attack.

Because public-key cryptosystems are inefficient, they are almost never used to encrypt sizable blocks; they handle relatively small blocks, and one of their most important uses is to encrypt secret keys for distribution. Hybrid cryptography works if we reliably know the receiver's public key, but simply advertising the key does not work: it is vulnerable to a person-in-the-middle attack.

  1. A announces a key — A generates {PUa, PRa} and transmits PUa ∥ IDA to B.
  2. D swaps in its own key — D intercepts, creates {PUd, PRd}, and sends PUd ∥ IDA to B. B believes it is A's key.
  3. B encrypts a secret to the wrong key — B generates Ks and sends E(PUd, Ks).
  4. D recovers it — D computes D(PRd, E(PUd, Ks)).
  5. D re-encrypts to A — D transmits E(PUa, Ks) to A.

Both A and B know Ks and are unaware D knows it too. D stops interfering and simply eavesdrops, decrypting everything. This simple protocol is only useful where the only threat is eavesdropping.

⚠️
Common trap: the attack works because there is no binding between key and identity. B sees a key claiming to be A's but cannot check the claim. Certificates supply exactly that binding.

The fix: confidentiality plus authentication with nonces

Assume A and B have already exchanged public keys by a trustworthy scheme. Then:

  1. A opens with a nonce — A encrypts to B an identifier IDA and a nonce N1 uniquely identifying the transaction.
  2. B answers both nonces — B sends A a message under PUa containing N1 and a fresh N2. Because only B could decrypt message 1, the return of N1 assures A the correspondent is B.
  3. A returns N2 — under B's public key, assuring B that its correspondent is A.
  4. A sends the secret key, double-wrapped — A selects Ks and sends M = E(PUb, E(PRa, Ks)). Under B's public key only B can read it; under A's private key only A could have sent it.
  5. B unwraps — B computes D(PUa, D(PRb, M)) to recover Ks.

This ensures both confidentiality and authentication in the exchange of a secret key.

A hybrid scheme

A third approach, in use on IBM mainframes, keeps a KDC that shares a secret master key with each user and distributes session keys under the master key, but uses a public-key scheme to distribute the master keys. The public-key layer gives a secure, efficient way to distribute master keys, an advantage when a single KDC serves a widely distributed set of users.

Four Schemes for Distributing Public Keys

Ideas for getting public keys to people fall into four families; each repairs the weaknesses of the previous one until certificates remain.

📣 1. Public announcement

Since public keys are meant to be public, anyone can send or broadcast their key. With PGP (Pretty Good Privacy, using RSA), users appended their public key to messages on public forums such as USENET newsgroups and mailing lists. Convenient, but anyone can forge such an announcement.

📖 2. Publicly available directory

A trusted entity maintains a dynamic directory of name-and-key entries. More secure than announcements, but still vulnerable: if the adversary obtains or computes the directory authority's private key they can hand out counterfeit keys and impersonate anyone, or they can simply tamper with the records.

🏛️ 3. Public-key authority

Tighter control over distribution, with a typical scenario based on the Needham-Schroeder protocol. But the authority becomes a bottleneck (a user must appeal for a key for every contact), and the directory remains tamperable.

📜 4. Public-key certificates

Participants exchange keys using certificates, without contacting an authority each time, yet as reliably as if the keys came from the authority. The best option and what we use today, used in PKIs.

Notice the progression: announcements can be forged; a directory centralises trust but creates a target; a live authority creates a bottleneck; certificates let each party carry a verifiable, offline proof. The remaining dependency is that CAs must verify identity honestly, and revocation must work.

🤔
Pause & predict: for certificates to be trustworthy, what exactly must be true of the CA? If a CA signs a false binding, which security guarantee of the certificate breaks?

Certificates and the X.509 Standard

A certificate is a trusted third party's signed statement that a public key belongs to an identity, and X.509 is the universal format for that statement.

A certificate consists of a public key (PU), an identifier (ID) of the owner, and the whole block signed by a trusted third party. Typically the third party is a Certificate Authority (CA), such as a government agency or financial institution trusted by the user community.

Certificate — a cryptographic binding between an identifier and a public key associated with that identifier. An issuer creates the binding and plays the trusted-third-party role of Boyd's theorem.

A user presents their public key to the authority in a secure manner, obtains a certificate, and can then publish it. Anyone needing the key verifies it with the attached trusted signature. For participant A:

Certificate creation and verification
CA = E(PRauth, [ T ∥ IDA ∥ PUa ])
D(PUauth, CA) = ( T ∥ IDA ∥ PUa )

PRauth is the authority's private key and T a timestamp. Because the certificate is readable only with the authority's public key, this proves it came from the CA. IDA and PUa give the name and public key; application for a certificate must be in person or via secure authenticated communication.

Why the timestamp matters: the credit-card analogy

Suppose A's private key is learned by an adversary. A generates a new key pair and applies for a new certificate, while the adversary replays the old certificate to B. If B encrypts to the compromised old key, the adversary reads the messages. Compromise of a private key is like the loss of a credit card: the owner cancels the number, but is at risk until everyone knows it is obsolete. The timestamp T is an expiration date: a sufficiently old certificate is assumed expired.

The X.509 standard

One scheme is universally accepted for formatting public-key certificates: X.509. It defines the format of certificates and is based on public-key cryptography and digital signatures; it is used in IP security (IPsec), Transport Layer Security (TLS) and S/MIME. The standard does not dictate a specific signature algorithm or hash function. A certificate for Bob includes identifying information for Bob, Bob's public key, information about the CA, and other fields; it is signed by hashing the whole block and generating a signature from the hash with the CA's private key. X.509 describes the signature as being formed by encrypting the hash value. Version 3 still does not mandate one algorithm, and you can inspect a real example in Google Chrome for a site such as ChatGPT.

💡
Why it matters: hashing before signing makes signing practical. You sign a short fixed-size digest, not the whole certificate, and any single-bit change to the certificate changes the hash and breaks the signature.

PKI, Chains of Trust, Root Stores and Intermediates

A Public Key Infrastructure is the whole human and technical apparatus around certificates, chaining trust from a website's certificate up to a self-signed root your device was born trusting.

RFC 4949 (Internet Security Glossary) defines public-key infrastructure (PKI) as the set of hardware, software, people, policies and procedures needed to create, manage, store, distribute and revoke digital certificates based on asymmetric cryptography. Its principal objective is secure, convenient and efficient acquisition of public keys.

The problem it solves: if Bob wants to encrypt to Alice he needs her public key, and Alice needs Bob's to verify his signatures. How does Bob know a given key is really Alice's? PKI answers this with certificates: a trusted authority, the trust anchor, digitally signs the binding between identity and key.

Certificate chaining

Bob may not have a certificate Alice can verify directly, so he presents a certificate chain tracing trust back to a common trusted entity. If CA I1 issues a certificate to subordinate CA I2, and I2 issues one to Bob (X), the chain is I1 → I2 → X. Each arrow is a certificate issued by the previous entity. Alice verifies X by following the chain up to I1, which she trusts. This is certificate chaining: each link establishes trust, and the top-most CA is trusted by all.

Root stores and the trust gap

A root store holds the certificates of trusted CAs. Your browser (Firefox or Chrome) has one, as does every operating system. It is the repository of trusted CA certificates; it specifically contains the self-signed root certificates that serve as the mathematical starting point for all validation chains; every application using X.509, or the OS itself, must maintain one. To trust Alice, Bob's computer checks every signature in the chain until it hits a pre-installed root.

Root certificates are self-signed because they are the apex of the hierarchy: no higher authority can verify them, so they are trusted by decree through pre-installation. This is the trust gap: you cannot verify a root CA through a chain.

⚠️
Common trap: not all CAs are in root stores. The classic browser "cert data" error occurs when a certificate's chain root is not in your root store, so no certificate whose chain ends there can be verified.

Intermediate certificates: why the root stays offline

Certificates signed by the root are intermediate certificates, "middle-link" certificates that are neither the trusted root nor the final end-entity. Most websites hold a certificate signed by an intermediate, not the root directly. Two reasons:

  • Delegated authority (sub-CAs): delegates signing to another organization, department or region, creating a sub-CA, so not every user must contact the root CA.
  • Risk mitigation and root protection: the root's private key can be kept offline in a highly secure, air-gapped location (often a physical safe), while day-to-day issuing uses the intermediate's key. If an intermediate is compromised it can be revoked without replacing the root in every user's store, which would take years.

X.509 and the PKIX model

X.509 defines a framework for authentication services provided by the X.500 directory: it can hold public-key certificates, each containing a user's public key signed with a trusted authority's private key, and it defines the certificate format, certificate revocation lists (CRLs), and hierarchical PKI with chains. If X.509 defines the data structure, the PKIX model defines how the data moves across the global internet. The IETF's PKIX working group proposed it (RFC 5280) so vendors interoperate under the same rules: CRLs, OCSP (a real-time validity query), and trust models formalising hierarchical PKI.

Identity Verification and Hierarchical PKI

A certificate is only as trustworthy as the identity check behind it, and because one global CA is impractical, we settle for many CAs shipped as trusted with our OS and browser.

Issuing a certificate embeds a critical step: before issuing, the issuer must be sure it is issuing to the correct identity and that the identity really holds that key. Industry-agreed verification steps vary with the purpose and value of the certificate.

Baseline verification

  • Often used for ordinary domains like suranga.me.
  • Proof of ownership: the CA asks the operator to place a specific file on the web server, or to receive email under that domain.
  • Cheap and fast, but it relies on technologies that may be insecure for identity verification.

Extended verification

  • Uses legal documents and background checks and issues high-assurance certificates.
  • More expensive and rigorous.
  • Provides a higher level of "human" trust in the entity's legitimacy.

The impossible global CA

In all hierarchical PKI variants the issuer is the CA. The naive idea is one global issuer trusted by everyone, but that is impractical: too many requests; deciding which global authority deserves the role is hard; the verification steps would have to be universally agreed; a global namespace would be needed to distinguish common names like "John Smith", effectively passports; identity verification differs between jurisdictions; and there is too much centralization.

The pragmatic "solution": many equal CAs

The working answer is to allow multiple CAs and accept them all as equals. To build trust, the public keys of "trusted" CAs are shipped with the operating system and/or browser. Consequently users do not choose which CAs to trust; the verification process is determined by agreements between vendors and CAs.

⚠️
Common trap: "trusted" here means pre-installed, not independently audited by you. One compromised CA anywhere can issue a valid-looking certificate for any domain, and your browser will trust it.
✅
Exam tip: be ready to name why a single global CA fails (trust determination, universal agreement, global namespace, jurisdiction differences, centralization) and the workaround (many CAs, keys shipped with OS/browser).

Certificate Revocation and the Problems of X.509

A certificate can become a lie after it is issued, so we need revocation checks, and X.509's real-world history shows how often CAs and operators get it wrong.

A certificate is merely a signed statement that the binding between identity and key was valid at the time of issuing. The key could be compromised later, so we must invalidate a certificate if the trust foundation collapses before expiry. Three triggers:

🔓 Private key compromise

The key is stolen, leaked, or suspected of being accessed by an unauthorised party (e.g. a server breach).

🎭 Identity fraud or change

Identity verification was flawed, domain ownership changed, or the entity no longer exists.

🏛️ CA compromise

The issuing CA is itself compromised, making all its "promises" untrustworthy.

To be sure the binding is still valid at verification time, a revocation check is needed, typically a Certificate Revocation List (CRL) or the Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP).

Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs)

A CRL is a list of certificates considered revoked. It should be issued, updated and maintained by every CA; certificates are identified by serial number; and every CRL must be timestamped and signed. Technically a browser should download the CRL, update it after the given time, and look up the host certificate on every connection, but this is too slow and is no longer done in practice.

⚠️
Common trap: CRLs are not merely a bit slow. They can be enormous and stale, giving a delayed, incomplete picture of what is revoked.

OCSP and OCSP stapling

Because CRLs can be massive and stale, OCSP provides live, per-certificate status checks. It is a query-response model with the CRL-like data hosted server-side: the query is a lookup of a serial number, and the response contains a signed status.

FlavourHow it worksDrawbacks / benefits
Standard OCSP (client-side)The client (Bob) queries the CA's responder with a serial number; the CA returns a signed status of Good, Revoked or Unknown.Slows the initial connection (latency) and leaks Bob's browsing history to the CA (privacy).
OCSP stapling (server-side)The server (Alice) periodically fetches the signed response and staples it to the certificate during the handshake, so Bob gets certificate plus proof of validity in one go.Zero extra latency for Bob and total privacy from the CA.

The problems of X.509 in the real world

  • Verification failures (identity fraud): adversaries convinced CAs of forged identities and obtained valid certificates for domains they did not own (the Comodo and DigiNotar incidents); direct attacks on CA infrastructure let hackers issue fraudulent certificates at will.
  • Lax certification practices: many CAs prioritise speed and profit over rigorous verification.
  • Operator mismanagement: administrators deploy certificates with mismatched domain names or expired chains.
  • High-value bias: effective X.509 deployment is mostly seen on top-tier, high-value sites; the "long tail" of the internet stays poorly secured.
  • Equally trusted roots: all pre-installed root CAs are equally trusted, so compromise of one obscure CA in a distant jurisdiction can fake a certificate for any global domain.

What Authentication Is: Corroborative Evidence

Authentication is being assured, through evidence, of another party's identity and that they actually took part, using factors from three classic categories.

Authentication is a key security goal and a preliminary step before access control. To use a filesystem a user must log in, and that login is authentication; success then leads to authorisation, granting permission to access resources or act. Authentication answers "who are you?"; authorisation answers "what may you do?".

Definition (Menezes et al.)
Authentication is the process whereby one party is assured (through the acquisition of corroborative evidence) of the identity of a second party involved in a protocol, and that the second has actually participated.

Three elements are needed: corroborative evidence (factors unique to the entity), a process between at least two parties, and the involvement and participation of the second party.

Three categories of corroborative evidence

🎒 Possession (have)

A physical key, or a phone to receive SMS codes. Strong because physical, but the obvious problem is loss of the device. Subtler: how is a phone identified (number? SIM?), SMS can be rerouted, and SS7, the global signalling network carrying SMS, has known vulnerabilities.

🧬 Inherence (are)

Biometrics: fingerprints, iris scans, face and voice recognition. Once thought secure because they cannot be changed, but some can be easily inferred or duplicated, for example a fingerprint captured with a standard camera. Generative AI and 3D printing make forgery easier, and the major risk is that you cannot change your fingerprint if it is compromised.

🧠 Knowledge (know)

Passwords and security questions (mother's maiden name). Cracked-password databases exist. Recovery questions are standard and predictable: surnames Smith, Chang, Kim, Schmidt, or first cars Golf, Yaris, Corolla. Social networks supply more clues.

🧠
Remember the three: possession (have), inherence (are), knowledge (know). A token, a fingerprint and a PIN are three different categories; two passwords are not.

Console login versus network login: the participation problem

Console login guarantees participation because someone or something must physically input the password, typically via a keyboard; even a USB attack device faces a defence, since UNIX systems introduce a slight delay between password attempts, defeating rapid automated guessing. Network login has no physical presence, so it is difficult to prove active participation: credentials transmitted over the network can be captured, replay attacks reuse them later, and the system cannot distinguish a legitimate user from malware sending stored credentials or an attacker replaying captured data.

Multi-Factor Authentication and One-Time Passwords

Combining factors from different categories raises the bar, and one-time passwords make a captured credential useless by design, though neither is a silver bullet.

Requiring multiple factors enhances security by combining evidence from different categories, such as knowledge and inherence. A special form is two-factor authentication (2FA), typically a password plus a code texted to a phone. This significantly increases security, requiring a highly motivated attacker, and usually thwarts broad, non-targeted attacks.

The choice of factors should suit context and need. Possession is strong because it is physical, but carries the risk of loss, which can lock a legitimate user out. The goal is to balance security with practical considerations like user behaviour and risk.

Plausibility checks and psychological acceptability

2FA is standard today for high-value sites such as webmail providers and social networks. MFA often includes mechanisms that check for plausibility. If a login comes from an unusual country the user has never used, or there are multiple failed attempts, or the IP is from a range known to originate other attacks, MFA may be triggered or extra factors required: for instance a password, a phone code, and an access token by email. Implementation must follow the principle of psychological acceptability: the process must not overly burden or frustrate the user, which is crucial for compliance and security integrity.

Limits of MFA

  • Bigger onus on the user. An attacker can trick a user into sending their OTP, bypassing MFA. MFA is more readily accepted in high-value, high-risk scenarios.
  • Same-category factors are not stronger. Asking for two passwords, both possibly weak, produces no change in security.
  • Social engineering still works. Phone scams trick users into disclosing passwords and codes, defeating multiple factors.

One-Time Passwords (OTP)

One-time passwords are valid for only one login session, so a captured password cannot be reused. Examples of dynamic-OTP systems include Google Authenticator, Microsoft OTP and Yubikey.

Static OTP

  • An indexed list of OTPs is given to the user.
  • Each password is used once, in order.

Dynamic OTP

  • Follows a challenge-response principle.
  • The OTP depends on the specific challenge presented, making it context-specific.

OTP serves as a form of possession and can be done as MFA. A bank might require an OTP for a high-value purchase, generated by a device the user possesses or in response to a challenge code from a website.

✅
Key idea: a one-time password is a freshness mechanism. It defeats replay not by being secret forever, but by being valid exactly once, making a recording worthless the second time.

Building Authentication Protocols

Sending an encrypted password, even with a MAC, still fails to replay; only a challenge-response exchange with a nonce proves the other side is participating right now.

Network communication relies on protocols, which dictate how layers interact. Previously we applied cryptography to single messages; for authentication over a network we need a sequence of steps, which is a protocol, and with cryptographic protection a cryptographic protocol. Creating secure cryptographic protocols is hard: even with flawless cryptography, loopholes in protocol design cause vulnerabilities. An attacker can intercept a password and reuse it later, a replay attack, and even an encrypted password can be replayed.

Concretely: Alice sends password p to Bob encrypted with key k, so an attacker records c = Enck(p) and replays it later. Bob does not know whether Alice participated. Neither MACs nor signatures help, because they can be replayed too. We must bind the transmission of the password to the current communication.

Building blocks

🎲 Secure random numbers

An essential ingredient in all that follows. Weak randomness breaks nonces, keys and Diffie-Hellman secrets.

#️⃣ Hash functions

Give us MACs by mixing in a shared secret.

🔐 Public-key cryptography

Gives key exchange over untrusted channels, confidentiality (hybrid: encrypt symmetric keys), and origin authentication and data integrity.

🔑 Symmetric-key cryptography

Gives confidentiality for the actual data transfer, plus origin authentication and data integrity via MACs.

Naive attempts and why they fail

Alice (A) must prove possession or knowledge of something only she has; assume she and Bob share kA,B.

AttemptProtocolWhy it fails
#1 Send the encrypted passwordA → B : c = Enck(p)Attacker C intercepts and replays c; Bob accepts C as Alice. C never needs p or k.
#2 Add a MACA → B : c, MACkA,B(c)The MAC prevents tampering but not replay. C replays both, Bob verifies, and still accepts C as Alice.

The insight: we need to ensure freshness and prove active participation, not just knowledge of the key.

Challenge-response with nonces

Let {m} denote an encrypted and integrity-protected message; a nonce is a "random number used once".

Challenge-response
A → B : NA
B → A : { NA, NB }
A → B : { NA, NB + 1 }

B is now assured A participated and reacted to precisely the second message, because A could read NB (the challenge) and increment it (the response). An old recording cannot do this, so replay is defeated. Nonces can be replaced with timestamps in some cases, though this lecture does not show that variant.

🧠
Remember the pattern: challenge → transformed response → liveness proven. The same shape appears in OTPs, the KDC scenario and Kerberos authenticators.

Authentication and Key Establishment (AKE)

Most real protocols do authentication and key establishment together, from a password or from signed Diffie-Hellman, obeying four strict session-key rules.

Most protocols combine authentication and key establishment, either by creating a password and securely encrypting and sending it, or by using Diffie-Hellman. Approaches fall into two families.

method Password-based

Uses challenge-response to prevent replay, nonces or timestamps for freshness, and derives session keys from the password exchange. Examples: encrypted key exchange protocols.

method Diffie-Hellman based

Establishes a shared secret without a pre-shared password, combined with authentication (certificates, signatures). Examples: TLS and SSH key exchange.

Four session-key rules
1. Generate session keys from your actual master keys
2. Use a session key for one session, then delete it
3. Never reuse session keys
4. Both parties should be involved in key creation

A simple AKE uses long-term asymmetric keys to protect short-term symmetric keys. In the simplest hybrid approach, Alice generates a key k and sends it encrypted under Bob's public key. A better schema involves both sides in key creation, so even if one party chooses a poor key half the combined scheme stays secure. Variants are in use but are being phased out.

AKE with Diffie-Hellman

Let p be prime and g a generator for ℤp*.

AliceBob
Choose random a < pChoose random b < p
Compute X = ga mod pCompute Y = gb mod p
Send X to BobSend Y to Alice
Compute k = Ya mod pCompute k = Xb mod p

Both sides agree because Ya = (gb)a = gab = (ga)b = Xb. Diffie-Hellman permits key establishment over an insecure channel, under a passive attacker. In practice we sign the values: Alice and Bob do every step the same, except when they send X and Y they also send SigA(X) and SigB(Y), protecting against an active attacker. They use their long-term secret private keys, for example RSA keys.

Forward secrecy, again

Using Diffie-Hellman every session gives fresh ephemeral keys, but we must choose fresh a, b every time and delete the old ones. Then, if an attacker obtains the long-term signing keys, messages encrypted with session keys stay secure, and learning one pair (ai, bi) leaves all other sessions secure.

Modern cryptographic APIs

There are too many ways to make mistakes, from inadequate key lengths and poor randomness to wrong padding or block modes. Common developer errors include poor key lengths or weak random values (predictable seeds); wrong or no padding, leading to padding oracle attacks; insecure block cipher modes (ECB reveals patterns); mixing encryption and authentication incorrectly; and reusing nonces or IVs with stream ciphers. The modern design philosophy is: do not give developers dangerous choices; make safe defaults the only option. Examples are libsodium/NaCl, the AWS Encryption SDK, Google Tink and TLS 1.3, which removed weak cipher suites entirely (no RSA key exchange, no CBC mode).

Needham-Schroeder and Kerberos

Kerberos is the deployed descendant of Needham-Schroeder: an authentication server issues tickets, a ticket-granting server issues access to services, and timestamps bound how long any of it is valid.

The Needham-Schroeder protocol uses an intermediary Authentication Server to help two parties establish a session key over an insecure network. Several variants exist; we focus on the symmetric-key one, which is fundamentally a way to build a Key Distribution Centre.

Needham-Schroeder (symmetric)
1. A → AS : IDA, IDB, N1
2. AS → A : E(Ka, [Ks ∥ IDA ∥ IDB ∥ N1]) ∥ E(Kb, [Ks ∥ IDA])
3. A → B : E(Kb, [Ks ∥ IDA])
4. B → A : E(Ks, N2)
5. A → B : E(Ks, f(N2))

Each line has a purpose: the nonce freshens the session; the AS creates Ks and encrypts the ticket with the key it shares with Bob; A forwards the ticket; B proves knowledge of the session key with N2; and A proves knowledge of the key and nonce by returning f(N2), where f is a generic function that modifies the nonce.

Kerberos: what it adds

Kerberos is widely used for authentication and access control, with much deployment as a backend protocol. Instead of implementing complex authentication on each server, Kerberos centralises it in an authentication server that authenticates users to servers and vice versa. Built on Needham-Schroeder, it offers:

  • A ticket concept that lets users access services securely.
  • Users first authenticate with a password, then request access to a server.
  • Timestamps give tickets a lifetime, after which reauthentication is necessary.
  • Ability to federate several administrative domains.

To address ticket-granting tickets being captured and misused before expiry, the Authentication Server gives both the client and the Ticket Granting Server a secret securely. A stolen ticket is useless without the matching secret, and the client proves identity to the TGS by revealing it securely. The practical way to manage the secret is an encryption key, called a session key: it is both the secret and the means of secure communication.

The Kerberos exchanges

  1. Client → AS: IDc ∥ IDtgs ∥ TS1. Requests a ticket-granting ticket; TS1 lets the AS check the client's clock.
  2. AS → Client: E(Kc, [Kc,tgs ∥ IDtgs ∥ TS2 ∥ Lifetime2 ∥ Tickettgs]). Kc derives from the password; Kc,tgs is the client↔TGS session key; TS2 and Lifetime2 set issue time and lifetime; Tickettgs is the TGT.
  3. Client → TGS: IDv ∥ Tickettgs ∥ Authenticatorc. Requests access to server V; Authenticatorc is fresh client proof.
  4. TGS → Client: E(Kc,tgs, [Kc,v ∥ IDv ∥ TS4 ∥ Ticketv]). Kc,v is the client↔V session key; Ticketv accesses V.
  5. Client → V: Ticketv ∥ Authenticatorc. The ticket proves AS authentication.
  6. Optional V → Client: E(Kc,v, [TS5 + 1]), proving the message is from V and not an old replay.
🧠
Remember the two servers: the AS (Authentication Server) proves who you are and issues the ticket-granting ticket; the TGS (Ticket Granting Server) turns that into tickets for individual services. A ticket is encrypted for the target; an authenticator is a fresh, timestamped client proof.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else from this week, remember these twelve points.

1

Key distribution is the bootstrapping problem. Diffie-Hellman and hybrid crypto need a trustworthy public key; MACs and AES need a pre-shared symmetric key.

2

Assume the Dolev-Yao attacker: full network control (eavesdrop, delay, modify, replay, inject, impersonate) but no power against cryptography itself.

3

Boyd's theorem: without a secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without a mutually trusted mediator. That is why KDCs and PKIs exist.

4

Diffie-Hellman needs origin authentication against an active attacker, and gives forward secrecy: long-term key compromise does not unlock past sessions if each session uses fresh ephemeral values that are deleted.

5

KDCs use a two-level hierarchy: long-lived master keys shared out-of-band with the KDC, and short-lived session keys sent encrypted under the master keys.

6

Naive public-key distribution invites a person-in-the-middle attack. The fix adds nonces and double-wrapped key material for confidentiality plus authentication.

7

Certificates beat live authorities: a CA-signed binding of identity to public key, verified offline as D(PUauth, CA) = (T ∥ IDA ∥ PUa), with the timestamp as expiry.

8

X.509 is the universal certificate format, used in IPsec, TLS and S/MIME; it is hash-then-signed and mandates no single algorithm.

9

PKI chains trust up to a self-signed root pre-installed in your OS or browser; intermediates keep the root key offline and can be revoked without replacing the root.

10

Certificates go stale. CRLs are signed blacklists but too slow; OCSP is live but leaks privacy; OCSP stapling is fast and private.

11

Authentication needs corroborative evidence and participation. Factors are possession, inherence and knowledge; MFA, OTPs and challenge-response exist to prove fresh, active participation.

12

An encrypted password and a MAC both replay. Only binding the exchange to the current conversation with nonces or timestamps defeats replay. Kerberos is the deployed standard built on these ideas.

Glossary

Every load-bearing term from this week, grouped so you can filter to what you need.

Key distribution problem
Securely establishing shared secret keys or authentic public keys without relying on a pre-existing secure channel.
Bootstrapping problem
Every primitive needs something it does not yet have: Diffie-Hellman and hybrid crypto need a trustworthy public key; MACs and AES need a pre-shared symmetric key.
Dolev-Yao model
An attacker with full network control who can eavesdrop, delay, modify, replay and inject messages and impersonate any party, but cannot forge protected messages or decrypt without the key.
Boyd's theorem
Without a pre-existing secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without a mutually trusted mediator both already share a secure channel with.
Nonce
A "number used once", unique to a transaction. It may be a timestamp, counter or random number; it must differ each time and be hard to guess.
Forward secrecy (Perfect Forward Secrecy)
Compromise of long-term keys does not compromise past session keys. FS and PFS mean the same thing.
Session key
A short-lived key used for the actual communication in one logical connection, obtained from the KDC encrypted under a master key, then discarded.
Master key
A long-lived key shared between the KDC and each end system or user, distributed non-cryptographically, used only to protect session keys.
Replay attack
Capturing a valid message and sending it again later. Encrypted passwords, MACs and signatures can all be replayed unless bound to the current communication.
Person-in-the-middle attack
An attacker intercepts and substitutes keys or messages between two parties who believe they talk directly, learning their shared secret unnoticed.
Key Distribution Centre (KDC)
A mutually trusted, always-online third party sharing a long-term master key with every member and brokering session keys. The basis of Kerberos.
Needham-Schroeder protocol
A protocol using an authentication server to establish a session key over an insecure network. The symmetric variant builds a KDC and is the basis of Kerberos.
Kerberos
A widely deployed authentication and access-control protocol built on Needham-Schroeder, using an Authentication Server, a Ticket Granting Server, tickets with lifetimes, and federation of domains.
Ticket
In Kerberos, a credential encrypted for the target (ticket-granting or service ticket) proving the client was authenticated by the AS and carrying a session key.
Authenticator
In Kerberos, a fresh, timestamped value generated by the client to validate a ticket to the TGS or a service, proving the holder is present now.
Diffie-Hellman key exchange
A method for two parties to agree on a shared key over an insecure channel. Secure against a passive attacker; must be signed against an active one.
Certificate
A cryptographic binding between an identifier and a public key, created and signed by a trusted third party (issuer). It asserts validity at issue time.
Certificate Authority (CA)
The trusted third party that verifies an identity and signs a certificate binding that identity to a public key.
X.509
The universally accepted standard defining the format of public-key certificates, based on public-key cryptography and digital signatures, used in IPsec, TLS and S/MIME.
Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)
The hardware, software, people, policies and procedures needed to create, manage, store, distribute and revoke digital certificates.
Certificate chain
A sequence of certificates in which each entity is certified by the one above, tracing trust from an end-entity certificate up to a root CA.
Root store / trust anchor
The self-signed root CA certificates pre-installed in an OS or browser that serve as the mathematical starting point for validating chains.
Intermediate certificate
A "middle-link" certificate signed by the root and used to sign end-entity certificates, keeping the root key offline and enabling sub-CAs.
Certificate Revocation List (CRL)
A timestamped, signed blacklist of certificates revoked before expiry, issued by each CA, with certificates identified by serial number. Too slow to fetch per connection.
Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP)
A live query-response check returning Good, Revoked or Unknown for a certificate. Fast but adds latency and leaks browsing history.
OCSP stapling
A server-side fix where the server fetches the signed OCSP response and staples it to the certificate during the handshake, giving no extra latency and full privacy.
Authentication
The process whereby one party is assured, through corroborative evidence, of a second party's identity in a protocol and that the second party actually participated.
Corroborative evidence
Factors unique to an entity in three categories: possession (what you have), inherence (what you are) and knowledge (what you know).
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Authentication requiring factors from more than one category, e.g. a password plus a phone code. Same-category factors do not strengthen it.
One-time password (OTP)
A password valid for a single login. Static OTPs are an indexed list; dynamic OTPs follow a challenge-response principle and depend on the challenge.

No terms in this category.

Answer, reveal, then tick "Mastered". Your progress is saved.
Q1

Define the key distribution problem and the bootstrapping problem, and give the missing key for Diffie-Hellman, MACs/AES and hybrid cryptography.

Answer: The key distribution problem is securely establishing shared secret keys (or authentic public keys) without a pre-existing secure channel. The bootstrapping problem is that every primitive needs something it lacks: Diffie-Hellman and hybrid cryptography need the other party's reliable public key; MACs/AES need a pre-shared symmetric key. KDCs and PKIs address this.
Q2

Describe the Dolev-Yao attacker and state Boyd's theorem. What does the theorem force us to introduce?

Answer: Dolev-Yao has full network control ("the attacker carries the message"): eavesdrop, delay, delete, modify, replay, inject, impersonate. Cryptography constrains them: they cannot modify an integrity-protected message undetected, nor decrypt without the key. Boyd's theorem: without a secure channel, two entities cannot establish an authenticated session without a mutually trusted mediator both share a secure channel with, forcing a KDC (symmetric) or CA/PKI (asymmetric).
Q3

Why is Diffie-Hellman not enough alone, what is forward secrecy, and how does signed ephemeral Diffie-Hellman provide it?

Answer: Diffie-Hellman is secure only against a passive attacker; an active attacker needs origin authentication (a MAC needs a pre-shared key; a signature needs a known public key and a correct entity-to-key mapping). Forward secrecy means long-term key compromise does not expose past session keys, because each session has an independent key. Signing each ephemeral X and Y proves origin and blocks modification while giving fresh per-session keys, so a later stolen signing key cannot decrypt past sessions.
Q4

Explain the KDC key hierarchy: master key versus session key, and why each is distributed as it is.

Answer: The master key is shared between the KDC and each end system or user, is long-lived, and is distributed non-cryptographically (physical delivery, bundled software, or copied by IT), because no secure channel exists to send it. It only protects session keys. The session key is used for the actual communication, is short-lived (one per logical connection), is obtained from the KDC encrypted under the master key, and is then discarded. This limits exposure to a single session.
Q5

Walk through the symmetric key distribution scenario. Which steps distribute the key and which authenticate?

Answer: (1) A asks the KDC for a session key for B, sending IDA, IDB, N1. (2) The KDC replies under Ka with Ks and N1 (freshness), plus E(Kb, [Ks ∥ IDA]) for B. (3) A forwards that ticket. (4) B sends a nonce N2 under Ks. (5) A replies f(N2). Key distribution is steps 1–3; steps 4–5 (and the ticket in step 3) perform authentication. This is Needham-Schroeder.
Q6

Describe the person-in-the-middle attack on naive public-key distribution and how the nonce-based scheme fixes it.

Answer: (1) A sends PUa ∥ IDA to B. (2) D intercepts, creates {PUd, PRd}, and sends PUd ∥ IDA to B. (3) B sends E(PUd, Ks). (4) D decrypts with PRd to learn Ks. (5) D re-encrypts to A. Both believe they share Ks while D knows it. The fix assumes authentic public keys: A sends N1; B returns N1 and fresh N2; A returns N2; A sends M = E(PUb, E(PRa, Ks)).
Q7

List the four public-key distribution schemes with the main weakness of each. Which do we use today?

Answer: (1) Public announcement (e.g. PGP keys on USENET): anyone can forge it. (2) Publicly available directory: compromising the authority's private key or tampering with records yields counterfeit keys. (3) Public-key authority (Needham-Schroeder based): a bottleneck, and the directory is still tamperable. (4) Public-key certificates: exchange offline-verifiable certificates. This is the best option and what we use today, in PKIs.
Q8

Write the certificate creation and verification formulas and explain the timestamp with the credit-card analogy.

Answer: CA = E(PRauth, [T ∥ IDA ∥ PUa]) and verification D(PUauth, CA) = (T ∥ IDA ∥ PUa). Because only the authority's public key makes it readable, this proves it came from the CA. The timestamp counters replay of an old certificate after a key compromise: A gets a new key, but an adversary replays the old certificate to B, who encrypts to the compromised key. It is like a lost credit card: canceling helps only once everyone knows the old number is obsolete. A sufficiently old certificate is treated as expired.
Q9

What does X.509 define, where is it used, and how is a certificate signed?

Answer: X.509 defines the format of public-key certificates, based on public-key cryptography and digital signatures, used in IP security, TLS and S/MIME; it mandates no specific signature algorithm or hash function. A certificate contains the subject's identifying information, its public key, CA information and other fields. It is signed by hashing the block and signing the hash with the CA's private key (X.509 describes this as encrypting the hash). A live example is in Google Chrome's certificate viewer for a site such as ChatGPT.
Q10

Explain certificate chaining, root stores, the trust gap, and why intermediate certificates exist.

Answer: Chaining traces trust from an end-entity certificate up through issuing CAs to a common trusted entity: if I1 certifies I2 and I2 certifies X, the chain is I1 → I2 → X. A root store holds trusted CA certificates, specifically the self-signed roots that start every chain; a root cannot be verified higher, so it is trusted by pre-installation, the trust gap. Verification checks every signature to a pre-installed root. Intermediates allow sub-CAs and keep the root key offline, so a compromised intermediate is revocable without replacing the root.
Q11

Contrast baseline and extended verification. Why is one global CA impractical, and what is the pragmatic solution?

Answer: Baseline verification (ordinary domains such as suranga.me) is proof of ownership: place a file on the web server or receive email under the domain; it may rely on insecure technologies. Extended verification uses legal documents and background checks for high-assurance certificates, giving higher "human" trust. A single global CA is impractical: too many requests, hard to determine a trustworthy authority, universal agreement needed, the global-namespace problem (common names like "John Smith"), differing jurisdictions, and over-centralization. The solution is many equal CAs whose keys ship with the OS or browser.
Q12

Compare CRLs, OCSP and OCSP stapling. When must a certificate be revoked, and what are X.509's real-world problems?

Answer: A CRL is a signed, timestamped list of revoked certificates, identified by serial number, issued by each CA; downloading and searching it per connection is too slow and no longer done. OCSP is a live query-response check returning Good, Revoked or Unknown; it adds latency and leaks browsing history. OCSP stapling has the server staple the signed response to the certificate, giving zero extra latency and full privacy. Revoke on private key compromise, identity fraud or change, or CA compromise. X.509 problems: forged identities (Comodo, DigiNotar), hacked CAs, lax practices, operator mismanagement, high-value bias, and all roots equally trusted.
Q13

Define authentication, give the three evidence categories and their weaknesses, explain network versus console login, and list MFA's limits.

Answer: Authentication is the process whereby one party is assured, through corroborative evidence, of a second party's identity in a protocol and that they actually participated. It needs corroborative evidence, a process between at least two parties, and the second party's participation. Categories: possession (key or phone; loss risk, SMS reroutable via SS7), inherence (biometrics; can be duplicated, and you cannot change a fingerprint), knowledge (passwords and predictable security questions). Console login guarantees participation by physical input and UNIX delays repeated attempts; network login has no physical presence, so credentials can be replayed and a real user cannot be distinguished from malware, requiring challenge-response, nonces and time-based tokens. MFA limits: user burden, same-category factors add nothing, and social engineering still works.
Q14

Why do an encrypted password and a MAC both fail against replay, how does challenge-response fix it, and outline Kerberos.

Answer: Sending c = Enck(p) fails because the attacker records and replays c; adding a MAC still fails because a MAC prevents tampering, not replay. Neither proves fresh participation. The fix is challenge-response with nonces: A → B : NA; B → A : {NA, NB}; A → B : {NA, NB + 1}; only a live party can read NB and increment it. Kerberos, built on Needham-Schroeder, centralises authentication in an AS and a TGS: the client gets a ticket-granting ticket from the AS, presents it (plus a fresh authenticator) to the TGS for a service ticket, then presents that to the service. Tickets carry lifetimes via timestamps; the AS gives client and TGS a shared secret so a stolen ticket is useless; and Kerberos can federate domains, primarily mitigating person-in-the-middle attacks.
