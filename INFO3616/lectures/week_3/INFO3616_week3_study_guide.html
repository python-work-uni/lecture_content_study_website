Who Gets In, and What May They Touch? The Machinery of Access Control

Every system must repeatedly answer one question: is this principal allowed to perform this operation on this object? This week we build the vocabulary for that question, meet discretionary and mandatory access control, tour the access control built into operating systems, middleware and hardware, and finish with the formal models, Bell-LaPadula and Biba.

Read this first: hold one mental model all week — access control decides a triple: subject, object, operation. A subject (user, process or machine) tries an operation (read, write, execute) on an object (a file, port, record or device). Everything else is machinery for making that decision quickly, safely, with the right authority behind it. When you meet a new mechanism, ask: who decides, and how is it enforced?

By the end you will be able to

  • Define access control and separate it from authentication and authorization.

  • Contrast discretionary and mandatory access control with a real example of each.
  • Describe access control lists and capability lists and their relation to the matrix.
  • Decode Unix permissions in symbolic and octal form, including SUID, SGID and the sticky bit.
  • Explain access control in macOS, Windows, iOS and Android.
  • Describe middleware, browser, sandboxing, virtualisation and hardware access control.
  • State and apply the Bell-LaPadula and Biba properties, including star and invocation.
  • Relate roles, attributes and least privilege to real organisational access control.

What Is Access Control?

Access control is the part of a security policy that decides who may do what to which resources.

Access control — an important element of a security policy, aiming to control access to resources and assets. It governs which principals (users, user groups, processes, machines) have access to which resources: which files they can read, which programs they can execute, and how they share data.

The lecture opens with two questions. Can a customer of a bank access the bank vault? And can a program access the passwords of other users? One is physical, one digital, but both ask whether somebody may reach a resource they may or may not be entitled to.

Three words people mix up

  • Authentication — acquiring evidence of the identity of another party, such as a password or fingerprint. It answers who you are.
  • Authorization — the allocation of a privilege to a party (notes), or verifying whether an entity is allowed an action (slides). It answers what you may do.
  • Access control — the machinery that restricts access to resources, turning an authorization decision into an enforced outcome.

They form a pipeline: identify, authenticate, authorize, then access the resource. Within the reference framework, access control is specified in the policy; it counters the incentives of an attacker who wants a resource; it is implemented by one or more mechanisms, such as a password; and every mechanism gives a level of assurance. Passwords are the example: password strength relates directly to the assurance provided.

💡
Why it matters: separating policy from mechanism prevents a common confusion. "Only managers may read payroll" is a policy; a password plus a permission check is a mechanism. Insecurity often comes from a mismatch between the two.
🤔

The Access Control Matrix

The cleanest picture of access control is a grid: subjects down the side, objects across the top, rights in the cells.

Access controls can be modelled as a matrix of access permissions, with columns for files (objects) and rows for users (subjects). The lecture writes r for read, w for write, x for execute, and - for no access. Cells can also hold read, append, read-write, execute, or the empty set.

  • Sam, the system administrator, has universal access except the audit trail, which even he should only read: the admin may not quietly alter the logs.
  • Alice, the manager, needs to execute the OS and applications but only through approved interfaces, so she has no write access to the OS or programs. She does need to read and write the data.
  • Bob, the auditor, can read everything. No cell in his row contains a w.

Each row is a job description expressed as permissions. The four nouns to keep: a subject is an active entity that requests access (a person, group, process or machine), forming the rows; an object is a passive thing (a file, process, port, record or device), forming the columns; an operation is a kind of action such as read, write or execute, and may be coarse (read/write/execute) or very fine-grained; and a right is the permission a subject holds over an object, living in a matrix cell.

🧠
Remember: rows are subjects, columns are objects, cells are rights. Every real mechanism this week is a way of storing or compressing that grid.

Policy, Mechanism and the Reference Monitor

A policy says what is allowed; a mechanism enforces it; a trusted reference monitor is the checkpoint every request must pass.

When analysing a system's security, the lecture recommends a fixed order: analyse incentives and build a threat model describing the attacker's motivations and capabilities; analyse the security policy, the goals to be achieved, which is relatively verbose; then analyse the security mechanisms that implement the policy and mitigate, detect and respond to threats. The policy is the "what"; the mechanism is the "how". You can have a perfect policy and a broken mechanism, or a flawless mechanism enforcing a careless policy.

Security policy model — a highly precise, succinct statement about the protection properties of a system. It can be highly formal and drives engineering. Bell-LaPadula and Biba are famous examples.

The reference monitor

How do we ensure the policy is applied every time, not skipped when inconvenient? The classic answer is a reference monitor: an abstract gatekeeper between subjects and objects that validates every request. The lecture gives two concrete faces of it. In mandatory access control, a 'security kernel' checks via security attributes whether a subject may interact with an object. In Windows, access is enforced via a Security Reference Monitor (SRM), a trusted component providing fine-grained security. A reference monitor must satisfy three standard requirements: complete mediation (every access to every object is checked, with no side door), tamper-proof (untrusted code cannot modify or disable it), and verifiable (small and simple enough to analyse).

💡
Why it matters: complete mediation separates real access control from security theatre. A lock checked only when the door is closed, never when it is ajar, is not access control. This is why the kernel and hypervisor get so much attention: natural places for a small, tamper-proof, verifiable monitor.
🤔

Discretionary Access Control (DAC)

In DAC the owner decides, and can pass privileges on at will.

Historically, anyone with physical access to a computer controlled all of it: you could load any software, inspect everything in memory or on disk, and change anything. You start your computer in supervisor mode and, as administrator, make less-privileged accounts available for less-trusted tasks, such as running apps from companies you do not entirely trust, or giving remote logon access to others.

Discretionary Access Control (DAC) — access to objects is determined by the identity of the subject that owns them. Subjects can transfer privileges over their objects to other subjects unless constraints exist, such as those defined by Mandatory Access Control. DAC offers high flexibility and considerable control to the subjects.

Discretionary means the owner uses their own judgement, so the answer to "may you read this file?" depends on who you are relative to the owner.

DAC in the wild: Microsoft OneDrive

  • Ownership — the creator of a file or folder is the owner by default, and owners can transfer ownership.
  • Sharing options — Private (private by default), Specific People (share via email), Anyone with the Link (viewing or editing), and Public (accessible to anyone).
  • Permission levels — View can see the file; Edit can see and modify it.

Every one of those is the owner's choice; the system supplies options but does not dictate the decision.

✅
Do this: when a user can decide who gets access and hand that access on, you are looking at DAC. Always ask the follow-up a security engineer asks: at scale, who tracks all these individual decisions? That leads straight to MAC.
🤔

Mandatory Access Control (MAC)

In MAC a central authority sets the rules, and no ordinary user can override them — not even the system administrator.

DAC hardens badly at scale and leaves final authority with the machine's owner or admin. In the 1970s the US military began a large computer-security research program to protect classified information: ensure a file marked Top Secret would never be made available to a user with only Secret clearance, regardless of the actions of any ordinary user or even of the supervisor.

Mandatory Access Control (MAC) — an external entity, the security policy administrator, defines access. A 'security kernel' checks via security attributes whether a subject may interact with an object. MAC offers a high level of rigour.

In a multilevel secure (MLS) system the sysadmin is no longer the boss: ultimate control rests with a remote government authority that sets policy, and root access itself is under remote control.

MAC in the wild: OneDrive in an enterprise

Microsoft 365 admins set sharing and access policies, for example restricting sharing to specific domains, and Data Loss Prevention (DLP) automatically protects sensitive information and restricts access based on policies. The individual no longer decides whether a sensitive document can be shared externally. OneDrive primarily uses DAC for personal use, with MAC elements in enterprise environments; many real systems mix the two.

Discretionary (DAC)

  • Decided by the object's owner
  • Based on the identity of the owning subject
  • Subjects can transfer privileges, unless constrained
  • High flexibility, hard to manage at scale

Mandatory (MAC)

  • Defined by an external authority
  • Enforced by a security kernel using security attributes
  • Users cannot change permissions
  • High rigour; root is under remote control
⚠️
Common trap: do not define MAC as "access control done by the operating system". DAC is also implemented by the OS. The defining feature is who holds ultimate authority: MAC's rules are imposed from outside the owner's control, typically through labels and clearances.

Access Control Lists and Capability Lists

The matrix can be stored two ways: a list attached to each object, or a set of tickets held by each subject.

Access Control Lists (ACLs)

Access Control List (ACL) — a list attached to an object defining which subjects may access it and how. It can be global or more fine-grained. An entry consists of a user ID and a mapping to allowed operations, from coarse (read/write/execute) to very fine-grained permissions.

ACLs are a widespread mechanism, common in all UNIX systems (the POSIX standards) and present in Windows. They are also used in social networking sites, content management systems and enterprise resource management. The lecture's example ACL for one object is Sam rw, Alice rw, Bob r — the Accounting Data column of the earlier matrix, stored beside that object. To learn who can touch the accounting data, you read one list.

Capability lists

The complement stores a row on each subject: a list of unforgeable tickets, each naming an object and the operations that subject may perform on it. Holding the ticket is the authority. The lecture shows capabilities in several places: Android's permissions "are in effect capabilities" granting access to services such as SMSes, the camera and the address book; iOS grants apps capabilities through APIs; and ARM's CHERI adds fine-grained capability support so a subthread can be granted access to specific memory ranges.

ACL (object side)

  • Stored with each object
  • Answers: who may access this thing?
  • Easy to audit one object; hard to answer "what can Alice reach?"

Capability list (subject side)

  • Stored with each subject
  • Answers: what may this subject access?
  • Easy to revoke a whole user's access; hard to audit one object
🧠
Remember: an ACL is the matrix column (object-centric: "who can touch me?"). A capability list is the row (subject-centric: "what can I touch?"). Both store identical information; they differ in which question is cheap to answer.
🤔

Unix and Linux File Permissions

Unix compresses access control into nine bits: three permissions for three audiences.

An OS typically authenticates principals with passwords or fingerprints, then authorises access to files, ports and other system resources. In Unix, each file and directory carries three permissions — read (r), write (w) and execute (x) — assigned to three categories: the owner, the group and others.

Each user has a User ID (UID) and a Group ID (GID), and may belong to extra groups such as sudo. Two files show this: /etc/passwd, whose entry sam:x:1000:1000:Sam Jones,,,:/home/sam:/usr/bin/zsh also states the login shell, and /etc/group, whose entry sudo:x:27:sam,alice states the group name, GID and members.

The nine bits

  • Owner ID — the user that owns the file. Group ID — the group that 'relates to' the file.
  • Owner R/W/X — R means read, W write, X execute. Directories with X set can be traversed.
  • Group R/W/X — for the group. Other R/W/X — for everyone else.

Permissions appear in symbolic notation (rwxr-xr-x) or octal notation (755), and are modified with commands such as chmod, chown and chgrp. Consider -rw-r--r-- 1 sam sam 24K Jun 12 10:20 email.png: the first - marks a regular file, then rw- gives the owner read and write, r-- gives the group read, and r-- gives others read. So all three audiences can read, but only the owner can write.

Octal notation

Octal sums the bits in each triple: r = 4, w = 2, x = 1. So rwx = 7, rw- = 6, r-x = 5 and r-- = 4. rwxr-xr-x therefore becomes 755: owner 7, group 5, others 5. A dangerous example is rwxrwxrwx (777): readable, writable and executable by owner, group and everyone, which is normally far too open.

✅
Do this: split a permission string into the file-type character and three triples. "Owner, group, others" never changes; only the letters do. Convert each triple to a digit by adding 4, 2 and 1.

Special Bits, Process Identity, Root and sudo

Three extra bits and three flavours of user ID decide what a program may do the moment it runs.

  • Set-user-ID (SUID) — the owner can mark a program so it runs with the privilege of its owner rather than the invoking user. Useful for specific privileged programs without being a privileged user; mount is an example.
  • Set-group-ID (SGID) — on a file, it executes as the group that owns the file. On a directory, files created inside inherit the group of the directory owner.
  • Sticky bit — does not affect individual files. At the directory level it restricts deletion: only the owner (and root) may remove a file. Common example: /tmp.

Processes inherit permissions, with three IDs

  • Real UID/GID — the actual user and group that initiated the process.
  • Effective UID/GID (eUID/eGID) — determines the permissions the process runs with. SUID or SGID makes it run as the file's owner or group rather than the launcher.
  • Saved UID/GID — used by the kernel so the process can revert to its original ID after temporarily assuming different privileges.

Read that as a small state machine: a process starts as its real user, may climb to a powerful effective identity thanks to SUID or SGID, and keeps the saved ID so it can climb back down.

Root: the all-powerful user

Root has UID and GID of 0. Root can access any file, become any user, and do things only root may do, such as starting certain communication processes. The root userid is typically made available to the system administrator in systems with discretionary access control. The sudo command runs a command as root; it works only if the invoking user is in the sudo group and usually requires a password. Non-sudo users can still run some commands via sudo if the sudoers file permits them.

⚠️
Common trap: SUID is a privilege-escalation risk by design. A program that runs as root while processing attacker-controlled input turns a small bug into full system compromise. That is why SUID binaries are few and audited, and why /tmp uses the sticky bit so strangers cannot delete each other's files.
🤔

Access Control in Other Operating Systems

macOS, Windows, iOS and Android reuse the same core ideas, then trade them off differently.

macOS

macOS is based on the FreeBSD version of Unix, so it has similar permissions. The BSD layer provides memory protection: applications cannot access system memory (or each other's) unless running with advanced permissions, which is why you can Force Quit a wedged application without rebooting. Files have attributes and ACLs. It also has the Keychain, storing extra passwords and some ACLs, and built-in network authentication such as Kerberos. The default installation has the root account disabled, but administrators belong to a group called wheel that lets them su to root; installing programs asks for the root password. Since version 10.5 (Leopard), macOS has been based on TrustedBSD, a BSD variant incorporating mandatory access control to protect core system components against tampering by malware.

Windows

Windows is powerful and shares similarities with Unix. Instead of RWX permissions it features about thirteen kinds of permissions (the notes give that figure; the slides say 15 to 16), such as Traverse Folder/Execute File, List Folder/Read Data and Read Attributes. This complexity can lead to errors despite secure default settings. Windows enforces access via a Security Reference Monitor (SRM), a trusted component providing fine-grained security.

Apple iOS

iOS is based on Unix. Apps have many user permissions, often for privacy, and request a capability to access device services such as the mobile network, phone, SMSes and camera the first time they attempt to use them; it is granted if the user consents. The many services open possible side-channel attacks: an app denied keyboard access could deduce keypresses using the accelerometer and gyro. Sensor values are read-only, reflecting principles similar to the Biba model. iOS uses sandboxing so apps cannot access other apps' files, grants capabilities through APIs, runs apps in non-privileged mode, keeps the OS read-only to apps, and implements hardware support, signed software and encryption.

Android

Android is based on Linux, and apps from different vendors run under different userids. Linux mechanisms control file-level access, preventing one app reading another's data and preventing apps exhausting shared resources such as memory and CPU. As in iOS, app permissions are in effect capabilities granting access to services such as SMSes, the camera and the address book. Android supports many hardware platforms, but a concession is that developers cannot assume uniform features across devices. Apps are signed .apk packages; while iOS apps are signed by Apple, Android verification keys are self-signed certificates acting as the developer's name, supporting integrity of updates while keeping an open ecosystem.

  • Each package has a manifest demanding permissions, and users must approve the 'dangerous' ones, roughly those that can spend money or compromise personal data.
  • Early Android required approving everything at installation or not running the app. Users clicked through, so even flashlight apps demanded the address book to sell. Android 6 moved to the Apple model of trust on first use; apps built for earlier versions still demand capabilities at installation.
  • Storage is a well-known problem: app-internal and 'public storage'. Developers often misuse public storage because it is easier, since it allows write access to everyone — but everyone can read it too.
  • The API is more permissive; apps can send SMS messages on your behalf. The Google Play Store is less tightly controlled than Apple's, but apps are still screened for malware.

Middleware Access Control

Middleware is a rich but risky place to enforce access control, because it is complex, exposed and easy to misconfigure.

Middleware, on one host or distributed, covers message brokering (for example AMQP) and Remote Procedure Calls including Java RMI, CORBA and XML-RPC. These components are often exposed to probing by external entities, increasing risk. Managing them is substantial: you must define access granularity precisely, monitor and enforce permissions meticulously, and face configuration challenges. Policy languages exist, but are hard to use effectively. One mitigation is to protect middleware from external access, which guards against random remote exploitation — though malware originating inside a participating system can still try to exploit it.

Database access control

Database Management Systems (DBMSes) supporting multiple users and processes have their own access control. The data often resides on disk or in RAM, so OS protections remain essential. Connecting typically requires a username and password, which makes internal privilege management important, including mapping users on the OS to roles within the database (role-based access control). The SQL standard defines fine-grained methods, theoretically letting any kind of object be controlled, though this can overcomplicate a system. DBMSes also provide integrity controls, crucial for accuracy but computationally intensive to verify.

Browsers

The web browser is another middleware platform we rely on for access control, and whose complexity often lets us down. Its main rule is the same-origin policy: JavaScript or other active content on a page may only communicate with the IP address it originally came from, and it runs in a sandbox to prevent it altering the host system.

We should probably have treated browsers as access control devices all along: the browser is where you run code written by people you do not trust, who are occasionally malicious. Mobile OSes handle this by running different apps as different users; even without malice, you do not want to reboot your browser because a script in one tab hangs, which is why Chrome runs each tab in a separate operating-system process.

Browser bugs are exploited in drive-by download attacks, where visiting a page can infect your machine. The modern web is also full of trackers from ad networks and data brokers that make a mockery of the same-origin policy's intent, and attackers can launder origin by mashing up a target site with evil scripts and having the victim view it through a proxy such as Google Translate. A prudent person types their bank URL or uses a bookmark, but marketing trains everyone to click links in email.

The confused deputy problem

Confused deputy problem — a privileged program (the "deputy") is tricked by a less-privileged entity into misusing its authority on the attacker's behalf. The deputy is "confused" about who the real requester is and misattributes its authority.

In browsers this happens through Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), where a malicious script is injected into a trusted site. First, malicious script injection: a user visits a trusted site with an XSS vulnerability, and the attacker injects malicious JavaScript. Second, executing malicious actions: the script runs in the browser with the website's permissions. Third, exploiting trust: the script performs actions on the user's behalf, such as authenticated requests, because the browser believes they originate from the trusted site. Subcomponents such as scripting, add-ons and cookies are especially vulnerable. Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF), covered later, is a typical example.

⚠️
Common trap: the confused deputy is not a broken program; it is too helpful. It holds real authority and can be induced to spend it for someone else. The fix is to check the authority of the requesting principal, not just the deputy's own rights.

Sandboxing, Virtualisation and Confinement

When you cannot trust code, confine it: sandboxes limit what it touches, and virtual machines raise the cost of escape.

Two strategies let a system run untrusted code without trusting it: a sandbox, or a virtual machine. Both are forms of confinement.

Sandboxing

Sandboxing — creating a shielded environment where malicious applications cannot interfere with the surrounding system. It usually offers a limited API, striking a balance between functionality and security.

Java applets, now largely obsolete, are a historical example. A sandbox gives code no access to the local hard disk (or at most temporary access to a restricted directory) and allows it to communicate only with the host it came from — the same-origin policy again. Other examples are JavaScript in browsers and Chrome's approach of isolating tabs in separate processes. Securing a sandbox is genuinely hard, and there are methods to break out of it, so confinement is layered rather than relied on alone.

Virtualisation

Virtual Machines (VMs) emulate a computer system. Virtualisation was invented in the 1960s by IBM; a single machine could be partitioned with VM/370 into multiple virtual machines. VMs let software run even when its original environment is unavailable, because the OS inside perceives only its designated "own" hardware. On the client end they let you run a guest OS on a host, for example Windows on macOS. The degrees are full virtualisation (complete hardware emulation, even across architectures), para-virtualisation (the guest OS must support it), and containers and jails (lightweight virtualisation giving an isolated environment).

A hypervisor manages and runs VMs with hardware support at the ring -1 level; examples are VirtualBox, VMware, Xen and QEMU. Virtualisation is not primarily designed for access control, but the significant effort required to break out raises the bar for attackers. Caveats: VM execution is detectable, because a VM does not perfectly imitate a real environment, so malware can tell it is inside one and act differently; hypervisors themselves have vulnerabilities. Keep hypervisor software up to date and never use it as the sole defence.

Example: Docker

Docker was initially developed for continuous integration and delivery. It abstracts over containers, supporting operating systems through their native jail implementations, and builds images packaging applications with their supporting libraries, which makes it efficient and resource-friendly. Containers spin up or delete quickly, and management systems such as Kubernetes simplify orchestration. Docker containers sandbox the app, preventing direct communication between apps in different containers. The sharp edge: the Docker daemon acts as broker but runs with root privileges, so any user who can start or stop containers can interact with it, expanding the attack surface.

🧠
Remember: sandboxes and VMs are speed bumps, not walls. They raise the cost of an attack and buy time, but each has known escape routes and detection tricks. Use them as part of a layered defence, never as the only line.

Hardware Access Control

Hardware is the bottom of the trust stack: it keeps processes apart and enforces privilege in silicon.

Software access control rests on hardware support, grouped into memory protection, privileged execution and isolated cryptographic components.

Memory protection

CPUs help the OS keep processes' memory allocations apart, primarily through segment addressing: memory is addressed using a segment register that identifies a segment and an address register that pinpoints a location within it. If a process accesses memory outside its address space, a segmentation fault (SIGSEGV) is triggered, protecting against unauthorised read and write operations.

Privileged execution and cryptographic isolation

CPUs have layered modes, or rings, organised into distinct privilege classes that delineate the operations permissible in each. ARM goes further with separate registers for each privilege class. Storing cryptographic keys in hardware restricts access to them, allowing only authorised functionality to use them through a dedicated interface: an example is the Intel Trusted Platform Module (TPM), initially conceived for Digital Rights Management.

Kernel, rings and least privilege

Code runs in one of the available CPU modes, called rings, which are hierarchical levels of privilege. CPUs enforce strict access control, preventing processes in higher-privileged rings from directly interacting with those in lower rings. This embodies the principle of least privilege: all code should run only with the minimal required privilege, never more, and the number of ways to communicate with the kernel should be limited to the required minimum and well controlled. A protection domain is this idea made concrete: the set of objects and rights a piece of code may touch. A ring is a hardware protection domain; a sandbox is a software one.

Intel processors

  • The Intel 80286 introduced segment addressing and protection rings. The 80386 added built-in virtual memory and expanded memory segments to 4 GB.
  • The current privilege level can only be changed by a process in ring 0, the kernel. Procedures cannot access objects in lower-level rings directly, but gates allow execution at a different privilege level and manage infrastructure such as multiple stack segments.
  • Modern CPUs have nine rings: rings 0 to 3 for normal code, a further 0 to 3 in VMM root mode for the hypervisor, and System Management Mode (SMM) for the BIOS. In practice the four levels used are SMM, ring 0 of VMX root mode, normal ring 0 for the OS, and ring 3 for applications.
Ring 3 · Applications→ Ring 0 · OS kernel→ Ring −1 · Hypervisor→ SMM · BIOS

ARM processors and CHERI

ARM is the processor core most used in phones, tablets and IoT devices. Unlike Intel, it licenses cores that chip designers include in their products. The core initially had no memory management, so ARM designs could have their hardware protection extensively customised; there are now variants with memory protection units (MPUs) and others with memory management units (MMUs) handling virtual memory. ARM's latest offering is CHERI (Capability Hardware Enhanced RISC Instructions), adding fine-grained capability support so a process spawning a subthread can allocate it read and write access to specific memory ranges, letting multiple sandboxes run in the same process. Used thoroughly in operating systems such as Windows, Android or iOS, it has the potential to prevent most of the zero-day exploits of recent years.

Multilevel Security, Labels and Clearances

Multilevel security labels every object and every person, then lets the labels, not the users, decide.

Multilevel security (MLS) — a foundational access control model that handles information across different levels of classification, such as Top Secret, Secret and Confidential. Every object carries a classification label, every subject holds a clearance, and the relationship between them decides access.

MLS holds enormous importance in military and government. Yet implementing it while maintaining high utility is surprisingly challenging. The lecture's example shows why: how can a system provide automated means to reduce a document's classification from Top Secret to Secret, so that dependent principals at a lower classification can read it? Lowering a label is deliberate and hard to automate, and if you cannot do it, work grinds to a halt.

In general, labels can be arranged into an ordering or lattice, where one label dominates another. A subject's clearance dominates an object's classification exactly when access is allowed: Top Secret dominates Secret, which dominates Confidential, which dominates Unclassified.

Homer also cannot enlist Marge, because she cannot write down, so she cannot copy the file to him. Bart can change all the codes, since with no clearance he can write up. This extreme example shows Bell-LaPadula focuses only on confidentiality, not integrity.

Under Biba. Homer can obtain the TV lock codes, because read up is allowed, and Marge can change all the codes, because write down is allowed. The same scenario gives opposite answers depending on which property you protect.

ℹ️
Context: keep the two pairs straight, because exam questions love them. Bell-LaPadula cares about secrets leaking down. Biba cares about corrupt data flowing up. Protecting one does not automatically protect the other.

The Bell-LaPadula Model

Bell-LaPadula stops secrets leaking downwards: no read up, no write down.

The classic multilevel security policy model that gained wide acceptance was proposed in 1973. In the 1970s came a growing recognition that operating systems would always contain vulnerabilities that even users could work around, underscoring the need for mandatory access control that could not be easily bypassed. Its basic property is that information cannot flow downwards. Formally it enforces two properties, both forms of MAC, plus one discretionary form.

Bell-LaPadula · confidentiality
Simple Security: no read up · ∗-property: no write down

The simple security property says no subject may read data at a higher level (no read up); implicitly, a subject may read at their own level or down. The ∗ (star) property says no subject may write data to a lower level (no write down); implicitly, a subject may write at their own level or up.

The star property is crucial. A low-level user wanting to exfiltrate high-level data writes a program, then waits for an administrator or higher-level user to accidentally execute it. If they do, it runs with higher privilege and could copy secrets down. The ∗ property prevents higher-level entities writing to lower-level objects, blocking this indirect exfiltration. Bell-LaPadula also has one discretionary form: an external entity defines subjects' access to objects in a matrix, for example Sam (read, append, ∅, read) and Bert (∅, read-write, append, execute) across objects o1 to o4.

Strengths and criticisms

Innovations

  • Blends well with formal analysis
  • Combines DAC and MAC

Criticisms

  • Administration via one omnipotent Trusted Principal — how do you secure that?
  • Defined with confidentiality in mind, not integrity
  • Does not consider covert channels
  • A system of two Bell-LaPadula-secure systems is not necessarily secure

The covert channel criticism is best seen with an example: one program encodes a 1 as high CPU load and a 0 as low CPU load, and a lower-level program estimates the load to read off the bits. No ordinary write down occurred, yet information crossed the boundary.

⚠️
Common trap: a covert channel is a method used to pass information that is not normally used for communication, such as hiding a message in images (steganography) or in CPU load. A side channel is different: the attacker infers what is inside a protected communication by observing artefacts such as packet lengths, timing or CPU heat, without changing the message.
🤔
Pause & predict: A Secret file is encrypted and sent over the internet — is that a covert channel? Now suppose the sender varies packet timing to encode bits. Is that a covert channel?

The Biba Model

Biba is Bell-LaPadula flipped: it protects integrity instead of confidentiality.

The Biba model focuses on data integrity rather than confidentiality, aiming to prevent malicious modifications. It can be seen as a reverse of Bell-LaPadula. Its three core properties are: the simple integrity property (a subject may only read data at their own level or higher — no read down), the ∗ integrity property (a subject may only write data at their own level or lower — no write up), and the invocation property (a process from below cannot request higher access; it may only invoke subjects at an equal or lower level).

The invocation property is the one students skip. Usually a subject means any user, program or process. Biba's first two rules use the word subject; the third uses processes. While the simple and star integrity properties control direct read and write access to data, the invocation property controls the ability of processes to call or execute other processes. It stops a low-integrity process invoking a high-integrity process to act on its behalf.

Two everyday examples

  • An aircraft's in-flight entertainment system can read avionics data such as airspeed but cannot alter it, so a vulnerability in the entertainment system cannot compromise flight controls.
  • An odometer can display mileage without the capability to alter it, ensuring the integrity of the mileage data.

In both cases a low-integrity, easily attacked component may read trusted data but may never write it — the mirror image of Bell-LaPadula.

Pure Bell-LaPadula, or any single security model, is rare in practice. However, variants of Biba, emphasising data integrity, find more application: Windows Vista, certain UNIX systems such as SELinux, some versions of Red Hat, and a FreeBSD module. Even where models are not implemented purely, their concepts are crucial for understanding and discussing real-world access control, which is why the rest of the lecture focuses on mobile, desktop and server operating systems and hardware.

Roles, Attributes and Least Privilege

Organisations rarely grant access to individuals; they grant it to roles, match attributes, and give everyone the least privilege that works.

Role-based access control (RBAC)

Role-based access control (RBAC) — permissions are attached to roles rather than individual identities. Users are assigned roles and gain exactly the permissions their roles carry. The lecture meets it in databases, where DBMSes map users on the OS to roles in the database.

RBAC scales because you manage a handful of roles rather than thousands of individuals: the "Database Administrator" has more privileges than the "Database Manager". Three features make it practical: a role hierarchy, where senior roles inherit the permissions of roles beneath them so each permission is defined once; constraints, rules restricting how roles combine, such as a maximum number of users in a powerful role; and separation of duties, splitting a sensitive process across roles so no single person can complete it alone. The classic case is payments: whoever creates a payment must not also approve it.

Attribute-based access control (ABAC)

Attribute-based access control (ABAC) — access decisions evaluate attributes of the subject, object, operation and environment against a policy, rather than relying on identity or a fixed role alone.

ABAC is the fine-grained end of the spectrum that began with coarse read/write/execute rights. A policy might allow access when the subject's department is Finance and the object is Internal and the operation is read and the environment is a managed device during business hours. Every attribute can be tested, so ABAC expresses very precise rules at the cost of more complexity to design and audit — the same trade-off the lecture flags for SQL's fine-grained access control.

Least privilege and privilege escalation

A privilege is authority to perform an action ordinary users cannot. The principle of least privilege says all code should run only with the minimal required privilege, never more. It shapes every choice above: give a role only what it needs, run a process in its own protection domain, and limit the kernel's communication paths. Privilege escalation is gaining more privilege than intended; watch for SUID and SGID programs that mishandle input, the confused deputy being tricked into spending its authority, sandbox and VM escape reaching the surrounding system, and root and sudo, where control of sudo membership and the sudoers file matters as much as any password. All point to one design idea: a protection domain. A process should live in the smallest domain that lets it do its job and cross into a more privileged domain only through a narrow, controlled gate.

💡
Why it matters: least privilege is the cheapest security control you can apply. It costs nothing at run time and shrinks the blast radius of every mistake. Most privilege escalation is not a broken model but a system granted more than it needed.

Key Takeaways

If you remember nothing else from this week, hold onto these twelve points.

1

Access control decides a triple — a subject performing an operation on an object — to protect resources and assets as part of a security policy.

2

Authentication proves identity; authorization allocates or verifies privilege; access control enforces the outcome. The pipeline is identify, authenticate, authorize, access.

3

The access control matrix has subjects as rows, objects as columns and rights in cells. ACLs are its columns; capability lists are its rows.

4

Policy says what is allowed; mechanisms enforce it and provide assurance. A reference monitor must mediate completely, be tamper-proof, and be verifiable.

5

DAC lets owners decide and transfer privileges (flexible, hard at scale). MAC imposes labels from a central authority that even root cannot override.

6

Unix permissions are nine bits: read, write and execute for owner, group and others, shown symbolically (rwxr-xr-x) or in octal (755), where r = 4, w = 2, x = 1.

7

SUID, SGID and the sticky bit change program identity or protect shared directories such as /tmp. Processes carry real, effective and saved UIDs/GIDs.

8

Root has UID 0 and can do anything; sudo grants root power to the sudo group or to commands in the sudoers file.

9

Mobile systems confine apps by construction: iOS uses signed software, capabilities, read-only sensors and sandboxing; Android runs apps as separate users with manifest permissions, though public storage is a weakness.

10

Middleware, databases and browsers have their own access control. The browser's same-origin policy runs code in a sandbox, but the confused deputy problem, XSS and CSRF turn a helpful privileged program against its owner.

11

Sandboxes and virtual machines are confinement, not guaranteed isolation. They can be broken out of and detected, and hypervisors have vulnerabilities, so use them as one layer.

12

Bell-LaPadula protects confidentiality (no read up, no write down); Biba protects integrity (no read down, no write up, plus invocation).

Glossary

Thirty terms from Week 3, grouped so you can filter to what you need.

Access control
The element of a security policy controlling which principals may access which resources. Specified in the policy, implemented by mechanisms.
Subject / principal
An active entity that can request access: a user, group, process or machine. The matrix rows.
Object
A passive thing that is accessed: a file, process, port, record or device. The matrix columns.
Authentication
Acquiring evidence of identity, such as a password or fingerprint. Answers "who are you?".
Authorization
Allocating a privilege to a party (notes), or verifying whether an entity is allowed an action (slides).
Least privilege
All code should run only with the minimal required privilege, and kernel communication paths should be minimised.
Role-based access control (RBAC)
Permissions attached to roles rather than identities; users gain permissions by assignment. Used to map OS users to database roles.
Attribute-based access control (ABAC)
Decisions evaluate attributes of subject, object, operation and environment against a policy, allowing context-aware rules.
Access control matrix
A table with subjects as rows, objects as columns and rights in the cells (r, w, x, -).
Access control list (ACL)
A list attached to an object giving each subject's permitted operations, coarse or fine-grained.
Capability list
The subject-side view: a list of objects each subject may access. Holding the capability grants access.
Discretionary access control (DAC)
The owner decides who may access an object and may transfer privileges. Flexible and identity-based, hard at scale.
Mandatory access control (MAC)
An external administrator defines access; a security kernel enforces it using security attributes. No user can override it.
Reference monitor
The trusted gatekeeper validating every access. It must mediate completely, be tamper-proof and be verifiable.
Multilevel security (MLS)
A foundational model for information at classification levels (Top Secret, Secret, Confidential), central to military and government systems.
Bell-LaPadula model
A 1973 model protecting confidentiality: no read up (simple security) and no write down (star property), plus a discretionary matrix.
Star (∗) property
The Bell-LaPadula rule of no write down. It blocks indirect exfiltration by higher-level entities.
Biba model
A model protecting integrity: no read down, no write up, plus the invocation property. Variants appear in Vista, SELinux, Red Hat and FreeBSD.
Unix file permissions
Nine bits giving r, w, x rights to owner, group and others, shown symbolically (rwxr-xr-x) or in octal (755).
SUID (set-user-ID)
A file bit making a program run with its owner's privilege rather than the invoking user. A privilege-escalation risk.
SGID (set-group-ID)
On a file, execute as the owning group; on a directory, new files inherit the parent directory's group.
Sticky bit
A directory setting so only a file's owner (and root) can remove it. Used on /tmp.
sudo
Lets a user run a command as root. Needs sudo-group membership and usually a password; the sudoers file can allow specific commands.
Same-origin policy
The browser's main rule: active content may only communicate with the IP address it originally came from, and runs in a sandbox.
Confused deputy problem
When a privileged program is tricked into misusing its authority on an attacker's behalf, as in browser cross-site scripting (XSS).
Sandboxing
Running code in a shielded environment with a limited API. Examples: Java applets, browser JavaScript, Chrome's per-tab processes.
Protection domain
The set of objects and rights within which code runs. A ring is a hardware domain; a sandbox is a software one.
Ring
A layered CPU privilege mode. Modern Intel CPUs have nine: rings 0 to 3, VMM root mode 0 to 3, and System Management Mode.
Trusted Platform Module (TPM)
An isolated cryptographic component storing keys in hardware and exposing only authorised functionality. Originally for DRM.
CHERI
Capability Hardware Enhanced RISC Instructions: an ARM technology giving subthreads fine-grained access to specific memory ranges.

No terms in this category.

Practice Questions

Fifteen open-ended questions covering the whole week. Answer aloud first, then reveal.

Answer, reveal, then tick “Mastered”. Your progress is saved.
Q1

Define access control and give two contrasting examples. How does it differ from authentication and authorization?

Answer: Access control controls which principals may reach which resources, such as whether a bank customer can access the vault or a program can read other users' passwords. It is specified in the policy, counters the attacker's incentives, and is implemented by mechanisms giving a certain assurance. Authentication acquires evidence of identity; authorization allocates a privilege or verifies allowed actions.
Q2

Draw the lecture's naive access control matrix for Sam, Alice and Bob. Explain one cell per person in terms of their job.

Answer: Rows are users; columns are Operating System, Accounts Program, Accounting Data and Audit Trail; cells hold r, w, x or -. Sam the sysadmin has rwx on the OS and accounts program, rw on accounting data and only r on the audit trail, because even he should only read it. Alice the manager can x the OS and accounts program but not write them, has rw on accounting data and no audit-trail access. Bob the auditor has r or rx everywhere.
Q3

Distinguish an access control policy from a mechanism. What is a reference monitor, and what three properties must it have?

Answer: A policy states the goals and rules; a mechanism is the concrete control enforcing them. A reference monitor is the trusted component mediating access requests between subjects and objects. It must provide complete mediation (every access is checked, with no bypass), be tamper-proof, and be verifiable. MAC implements it as a security kernel; Windows calls its implementation the Security Reference Monitor.
Q4

Contrast discretionary and mandatory access control, using Microsoft OneDrive to illustrate both.

Answer: In DAC, access depends on the identity of the object's owner, who can transfer privileges unless constrained; it is flexible but hard at scale. In MAC, an external security policy administrator defines access and a security kernel enforces it via security attributes, with even the supervisor under remote control. In OneDrive, DAC appears in personal use (creator owns the file, can transfer ownership, and share privately, with specific people, by link, or publicly), while MAC appears in the enterprise through admin sharing policies and Data Loss Prevention.
Q5

Explain what an ACL is. How does a capability list differ, and when would you prefer each?

Answer: An ACL is a list attached to an object defining which subjects may access it and with what operations; an entry pairs a user ID with a mapping to operations, coarse or very fine-grained. ACLs are common in all UNIX systems (POSIX), exist in Windows, and are used in social networking, content management and enterprise resource management. A capability list is the subject-side complement: each subject holds a list of objects it may access. ACLs are matrix columns, capabilities are rows.
Q6

A file shows -rw-r--r--. Who can do what? Convert rwxr-xr-x to octal and explain the arithmetic.

Answer: The leading - marks a regular file. The triple rw- gives the owner read and write but not execute; r-- gives the group read only; r-- gives others read only. So all three audiences can read, but only the owner can write. For octal, r = 4, w = 2 and x = 1: rwx = 7, r-x = 5, r-x = 5, so rwxr-xr-x is 755. By contrast rwxrwxrwx (777) lets everyone read, write and execute.
Q7

Describe SUID, SGID and the sticky bit. Which one protects files in /tmp, and how?

Answer: SUID lets an owner mark a program to run with the privilege of its owner rather than the invoking user; it is useful for privileged programs such as mount. SGID does the same as the file's group, and on a directory makes new files and subdirectories inherit the parent directory's group. The sticky bit does not affect individual files: at the directory level it restricts deletion so only the file's owner (and root) can remove it, which is why it is used on /tmp.
Q8

What are the real, effective and saved UIDs/GIDs of a process? Explain what root and sudo allow.

Answer: Processes inherit permissions from the running user through three IDs. The real UID/GID identify the actual user and group that initiated the process. The effective UID/GID determine the permissions it runs with; SUID or SGID makes it run as the file's owner or group instead of the launcher. The saved UID/GID let the kernel restore the original identity afterwards. Root has UID and GID 0 and can access any file, become any user, and do things only root may do. sudo runs a command as root, needing sudo-group membership and usually a password, though the sudoers file can allow specific commands for non-sudo users.
Q9

Mobile OSes use sandboxing for apps. Which are features of that sandboxing: (a) each app runs as a separate user, (b) apps communicate only via controlled APIs, (c) the OS is read-only to an app, (d) apps can access any part of main storage?

Answer: (a), (b) and (c) are correct; (d) is incorrect. Each app running as a separate user, communicating only through controlled APIs, and seeing the OS as read-only are features of mobile sandboxing, analogous to user permissions in Linux. Free access to any part of main storage would defeat the isolation: iOS sandboxing stops apps accessing other apps' files, and Android's Linux mechanisms stop one app reading another's data.
Q10

Describe the Android storage problem and how Android's permission model changed over time.

Answer: Android runs apps from different vendors under different userids. Its storage has two forms: app-internal and 'public storage'. Developers often misuse public storage because it is easier, since it allows write access to everyone — but everyone can read it too. Apps come in signed .apk packages whose self-signed certificates act as the developer's name, and each package has a manifest demanding permissions; users must approve the 'dangerous' ones. Early Android required approving everything at installation and users clicked through; Android 6 moved to the Apple model of trust on first use.
Q11

Why does the lecture call the browser an access control device? Explain the same-origin policy and two ways its intent is undermined.

Answer: The browser is where you run code written by people you do not trust and who are occasionally malicious, so its job is fundamentally about mediating access. The same-origin policy is its main rule: JavaScript or other active content may only communicate with the IP address it originally came from, and it runs in a sandbox so it cannot alter the host system. Its intent is undermined first by bugs exploited in drive-by download attacks, where merely visiting a page can infect a machine, and second by trackers from ad networks and data brokers; attackers can also launder origin through a proxy such as Google Translate.
Q12

What is the confused deputy problem? Show how it plays out through cross-site scripting in a browser.

Answer: The confused deputy problem occurs when a privileged program (the deputy) is tricked by a less-privileged entity into misusing its authority on the attacker's behalf; it misattributes its authority because it is confused about the real requester. Through XSS: first, malicious script injection — a user visits a trusted site with an XSS vulnerability and the attacker injects malicious JavaScript; second, executing malicious actions — the script runs with the website's permissions; third, exploiting trust — it performs actions on the user's behalf, such as authenticated requests, because the browser believes they come from the trusted site. Subcomponents such as scripting, add-ons and cookies are especially vulnerable; CSRF is a typical example.
Q13

Compare sandboxing and virtualisation as access control. Use Docker as your example and name the risks of each.

Answer: Sandboxing creates a shielded environment where malicious applications cannot interfere with the surrounding system, offering a limited API; examples are Java applets, browser JavaScript, and Chrome running each tab in a separate OS process. Its weakness is that the sandbox is hard to secure and can be broken out of. Virtualisation emulates a computer system (invented by IBM in the 1960s with VM/370) in degrees: full virtualisation (complete hardware emulation, even across architectures), para-virtualisation (the guest OS must support it), and containers and jails (lightweight). A hypervisor manages VMs with hardware support at ring -1, for example VirtualBox, VMware, Xen and QEMU. Breaking out is hard, raising the bar, though VM execution is detectable and hypervisors have vulnerabilities, so never rely on them alone. Docker packages apps and libraries into images and is orchestrated by Kubernetes; containers stop apps communicating directly, but the Docker daemon runs as root, so anyone who can start or stop containers can interact with it, expanding the attack surface.
Q14

State the Bell-LaPadula and Biba properties. Why is the star property crucial for Bell-LaPadula, and what is a covert channel?

Answer: Bell-LaPadula protects confidentiality with the simple security property (no read up) and the ∗ (star) property (no write down), plus one discretionary access matrix. Biba protects integrity with the simple integrity property (no read down), the ∗ integrity property (no write up) and the invocation property (a process from below cannot request higher access). The star property blocks indirect exfiltration: a low-level user might write a program to copy secrets down and wait for a high-level administrator to accidentally execute it, but no write down prevents high-level entities writing to lower-level objects. A covert channel is a method of passing information not normally used for communication, such as encoding data in CPU load or images; a side channel instead infers content by observing artefacts such as timing or heat.
Q15

Using the clearance example — Bart (None), Homer (Low), Lisa (Medium), Marge (Ultimate), with fridgecodes needing Ultimate, tvlockcodes Medium and playstationcodes Low — answer under both models: can Homer read the TV codes? Can Bart change the fridge codes? Can Homer enlist Marge to fetch the TV codes?

Answer: Under Bell-LaPadula: Homer cannot read the TV lock codes, because no read up forbids reading a Medium object with Low clearance. Homer cannot enlist Marge, because no write down stops her copying the file down to him. Bart can change all the codes, including the fridge codes, because no write down lets a lower-clearance subject write up — showing BLP protects confidentiality, not integrity. Under Biba: Homer can obtain the TV lock codes, because read up is allowed, and Marge can change all the codes, because write down is allowed.
