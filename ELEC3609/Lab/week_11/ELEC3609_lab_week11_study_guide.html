Project Help & Review: Turning the Final Lab into Assignment 2 Momentum

Week 11 has no in-person project demonstration. Instead the lab is a working review clinic where teams check their Assignment 2 progress and get tutor guidance before submission — and start preparing for the Week 12 technical interview.

Read this first: Treat the Week 11 lab as the last cheap chance to find problems while they are still fixable. Nothing here is marked, so the only thing you can lose is the opportunity — come with code, a report draft, and questions you have already tried to answer yourself.

What the Week 11 Session Actually Is

Week 11 is a support and review clinic — there is no in-person project demonstration, so the value you get equals the value you prepare for.

There is no in-person project demonstration in this lab. If you expected to stand up and show your project, that expectation is wrong for Week 11. The session is reserved for teams to review their Assignment 2 work and to ask tutors for help before submission.

That single fact changes how you should treat the hour. A demonstration rewards performance; a review clinic rewards preparation. You are not being watched for marks — you are being given access to experienced eyes while your work can still change.

💡
Why it matters: The session sits just before Assignment 2 submission. After it, feedback gets more expensive because every fix must fit inside the remaining time. Finding a gap now costs minutes; finding it after submission costs marks.
🤔
Pause & predict: Before reading on, write the one question about your Assignment 2 that you would most regret not asking a tutor. Keep it — you will refine it later.

The Five Jobs of the Session

Lab time has five legitimate uses; if your plan does not map onto one of them, you are probably about to waste the session.

Use the lab time to:

  1. clarify the Assignment 2 requirements and the submission process;
  2. ask questions about implementation, testing, documentation, or the recorded demonstration video;
  3. troubleshoot remaining technical issues with guidance from a tutor;
  4. review whether your report and video provide sufficient evidence for the features you implemented;
  5. prepare for the Week 12 technical interview.

Each job needs different input from you. Job 1 needs the requirements document open. Job 2 needs a specific question. Job 3 needs a reproducible failure. Job 4 needs the report and video in front of you. Job 5 needs a list of likely interview questions.

Clarify

Requirements and submission process. Bring the official requirements text so you can point at the clause you are unsure about.

Question

Implementation, testing, documentation, video. Bring one precise question per topic rather than a general worry.

Troubleshoot

Remaining technical issues. Bring a minimal reproduction and the exact error.

Evidence review

Do the report and video prove each feature? Bring the drafts and a feature list.

Interview prep

Week 12 technical interview. Bring practice questions and your own explanations.

✅
Exam tip: The Week 12 technical interview is the next assessment after submission. A question you ask in Week 11 about your own design is free rehearsal for it.

Come Prepared: The Three Things to Bring

Walk in with current source code, a report draft and a written list of specific questions — teams that bring these get concrete help instead of generic advice.

The session expects a specific kit:

  • your current source code;
  • your report draft;
  • a list of specific questions.

The word "current" matters. Bring the code as it is today, not the version you remember or plan to write. A tutor can only diagnose what you actually have. Likewise, an outline is not a report draft — bring prose, even if it is rough.

Why a written question list beats improvising

A written list forces you to commit. When you write a question down, you usually discover half the answer yourself, which sharpens what remains into a genuinely hard question worth a tutor's time.

🧠
Memory hook: Code, draft, questions — C·D·Q. If you cannot carry all three into the room, you are not ready to use the session.
🤔
Pause & predict: Which of the three items is your team weakest on right now? Name the gap before the session, not during it.

What Tutors Will and Will Not Do

Tutors give guidance and feedback; they will not complete the assignment or pre-mark it, and the session is not an assessed presentation.

Tutors can provide guidance and feedback. That is the whole offer, and it is valuable — expert direction while you still own the work.

The limits are just as important:

  • the session is not an assessed presentation — nothing you say or show here is graded;
  • tutors will not complete the assignment for you;
  • tutors will not pre-mark the assignment for you.

Tutors will

  • Explain requirements and the submission process.
  • Answer questions on implementation, testing, documentation and the video.
  • Guide your troubleshooting of technical issues.
  • Give feedback on whether your evidence is sufficient.
  • Help you prepare for the Week 12 technical interview.

Tutors will not

  • Write or finish your code for you.
  • Tell you what mark you would receive.
  • Grade or pre-mark your report or video.
  • Replace your own reading of the requirements.
  • Treat the session as an assessed presentation.
⚠️
Common trap: Asking "is this good enough?" invites a vague answer. Asking "does this paragraph evidence feature X strongly enough, or should I add the test output?" invites a useful one.

Know the Requirements and the Deadline

For deliverables and the submission deadline, the Assignment 2 requirements are the authority — the session only helps you interpret them.

The session will not restate your deliverables or your deadline. For both, refer to the Assignment 2 requirements. That document is the single source of truth for:

  • what you must submit;
  • how you must submit it;
  • when it is due.

How to use the requirements in the session

  1. Open the requirements before you arrive and read them end to end.
  2. Mark every clause you cannot confidently act on.
  3. Turn each mark into a question for the tutor.
  4. After the session, update your plan so each clause has an owner and a due date.
ℹ️
Note: Deadlines and deliverables change between offerings. Do not rely on a friend's memory or last year's version — check the current requirements for this offering.
🤔
Pause & predict: How many deliverable items can you name from memory right now? If the number is uncertain, that uncertainty is your first question.

Run a Team Self-Review Before You Ask

Review your own Assignment 2 work first, so the session is spent on the genuinely hard questions rather than things the team could have answered itself.

One listed use of lab time is to review your Assignment 2 work. Review is something the team does; the tutor helps where review stalls. Do the cheap passes yourselves.

  1. Re-read the Assignment 2 requirements and list every required deliverable.
  2. Map each requirement to where it is satisfied in your code, report or video.
  3. Mark each mapping as done, partial or missing.
  4. For every partial or missing item, attempt a fix or write down why it is blocked.
  5. Bring only the blocked items and the hard judgement calls to the tutor.

A short self-review turns a diffuse worry into a tight, high-value agenda. It also protects you from the most common waste: spending the session on something you could have resolved by reading.

🧠
Memory hook: Review → map → mark → attempt → ask. Only the last step needs a tutor.

Does Your Evidence Prove Your Features?

A feature exists for assessment purposes only if your report and recorded demonstration video provide sufficient evidence that you implemented it.

One of the five jobs is to review whether your report and video provide sufficient evidence for the features you implemented. This is a subtle and high-stakes point: implementation and demonstrated implementation are not the same thing.

Ask, for every feature:

  • Does the report explain what the feature does and why it was built that way?
  • Does the report show how it was tested, and what the result was?
  • Does the recorded demonstration video show the feature working?
  • Could a marker who has never met you confirm the feature from the evidence alone?

If any answer is no, the gap is not in your code — it is in your evidence. Evidence gaps are usually faster to close than code gaps, which makes this one of the best uses of the session.

⚠️
Common trap: A feature that works but is never shown or explained reads, to a marker, like a feature that does not exist. Show it and write it down.
🤔
Pause & predict: Pick your best feature. Could a stranger verify it from your report and video alone? If not, what single piece of evidence is missing?

Troubleshooting with a Tutor

Convert "it doesn't work" into a minimal, reproducible failure so a tutor can actually help you fix it.

Another use of lab time is to troubleshoot remaining technical issues with guidance from a tutor. Guidance means the tutor directs your thinking; you still do the fixing. That works best when the problem is sharply defined.

A good bug report to a tutor contains:

  • what you expected to happen;
  • what actually happened;
  • the exact steps to reproduce it;
  • the smallest input or code path that triggers it;
  • the full error message or unexpected output;
  • what you have already tried and what it changed.

Reduce before you report

Strip the problem down until removing anything more makes it disappear. That boundary usually reveals the cause, and it saves the tutor from reading your whole project.

Expected: upload succeeds and file appears in list
Actual:   500 error, nothing saved
Steps:    1) login  2) open Upload  3) choose 2MB png  4) submit
Error:    TypeError: cannot read property 'path' of undefined
Tried:    switched to jpg (same error); disabled validation (works)
✅
Exam tip: The reduction skill you use here is exactly the skill a Week 12 technical interviewer probes. Practise it out loud.

Preparing for the Week 12 Technical Interview

The Week 11 session is also your rehearsal room for the Week 12 technical interview, where you must explain your own work without hiding behind the code.

A listed use of lab time is to prepare for the Week 12 technical interview. There you will be expected to explain what you built and why, so the session is a chance to test your explanations on a tutor.

Prepare answers to questions such as:

  • What problem does your project solve, and for whom?
  • What is the overall architecture, and why did you choose it?
  • Which feature are you most proud of, and how does it work?
  • What was the hardest bug, and how did you find its cause?
  • How did you test the system, and what did you not test?
  • What would you change if you started again?
  • How did your team divide the work?

Practise saying the answers in plain language, without reading from the screen. If you cannot explain a part of your own system, that is a gap to close before the interview.

🧠
Memory hook: What · Why · How · Hardest · Tested · Redo. Six angles cover most interview questions.
🤔
Pause & predict: Answer "what was the hardest bug?" out loud in under 60 seconds. Where you hesitate is what to raise in the session.

Asking Better Questions in a Short Session

Time in the session is finite, so question quality — not question quantity — determines what you walk away with.

A help session compresses a lot of demand into a short window. The teams that benefit most ask questions that are specific, owned and bounded.

The anatomy of a good question

  • Context: one sentence on what you are trying to do.
  • Attempt: what you tried and what happened.
  • Ask: the precise decision or explanation you need.
  • Constraint: any rule or requirement you must respect.

Weak: "How do we do the report?" Strong: "Our report has a testing section but no results table. Does the evidence need raw outputs, and where should they go?"

Spread the load

Do not let one person ask everything. Give each team member a topic — implementation, testing, documentation, video — so the team's questions cover the whole submission.

✅
Exam tip: Write the answer down as the tutor speaks, in your own words. A question asked twice is time lost twice.
