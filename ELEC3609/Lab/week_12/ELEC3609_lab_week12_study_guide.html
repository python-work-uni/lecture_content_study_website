The A2 Technical Interview: Presenting, Defending and Walking Through Your System

In Week 12 your group sits a short, table-side oral assessment instead of a normal lab exercise. Tutors interview each group individually about the system you built for Assignment 2 — your architecture, your code, your report and the trade-offs you made — and award part of your final project mark based on how well you can explain and justify your own work.

Read this first: The interview is not a test of whether your app runs, it is a test of whether you understand why it is built the way it is. Prepare a five-minute top-down walkthrough, know where every important function lives in your codebase, and be ready to name the alternative you rejected for each design decision. If you can explain your system to a smart stranger without notes, you are ready.

What the A2 Technical Interview Actually Is

The A2 technical interview is a 10-minute, table-side oral assessment in which tutors test whether you truly understand the system you built — not merely whether it runs.

In Week 12 there is no ordinary lab exercise. Instead, each group participates in a technical interview conducted by the tutors. The subject is your Assignment 2 (A2) implementation: your source code, your system architecture and your development process. This is an interview, not a presentation to the class and not a written test — you talk, the tutors probe, and the mark reflects how convincing your understanding is.

Technical Interview — A short oral assessment where tutors ask questions about your submitted architecture, code, report and trade-offs, and judge your technical understanding and individual contribution.

The purpose is straightforward. In a group project it is possible for a system to work while one member could not explain how. The interview is the assessment mechanism that separates "my group built this" from "I understand this". That is why the mark focuses on technical understanding and individual contributions rather than on feature count.

💡
Why it matters: You are being assessed on the depth of your own understanding. A modest but well-understood system that you can defend beats an impressive system you cannot explain, because the tutors can only mark what you can articulate.

The interview contributes to your final project mark. Treat it as a real assessment worth preparing for, not a formality at the end of semester.

The Four Things Tutors Assess

Every question a tutor asks maps to one of four targets — your project architecture, your code structure, the contents of your report, and the technical challenges and trade-offs you encountered.

The task description names exactly what the interview covers. Learn these four buckets, because they tell you what to prepare and how to organise your answers.

  1. Project architecture and design choices. How is the system structured? Why is it structured that way? What are the main components and how do they communicate?
  2. Code structure and key implementation decisions. Where does the important logic live? What non-obvious implementation choices did you make, and why?
  3. The content in your submitted assignment report. You will be asked about what you wrote. Your report is a document you must stand behind, not one you forget after submission.
  4. Any technical challenges or trade-offs encountered. What was hard? What did you have to give up to get what you got?
TargetExample question you should be able to answer
Architecture & design"Walk me through the main components of your system and how a request flows through them."
Code structure"Where is the logic for X, and why did you put it there?"
Report content"Your report claims Y — justify that claim."
Challenges & trade-offs"What was the hardest bug, and what did you trade away to meet the deadline?"
ℹ️
Note: These four buckets are also the natural structure for your preparation. If you can produce a confident two-minute answer for each, the interview has few surprises left.

Order, Timing and the Shape of the Ten Minutes

Groups are interviewed in ascending group-number order, and each group receives ten minutes split into roughly five minutes of introduction and five minutes of questions.

Two rules govern the timetable.

  • Presentation order: groups are interviewed in ascending group number order — Group 1, then Group 2, then Group 3, and so on. Know where your group sits so you can be ready and attentive when your turn approaches.
  • Time allocation: each group receives 10 minutes total for the interview.

That ten minutes normally breaks down as:

Group 1→Group 2→Group 3→… (ascending order)
  1. ~5 minutes — introduce your architecture and key implementation points. This is your prepared walkthrough. You control the content.
  2. ~5 minutes — respond to questions tailored to your project. The tutors now probe, challenge and dig into specifics.

The first five minutes are the part you can rehearse exactly. The second five are the part you can only prepare for by knowing your system deeply. Because the whole thing is only ten minutes, time discipline is itself a skill: a walkthrough that overruns steals time from the question phase and makes the group look disorganised.

✅
Exam tip: Rehearse your introduction with a stopwatch and aim to finish at about 4:30, not 5:00. Leaving thirty seconds of slack absorbs nerves and interruptions and guarantees you reach your key implementation points.

What to Have Ready Before the Tutor Arrives

Before your turn, your group must have a locally running build, your assignment report, and your source code open and ready to access.

Tutors do not interview you in front of the whole lab. They visit your table and conduct the interview individually, group by group. That means your workspace matters, and nothing destroys momentum faster than fumbling for a laptop that will not boot.

Ensure your group has:

  • A running version of your project, run locally only. Do not depend on an internet-hosted deployment that might be slow or unavailable in the room.
  • Your assignment report open and accessible.
  • Your source code open and accessible — ideally in your editor with the project structure visible.
⚠️
Common trap: "It works on the deployed server but I can't run it locally" is a self-inflicted wound. Test your local run the morning of the lab, on the same machine you will bring, while disconnected from the university network if possible.
🤔
Pause & predict: If the tutor sits down and says "show me the part of the code that handles your toughest feature," how many seconds would it take you to navigate there right now?

Beyond hardware, prepare the human side:

  • Be respectful and quiet while other groups are being interviewed.
  • All group members must attend, and everyone should be ready to answer.
💡
Why it matters: Because tutors move table to table in ascending order, the room is a shared space. A group that is loud during another group's interview disrupts it; a group with nothing open is visibly unprepared before a single question is asked.

Building Your Five-Minute Architecture Walkthrough

A strong walkthrough moves top-down: the system as a whole first, then the few implementation choices that actually mattered.

Five minutes is short. The instinct is to describe everything; the skill is to choose. Structure your introduction so a listener who knows nothing about your project builds an accurate mental model in the first sixty seconds.

A reliable top-down pattern:

  1. One-sentence framing. What problem does the system solve and for whom?
  2. The architecture. Name the main components (for example front end, back end, database, external services) and how a request flows between them.
  3. The key implementation points. Pick the two or three decisions that were genuinely interesting or hard, and explain what you did and why.
  4. A brief nod to challenges. One line on the hardest problem and how you handled it, to tee up the question phase.

Weak walkthrough

  • Starts with a click-by-click demo of the UI.
  • Lists every technology used with no reason.
  • Runs chronologically through "first we did this, then we did that".
  • No mention of why anything was chosen.
  • Overruns and never reaches the hard parts.

Strong walkthrough

  • Starts with a simple diagram or verbal model of the components.
  • Names each technology and the reason it was chosen.
  • Groups the story around architecture, not the calendar.
  • Explicitly flags the two or three key decisions.
  • Finishes early and invites questions.
🧠
Memory hook: Problem → Parts → Path → Points. Say what it is for, name the parts, trace a request through them, then highlight the decisions that mattered.

Surviving the Question Phase

The question phase rewards honest, specific, evidence-backed answers and quietly punishes bluffing.

After your introduction, tutors ask questions tailored to your project. These are not generic viva questions; they follow the materials you submitted and the claims you just made. Two principles carry you through.

Answer specifically, not vaguely. "We used a good design" is worthless. "We separated request handling from business logic so we could unit-test the logic without a database" is an answer.

Point at evidence. Your report and source code are on the table for a reason. When a claim is challenged, open the file, show the function, reference the report section. Grounding an answer in the actual artefact is far more convincing than asserting it from memory.

  • If you know the answer, give it directly, then add the why.
  • If you do not know, say so honestly and reason toward it out loud — "I'm not certain, but I'd expect X because Y; let me check the code."
  • If the question is ambiguous, ask for clarification rather than guessing wrong.
  • If a teammate knows it better, hand over — it demonstrates that the group genuinely collaborated.
⚠️
Common trap: Bluffing. Tutors are experienced engineers and they are asking about code you wrote. A confident-sounding but hollow answer invites a follow-up that exposes it. Honest uncertainty with sound reasoning scores better than fabricated certainty.
🤔
Pause & predict: Think of the three weakest points in your project — the parts you secretly hope nobody asks about. Those are the most likely questions. Write the honest answer to each now, before the interview.

Justifying Design Choices with Trade-offs

Every design decision is a trade-off; a strong answer names the alternative you rejected and why.

"Design choices" and "trade-offs" are explicitly listed assessment targets, and they are the deepest thing a tutor can test. A decision with no alternative is not a decision. So the mature answer shape is:

The trade-off answer
Choice → Alternative considered → Why this one wins for our constraints

For example: "We chose a relational database rather than a document store. A document store would have been faster to set up for our loose data shapes, but our data has strong relationships and we needed joins and referential integrity, so the relational model saved us defensive code later."

Notice the anatomy: a clear choice, a real alternative, and a reason grounded in constraints — time, team skills, requirements, scale, correctness. Constraints are what turn a preference into an engineering justification.

Trade-off — A conscious exchange in which choosing one quality or approach (for example simplicity) costs you another (for example flexibility), and the choice is justified by the project's constraints.

Common axes you can reason along:

  • Simplicity vs flexibility.
  • Speed of development vs robustness.
  • Performance vs clarity.
  • Building it yourself vs using a library or framework.
  • Consistency with the rest of the codebase vs local cleverness.
✅
Exam tip: If a tutor asks "why did you do it this way?" and your honest answer is "it was the first thing that worked," upgrade it: explain what you would do differently now and why. Reflective awareness of the trade-off still earns credit; pretending there was none does not.

Walking Through Code Like an Engineer

Navigate to the exact function that answers the question and narrate the data flow, not the punctuation.

Tutors will ask you to walk through parts of your system using your submitted source code. The skill being tested is navigation and comprehension, not typing speed.

When asked to show some behaviour:

  1. Go straight to the relevant file and function. Practise these jumps beforehand.
  2. Zoom out first: "This module handles Y; the entry point is here."
  3. Narrate the data flow: inputs, the transformation of interest, outputs, and where errors are handled.
  4. Highlight key implementation decisions only where they matter — for example, why a value is validated here rather than there.
  5. Stop when you have answered the question. Do not read the file aloud.

What to do

  • "Here's the entry point; here's where it calls the service."
  • "This function is pure, which is why it's easy to test."
  • "The check lives here because it's the last place we have the raw input."
  • "I'll grep for the handler."

What to avoid

  • Scrolling silently while hunting.
  • Reading every line aloud like a recital.
  • Getting lost in configuration boilerplate.
  • Describing what a library does instead of what your code does.
  • Editing code live to make it look better.
💡
Why it matters: Code structure is one of the four assessment targets. Your ability to find and explain the right piece of code instantly is the evidence that you wrote or genuinely understand it. A familiar codebase reads as ownership; a strange one reads as borrowed work.

Talking About Challenges, Failures and Trade-offs

A well-told failure is stronger evidence of engineering maturity than a claim that nothing went wrong.

The interview explicitly asks about technical challenges and trade-offs encountered. Beginners fear this question; experienced engineers welcome it, because describing a hard problem and your reasoning through it is where real understanding shows.

Use a compact story structure:

Challenge answer
Situation → what you tried → what you changed → what you learned

Worked example: "Concurrent updates were overwriting each other. We first added a lock, which fixed the symptom but hurt responsiveness. We changed to optimistic concurrency with a version field, which matched our actual contention pattern and kept the fast path fast. If we had more time we'd revisit how we surface conflicts to the user."

That answer demonstrates diagnosis, iteration, trade-off awareness and honesty — four things a single "it all worked" cannot show.

⚠️
Common trap: Blaming teammates, tutors, or "the framework" for a problem. Describe challenges as technical problems the group solved together. Assigning blame reads as a collaboration failure and invites questions about individual contribution that you do not want.
🤔
Pause & predict: Pick the hardest bug your group actually fixed. Can you tell its story in four sentences — symptom, first fix, better fix, lesson — without blaming anyone?

Individual Contributions and the All-Hands Rule

All members must attend, and every member should be ready to answer about their own contribution.

The interview focuses on technical understanding and individual contributions. This is deliberate: it lets the tutors verify that the mark reflects each member's work, not just a shared repository.

Practical consequences:

  • Attendance is compulsory for every member. There is no "one person presents" option.
  • Anyone can be asked anything, but expect questions that drill into the components you personally built.
  • Be able to state, specifically, what you owned: which features, which modules, which parts of the report.
  • Be able to explain a teammate's component at a high level too — collaboration is visible when you understand the interfaces between your work and theirs.
✅
Exam tip: Before the lab, have each member write one sentence per component of their own work and one sentence describing how it connects to the rest of the system. That single page becomes each person's answer bank and exposes gaps to fix while there is still time.
ℹ️
Note: "Individual contribution" does not mean you must have written the most code. It means you must be able to explain, justify and defend the parts that were yours, and understand how they fit the whole.

Logistics, Etiquette and Special Circumstances

Small procedural details decide whether your interview starts calm or rushed — and there is a formal way to request a different slot.

The mechanics of the session are simple but worth memorising.

  • Interviews happen in the lab, at your table, one group at a time, in ascending group-number order.
  • Tutors come to you; you do not present to the whole lab.
  • Keep your space ready and your running build, report and source code open.
  • Stay quiet and respectful during other groups' interviews.
  • Everyone attends and is ready to answer.

If your group genuinely needs to be interviewed earlier because of urgent circumstances, you must notify the tutors before the lab begins. Asking on the day, mid-session, is too late and disrupts the schedule.

  1. Before the lab: confirm your slot position in the ascending order and, if needed, request an earlier interview from the tutors in advance.
  2. At the start of the lab: launch your project locally, open the report, open the source tree.
  3. While waiting: stay quiet and listen respectfully to other groups being interviewed.
  4. At your turn: deliver the ~5-minute introduction, then answer questions for the remaining ~5 minutes.
💡
Why it matters: The schedule only works if groups respect the order and the noise level. Pre-arranged exceptions plus quiet waiting are what keep a room of fifteen groups running on time.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Technical Interview
A short oral assessment in which tutors question you about your submitted system to judge your understanding.
A2
Assignment 2, the group software project whose source code and report are the subject of the Week 12 interview.
Architecture
The arrangement of a system's main components and the way they communicate to fulfil requirements.
Design Choice
A deliberate decision about how to build the system, which should be justifiable against alternatives.
Code Structure
How source files, modules and functions are organised and separated within a codebase.
Key Implementation Decision
A non-obvious coding choice that materially shaped how the system behaves or how maintainable it is.
Assignment Report
The written submission documenting your system, which you must be able to explain and defend.
Trade-off
A conscious exchange where choosing one quality costs another, justified by the project's constraints.
Running Build (Local)
A version of the project that starts and works on your own machine without relying on a remote deployment.
Walkthrough
A structured spoken tour of your architecture, code or design reasoning, delivered to the tutor.
Ascending Group Order
The rule that groups are interviewed starting from the lowest group number upward.
Time Allocation
The division of the 10-minute interview into roughly 5 minutes of introduction and 5 minutes of questions.
Question Phase
The second half of the interview, where tutors ask questions tailored to your specific project.
Individual Contribution
The specific work and understanding each group member personally owns and must be able to explain.
Technical Challenge
A difficulty encountered during development, ideally told as symptom, attempted fix, final fix and lesson.
Tutor Panel
The tutors who conduct the interview by visiting each group's table in turn.
Table-side Interview
Conducting the interview at the group's own table rather than in front of the whole lab.
Final Project Mark
The overall grade for the project, to which the technical interview contributes.
Bluffing
Pretending to understand something you do not; in this interview it is reliably exposed and penalised.

Practice Questions

Look away from the page first — retrieval is what builds memory.

Answer, reveal, then tick “Mastered”. Your progress is saved.
Q1

What exactly is the A2 technical interview, and which submission is it based on?

Answer: It is a 10-minute oral assessment conducted by tutors at your table in Week 12, focused on your Assignment 2 implementation. It draws on your submitted Week 11 materials — your source code and your assignment report — and you are expected to explain, justify and walk through parts of your system.
Q2

What four areas do tutors ask questions about?

Answer: (1) Project architecture and design choices, (2) code structure and key implementation decisions, (3) the content of your submitted assignment report, and (4) technical challenges or trade-offs encountered.
Q3

In what order are groups interviewed, and how long does each group get?

Answer: Groups are interviewed in ascending group-number order (Group 1, then Group 2, then Group 3, and so on). Each group is allocated 10 minutes total.
Q4

How is the 10 minutes meant to be spent?

Answer: Approximately 5 minutes introducing your architecture and key implementation points, followed by approximately 5 minutes responding to questions tailored to your project.
Q5

What must your group have ready for the interview?

Answer: A running version of your project (local only), your assignment report ready to access, and your source code ready to access. Additionally, all group members must attend and be ready to answer.
Q6

How does the interview physically run in the room?

Answer: Tutors visit your table and interview your group individually. Groups do not present to the whole lab, and you should be quiet and respectful while other groups are being interviewed.
Q7

What should you do if your group needs an earlier interview slot?

Answer: Notify the tutors before the lab begins. The exception must be arranged in advance rather than raised during the session.
Q8

Give a strong top-down structure for the 5-minute introduction.

Answer: Start with a one-sentence statement of the problem the system solves; name the main architectural components and how a request flows between them; highlight the two or three key implementation decisions and why you made them; close with a brief note on the hardest challenge to set up the question phase.
Q9

Why is "we used the first thing that worked" a weak answer to a design question, and how do you fix it?

Answer: It gives no reasoning and no alternative, so it fails the design-choice and trade-off targets. Fix it by stating the choice, the alternative you considered, and the constraint (time, skills, requirements, correctness, scale) that made your choice win — or by honestly explaining what you would change now and why.
Q10

How should you answer a question you genuinely do not know?

Answer: Do not bluff. Say honestly that you are not certain, reason toward an answer out loud from first principles, then verify against your code or report. Honest reasoning scores better than fabricated confidence.
Q11

Why is it dangerous to blame teammates for a technical challenge?

Answer: It signals a collaboration failure and invites scrutiny of individual contributions. Describe challenges as technical problems the group solved together, using the Situation → tried → changed → learned structure.
Q12

When asked to walk through a piece of code, what is the right approach?

Answer: Navigate directly to the relevant file and function, zoom out to state the module's role, narrate the data flow (inputs, transformation, outputs, error handling), point out the key implementation decisions, and stop once the question is answered rather than reading the file aloud.
Q13

Why does the interview specifically emphasise individual contributions?

Answer: So the mark reflects each member's own understanding and work rather than a shared repository. Every member must attend and be able to explain the components they personally owned and how those components connect to the rest of the system.
Q14

A group has an impressive deployed app but cannot run it locally and cannot find key functions in the repo. Why is this a problem?

Answer: The interview requires a locally running build and expects you to walk through the source code. Without these the group wastes its limited time, cannot ground answers in evidence, and appears not to understand or own its own codebase.
