Defending Your Deployment: The A3 Technical Interview

The A3 technical interview is a 10-minute oral defence of your Assignment 3 deployment and security setup. Two tutors run it in parallel: one interviews your group in ascending group order, while the other walks the room testing your live site. You must explain, justify and demonstrate a working deployment on an AWS Learner Lab EC2 instance, using a terminal session and your configuration files.

Read this first: Treat the interview as a viva for your infrastructure, not a quiz. Every answer should follow the same spine — what you built, why you chose it, and how you can prove it live. If you can trace one request from the browser through HTTPS, Nginx, uWSGI and into Django, and then show the settings and permissions that protect it, you can answer almost any question they ask.

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain exactly what the A3 technical interview assesses and how it is run.
  • Trace a request through the full stack: browser → HTTPS → Nginx → uWSGI → Django.
  • Locate and justify nginx.conf, uwsgi.ini and your Django settings on demand.
  • Describe how HTTPS, reverse proxying and certificate renewal work in this deployment.
  • Explain your authentication, permission and firewall decisions using least privilege.
  • List the Django production settings that remove development-mode risk.
  • Set and defend file permissions for your app files and SSH keys.
  • Diagnose common deployment failures such as 502 Bad Gateway and missing static files.
  • Run a confident live demonstration and prepare a backup instance in case of failure.

What the A3 Technical Interview Is

The interview is an oral defence of the deployment and security work you already did in Assignment 3 — not a new task you build on the day.

Each group participates in a technical interview conducted by the tutors, focusing on your deployment setup and security configurations for Assignment 3. It is a conversation, not a written test, and it happens on your own machine against your own running system.

The tutors will ask questions about four broad areas:

  • Your AWS deployment environment and the configuration decisions behind it.
  • Web server and backend setup, for example Nginx, uWSGI and HTTPS.
  • Your implementation of key security requirements — authentication, permissions, firewall, HTTPS and similar controls.
  • The technical challenges or decisions you encountered while deploying and hardening the system.

Notice the recurring word: decisions. The tutors already assume you can copy a working configuration. What they are testing is whether you understand it, because that is what separates someone who followed a tutorial from someone who can run this platform.

Three verbs — Explain what each component does. Justify why you configured it that way. Demonstrate it working on the live instance.

Format, Order and Timing

Ten minutes, ascending group order, and two tutors working in parallel — one at the desk, one walking the room.

To manage lab time efficiently, interviews and deployment checks run in parallel.

The interview flow

  • Groups are interviewed in ascending group number order (Group 1 → Group 2 → Group 3 …).
  • When your group number is called, bring your laptop to the tutor's desk.
  • Each group has 10 minutes total to answer questions on deployment and security.

The parallel deployment check

  • One tutor conducts interviews at the tutor desk, in ascending group order.
  • At the same time, the second tutor moves around the room to test deployments.
  • The second tutor verifies your website is successfully deployed and functioning by interacting with it on your machine.

Logistics that catch groups out

  • Have your laptop ready and your deployed website accessible before your turn.
  • If your group needs an earlier interview because of urgent circumstances, inform the tutors before the lab begins.
  • Be respectful and quiet while other groups are being interviewed.
⚠️
Common trap: Because the two checks are parallel, your site can be tested while you are still waiting or while you are mid-interview. It must be live and working for the whole session, not just the ten minutes at the desk.
🤔
Pause & predict: If the second tutor opens your site right now, on the laptop in front of you, does it load over HTTPS with no errors? If you are not certain, that is your first preparation task.

How You Are Marked

The interview is part of your final project mark and rewards technical understanding of deployment and security — demonstrated live.

This interview is part of your final project mark and focuses on your technical understanding of deployment and security. That single sentence tells you what earns marks:

  • Understanding over memorisation. You are expected to reason about your own setup, not recite generic commands.
  • Live evidence. You must be prepared to demonstrate the deployment setup and security measures, not just describe them.
  • Coverage. Questions range across the environment, the server stack, the security controls and your problem-solving.
  • Whole-group readiness. All group members must attend and be prepared to answer. There is no hiding behind one strong teammate.

The biggest avoidable mark loss is time wasted hunting for files. Groups that cannot quickly find their configuration files burn minutes of a ten-minute interview before they have answered anything.

✅
Exam tip: Practise until you can open a terminal and pull up each configuration file in under ten seconds. Fluency itself reads as competence, and it buys you time to give richer answers.

The Live Environment: AWS, EC2 and SSH

Everything you defend runs on one group member's AWS Learner Lab EC2 instance, and you reach it through SSH.

During the interview your group must have:

  • A deployed website running in one group member's AWS Learner Lab EC2 instance.
  • Access to your EC2 instance via terminal (SSH).
  • Access to key configuration files such as nginx.conf, uwsgi.ini, and your Django settings.
EC2 — Elastic Compute Cloud — a virtual server in AWS. Your application, web server and database client all live inside it.
SSH — Secure Shell — an encrypted terminal connection used to log into the EC2 instance and run commands remotely.
AWS Learner Lab — The teaching sandbox that gives you temporary AWS credentials, a spending cap and EC2 instances without a real AWS bill.

A typical login looks like this:

# connect to the instance using your private key
ssh -i ~/.ssh/learner.pem ubuntu@<ec2-public-ip>

# once inside, confirm the site's services are running
sudo systemctl status nginx
sudo systemctl status uwsgi
ℹ️
Note: Because the instance is temporary, credentials and public IPs change between sessions. Re-check the address before the interview rather than trusting a bookmark from last week.
🤔
Pause & predict: Without looking anything up, can you write the exact SSH command you would type to reach your instance? If not, that gap will cost you time in a ten-minute interview.

The Deployment Stack, End to End

Four layers cooperate to serve one page: HTTPS, Nginx, uWSGI and Django. Know the path and you can place any question on the map.

The whole point of the setup is to separate concerns: a web server handles public traffic and encryption, an application server runs your Python code, and the framework produces the response.

Browser→HTTPS (443)→Nginx (reverse proxy)→uWSGI socket→Django app→response
LayerRoleWhere it is configured
HTTPS / TLSEncrypts traffic between browser and serverNginx + certificates
NginxWeb server and reverse proxy; terminates TLS, serves static files, forwards dynamic requestsnginx.conf / site config
uWSGIApplication server; translates web requests into Python callsuwsgi.ini
DjangoYour application logic, models, views, auth and permissionsDjango settings.py

Why this shape

A browser cannot talk to Python directly. Nginx speaks HTTP(S) to the world; uWSGI speaks to Python; Django produces the actual page. Splitting the work means each layer can be hardened and scaled independently, and a crash in one is easier to diagnose.

🧠
Memory hook: Nginx is the Negotiator (faces the public), uWSGI is the understudy (runs the code), Django is the director (decides what the page is). HTTPS is the sealed envelope they travel in.

Configuration Files You Must Locate and Explain

You will be asked to open these files live, so know what each one controls and why it is set that way.

The brief names three files explicitly. Be ready to open, read and justify each.

nginx.conf (or the site config under sites-available)

Controls how Nginx listens, terminates TLS and forwards traffic to the app.

  • listen 443 ssl; — accept HTTPS on the standard port.
  • ssl_certificate / ssl_certificate_key — point to your TLS certificate and private key.
  • server_name — the hostname Nginx answers for.
  • location / with proxy_pass / uwsgi_pass — forward dynamic requests to the app server.
  • location /static/ and location /media/ — serve static and uploaded files directly.
  • A second server block on port 80 that redirects HTTP to HTTPS.

uwsgi.ini

Controls how the application server starts and talks to Django.

  • socket or http — the address uWSGI listens on (a Unix socket is typical behind Nginx).
  • chdir — the project directory.
  • module / wsgi-file — the WSGI entry point (for example project.wsgi:application).
  • master, processes, threads — process management.
  • uid / gid — the user uWSGI drops to after binding.

Django settings.py (production values)

Controls the application's runtime behaviour and security. See the dedicated hardening section for the settings that matter most.

✅
Exam tip: Open the files before the interview and put the exact paths on a sticky note or in your notes app. "Many groups waste time here" is a direct warning from the brief.
🤔
Pause & predict: Pick one line from your nginx.conf. Can you say what would break if you deleted it? If not, that is the line the tutor is most likely to ask about.

HTTPS and the Reverse Proxy

Nginx terminates TLS so that encrypted public traffic becomes safe internal traffic to your app.

HTTPS is HTTP carried inside TLS, which encrypts the connection and proves the server's identity. In this stack Nginx is the reverse proxy: it is the single public face of the system, and it forwards requests inward to uWSGI.

What HTTPS gives you

  • Confidentiality — login credentials, session cookies and form data cannot be read in transit.
  • Integrity — responses cannot be silently altered on the way.
  • Authentication — the certificate proves the browser is talking to the right server, not an impostor.

How certificates work here

A certificate is issued for your hostname by a certificate authority. In a teaching deployment this is usually automated with Let's Encrypt and Certbot, which also handles renewal:

sudo certbot --nginx -d your-host.example
sudo certbot renew --dry-run

Why a reverse proxy

  • Single entry point. Only Nginx is exposed; uWSGI can listen on a local socket that the internet cannot reach.
  • TLS offloading. Nginx does the expensive encryption work, keeping Django simple.
  • Static files. Nginx serves CSS, JS and images far faster than Python can.
  • Redirects and headers. HTTP→HTTPS redirects and security headers (such as HSTS) are added in one place.
⚠️
Common trap: If you redirect to HTTPS but never enforce it, or your certificate has expired, the browser shows a warning and the deployment check fails. Verify the padlock, not just the page.
🧠
Memory hook: The reverse proxy is a receptionist: visitors never walk into the back office (uWSGI) themselves; the receptionist checks them in and passes messages through.

uWSGI and the Application Server

uWSGI bridges the web server and your Python code by running the WSGI application Django exposes.

WSGI (Web Server Gateway Interface) is the standard contract between a Python web app and a server. Django provides a WSGI entry point; uWSGI loads it and runs it as a long-lived process.

A representative uwsgi.ini:

[uwsgi]
chdir = /home/ubuntu/myproject
module = myproject.wsgi:application
master = true
processes = 4
socket = /run/uwsgi/myproject.sock
chmod-socket = 660
vacuum = true
uid = www-data
gid = www-data

Key ideas to be able to justify:

  • socket vs http. A Unix socket is faster and is not exposed to the network; Nginx talks to it directly. http opens a TCP port, which must then be protected.
  • master and processes. One master supervises several worker processes so a crash or a slow request does not take the whole site down.
  • uid / gid. After binding, uWSGI drops privileges to an unprivileged user — a least-privilege decision.
  • vacuum. Cleans up the socket file on shutdown, preventing stale-socket errors.
💡
Why it matters: If the socket file has the wrong owner or permissions, Nginx cannot reach uWSGI and users see 502 Bad Gateway. That single symptom ties together the socket, the user and file permissions — a favourite interview probe.
🤔
Pause & predict: Your site returns 502 Bad Gateway. List three things you would check, in order, before touching any code.

Security Requirements: Authentication, Permissions and Firewall

The security section of the interview rewards one principle above all: least privilege.

Tutors ask how you implemented the key security requirements — authentication, permissions, firewall and HTTPS. Frame every answer around least privilege: every user, process and network path should have the minimum access it needs.

Authentication vs authorization

  • Authentication answers who are you? — logging in, verifying credentials, issuing a session or token.
  • Authorization (permissions) answers what may you do? — which records a user can read or change.

Django gives you both: its auth system handles login and password hashing, and its permission model (plus object-level checks in your views) controls access. Explain where in your code each check happens.

Firewall layers

LayerWhat it controlsExample
AWS Security GroupNetwork access to the instanceAllow 22, 80, 443; deny everything else
Host firewallTraffic inside the OSufw allow 'Nginx Full'
Database permissionsWhat the app's DB user may doGrant only needed tables/operations
Least privilege — Give each user, service and network path the smallest set of rights that still lets it do its job.

A strong answer sounds like this

"We expose only 22, 80 and 443 in the Security Group. Nginx terminates TLS; uWSGI listens on a Unix socket that is not reachable from the network. The app runs as www-data, and the database user can only access this application's tables. Debug mode is off and secrets come from the environment."

⚠️
Common trap: Opening SSH (port 22) to 0.0.0.0/0 or exposing the database port is an easy way to lose security marks. Restrict access and be ready to explain why.

Hardening: Django Production Settings

Hardening means removing the conveniences that make development easy and production dangerous.

The brief specifically calls out Django production settings and hardening such as limited database permissions, disabled debug mode, and cookie/JWT protection. Know each setting and the risk it removes.

SettingProduction valueRisk it removes
DEBUGFalseLeaks stack traces, source paths and settings to attackers
ALLOWED_HOSTSYour real hostname(s)Host-header attacks and stray-domain serving
SECRET_KEYLoaded from environment, never committedSession/token forgery if leaked
SESSION_COOKIE_SECURETrueSession cookie sent over plain HTTP
CSRF_COOKIE_SECURETrueCSRF token exposed on HTTP
SECURE_SSL_REDIRECTTrueAccidental plain-HTTP access
SECURE_HSTS_SECONDSNon-zeroDowngrade and cookie-hijacking attacks

Cookies and tokens

Session cookies should be marked Secure (HTTPS only) and ideally HttpOnly (hidden from JavaScript). If you use JWT (JSON Web Tokens) for API authentication, protect them the same way: transmit only over HTTPS, keep lifetimes short, store them where scripts cannot trivially steal them, and validate signatures on every request.

Database permissions

Create a dedicated database user for the application and grant it only the privileges it needs. It should not be able to drop databases or manage other users.

# settings.py (production, values read from the environment)
DEBUG = False
ALLOWED_HOSTS = ["your-host.example"]
SECRET_KEY = os.environ["DJANGO_SECRET_KEY"]
SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE = True
CSRF_COOKIE_SECURE = True
SECURE_SSL_REDIRECT = True
SECURE_HSTS_SECONDS = 31536000
🧠
Memory hook: Hardening checklist — D.A.S.H. Debug off, Allowed hosts set, Secrets from environment, HTTPS-only cookies.

SSH Configuration and File Permissions

Permissions are a security control, not paperwork — wrong bits on a key or socket cause both breaches and outages.

The brief lists SSH configuration and file permissions as a topic you must be familiar with, so expect direct questions.

SSH hardening

  • Use key-based authentication, not passwords.
  • Keep the private key readable only by you: chmod 600 ~/.ssh/learner.pem.
  • Lock down the .ssh directory: chmod 700 ~/.ssh.
  • Disable direct root login and, where practical, password authentication.
chmod 600 ~/.ssh/learner.pem   # private key: owner read/write only
chmod 700 ~/.ssh               # directory: owner only

Application file permissions

  • Application files should be owned by the service user (for example www-data) and not world-writable.
  • Typical modes: directories 755, files 644, the uWSGI socket 660.
  • Never set 777 on anything — it lets any local user modify your code or socket.
sudo chown -R www-data:www-data /home/ubuntu/myproject
sudo find /home/ubuntu/myproject -type d -exec chmod 755 {} \;
sudo find /home/ubuntu/myproject -type f -exec chmod 644 {} \;
💡
Why it matters: File permissions sit at the intersection of security and reliability. A private key with loose permissions is rejected by SSH; a socket with the wrong owner breaks the reverse proxy. Being able to explain both directions shows real understanding.

Technical Challenges and Decisions

The "challenges" question is where you show engineering judgement — name the problem, the cause and the fix.

The brief invites discussion of technical challenges or decisions encountered during deployment and hardening. Prepare two or three real stories in a simple structure.

Use this answer shape

  1. Symptom — what the user or you observed (for example, 502 Bad Gateway).
  2. Diagnosis — how you isolated the cause (check logs, test the socket, inspect permissions).
  3. Cause — the root reason (socket owner mismatch, DEBUG left on, certificate expired).
  4. Fix and lesson — what you changed and what you now do differently.

✅
Exam tip: A challenge you diagnosed and fixed is more impressive than one you avoided. Rehearse it as a short narrative, because interviewers remember stories, not lists.

Preparing as a Group

Preparation is mostly rehearsal: practise the terminal, memorise file locations, and build a backup instance.

The brief gives explicit preparation guidance. Turn each item into a task you complete before the lab.

  • Practise accessing your EC2 instance via the command line until it is muscle memory.
  • Make sure you can locate and explain configuration files quickly — many groups waste time here.
  • If possible, deploy your website on more than one member's AWS Learner Lab environment as a backup in case your primary instance is down.
  • Be familiar with how you implemented:
  • HTTPS, reverse proxy and uWSGI
  • SSH configuration and file permissions
  • Django production settings
  • Security hardening (limited database permissions, disabled debug mode, cookie/JWT protection)

Assign roles, but keep everyone able to answer

All group members must attend and be prepared to answer, so do not silo knowledge. A workable split:

Environment lead

Owns the EC2 instance, Security Groups, SSH access and the backup deployment.

Server lead

Owns Nginx, HTTPS and uWSGI configuration and can trace a request through them.

Application lead

Owns Django production settings, authentication, permissions and hardening.

Each person should still be able to give a short answer on any area, because the tutors will direct questions across the group.

What a Strong Answer Looks Like

Strong answers are short, structured and anchored to something you can show on screen.

Use a simple template for almost any question:

Answer spine
What I built → Why I chose it → How I can prove it live

Worked examples

"How does a request reach your Django app?" "The browser hits Nginx on 443 over TLS. Nginx terminates the certificate and forwards the request over a Unix socket to uWSGI, which calls the Django WSGI application. Django builds the response and it travels back the same way. I can show you the proxy_pass and socket lines that make that path work."

"How did you secure the deployment?" "Least privilege at every layer. The Security Group exposes only 22, 80 and 443; uWSGI listens on a socket the network cannot reach; the app runs as www-data; the database user can only touch this app's tables. In Django, debug is off, ALLOWED_HOSTS is set, the secret key comes from the environment, and cookies are HTTPS-only."

"What was your hardest challenge?" "We hit 502 Bad Gateway. The uWSGI socket was owned by the wrong user, so Nginx could not connect. We checked the logs, found the permission mismatch, fixed the socket owner and mode, and now we verify socket permissions as part of every deploy."

🧠
Memory hook: Show, don't tell. Whenever you can, back the sentence by opening the file or running the command. Evidence ends arguments.

Glossary

Every term you need to speak this week's language.

Technical interview
An oral assessment in which tutors question your group about your deployment and security decisions using your live system.
Deployment
Publishing a working application onto a server so users can reach it over the network.
Security configuration
The set of settings and controls that protect an application: auth, permissions, firewall, HTTPS and hardening.
AWS Learner Lab
The AWS teaching sandbox providing temporary credentials, a spending cap and EC2 instances.
EC2
Elastic Compute Cloud — a virtual server in AWS where your web server, app server and application run.
SSH
Secure Shell — an encrypted terminal connection used to log into and administer the EC2 instance.
Nginx
The web server and reverse proxy that faces the public, terminates TLS and forwards requests inward.
Reverse proxy
A server that accepts public requests on behalf of internal services and forwards them, hiding the backend.
uWSGI
The application server that loads the Django WSGI application and runs it as managed worker processes.
WSGI
Web Server Gateway Interface — the standard contract between a Python web application and a server.
HTTPS
HTTP carried over TLS, providing encryption, integrity and server authentication.
TLS certificate
A signed credential proving a server's identity for a hostname, usually issued by Let's Encrypt and renewed with Certbot.
Firewall
A control that allows or blocks network traffic according to rules, applied at the cloud or host level.
AWS Security Group
A virtual firewall controlling which ports and sources may reach an EC2 instance.
Least privilege
The principle that every user, process and network path gets only the minimum access required.
Authentication
Verifying who a user is, typically by logging in and checking credentials.
Authorization
Deciding what an authenticated user is permitted to do, enforced through permissions.
Django production settings
The configuration values that make Django safe in production, such as DEBUG=False and secure cookies.
DEBUG mode
Django's development mode that exposes stack traces and settings when enabled; must be False in production.
ALLOWED_HOSTS
The list of hostnames Django will serve, protecting against host-header attacks.
SECRET_KEY
Django's cryptographic key for signing sessions and tokens; must be secret and loaded from the environment.
JWT
JSON Web Token — a signed token used for API authentication that must be transmitted over HTTPS and validated per request.
File permissions
The Unix read/write/execute bits governing who may access a file; a key security and reliability control.
502 Bad Gateway
An error returned when Nginx cannot reach the application server, often due to a socket permission or ownership problem.

Practice Questions

Look away from the page first — retrieval is what builds memory.

Answer, reveal, then tick “Mastered”. Your progress is saved.
Q1

What are the three things the interview expects you to do with your deployment, and what do you use to do each?

Answer: Explain it (describe what each component does), justify it (give the reason for each configuration decision), and demonstrate it (show it working live on your EC2 instance using SSH and your configuration files).
Q2

In what order are groups interviewed, how much time does each group get, and where do you go?

Answer: Groups are interviewed in ascending group number order (Group 1, then Group 2, and so on). Each group has 10 minutes total. When your number is called, you bring your laptop to the tutor's desk.
Q3

Describe the parallel deployment check and what it means for your preparation.

Answer: One tutor interviews groups at the desk in ascending order while a second tutor moves around the room and tests each deployment by interacting with the site on the group's machine. Because the check can happen while you are waiting or being interviewed, your site must be live, accessible and working for the whole session, not just your ten minutes.
Q4

Name the three configuration files the brief says you must be able to access, and what each one controls.

Answer: nginx.conf controls the web server and reverse proxy — listening ports, TLS certificates, and forwarding to the app. uwsgi.ini controls the application server — the socket, project directory, WSGI module and process settings. The Django settings file controls application behaviour and security, including DEBUG, ALLOWED_HOSTS and cookie settings.
Q5

Trace a request from the browser to Django in this stack.

Answer: The browser connects to Nginx over HTTPS on port 443. Nginx terminates TLS and forwards the dynamic request over a local socket to uWSGI, which calls the Django WSGI application. Django processes the request and returns a response, which travels back through uWSGI and Nginx to the browser. Static files are typically served directly by Nginx.
Q6

Why is Nginx called a reverse proxy, and what advantages does that give?

Answer: It accepts public requests on behalf of internal services and forwards them inward. Benefits include a single exposed entry point (uWSGI stays on a private socket), TLS offloading so Django does not handle encryption, fast static-file serving, and one place to apply redirects and security headers such as HSTS.
Q7

What is uWSGI's role, and why might you use a Unix socket instead of an HTTP port?

Answer: uWSGI is the application server that loads and runs the Django WSGI application as managed worker processes. A Unix socket is faster than TCP and is not reachable over the network, so only Nginx on the same machine can talk to it — reducing attack surface compared with an exposed HTTP port.
Q8

Give four Django production settings and the risk each one removes.

Answer: DEBUG = False stops stack traces and settings leaking to attackers. ALLOWED_HOSTS restricts which hostnames are served, preventing host-header attacks. SECRET_KEY loaded from the environment keeps the signing key out of source control, preventing session/token forgery. SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE = True (with CSRF_COOKIE_SECURE) ensures cookies are sent only over HTTPS.
Q9

What firewall layers exist in an AWS EC2 deployment, and what should each allow?

Answer: An AWS Security Group controls network access to the instance (allow 22, 80 and 443; deny the rest). A host firewall such as ufw controls traffic inside the operating system (for example, ufw allow 'Nginx Full'). Database permissions form a third layer, granting the app's DB user only the tables and operations it needs.
Q10

How should file permissions be set for your SSH key, the .ssh directory, application files and the uWSGI socket?

Answer: The private key should be 600 (owner read/write only), the .ssh directory 700, application directories 755 and files 644, and the uWSGI socket 660. Nothing should be world-writable (777), because that would let any local user modify your code or hijack the socket.
Q11

Your site returns 502 Bad Gateway. Diagnose it and explain the likely cause.

Answer: 502 means Nginx reached the request but could not get a valid response from the application server. Check that uWSGI is running, confirm the socket path in uwsgi.ini matches the one Nginx forwards to, and verify the socket's owner and permissions. The usual cause is a socket ownership or permission mismatch preventing Nginx from connecting.
Q12

What backup should you prepare in case your primary instance is down?

Answer: Deploy the website on more than one group member's AWS Learner Lab environment, so if the primary EC2 instance fails or is unavailable you can switch to the backup and still demonstrate a working deployment.
Q13

Why must all group members be prepared to answer, and how should you prepare as a team?

Answer: All group members must attend and be prepared to answer, and questions can be directed at anyone, so knowledge cannot be siloed. Assign area leads (environment, server, application) but rehearse until everyone can give a short answer on any area, and run a timed mock interview.
