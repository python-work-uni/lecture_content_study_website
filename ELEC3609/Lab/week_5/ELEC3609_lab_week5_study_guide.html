The A1 Milestone: Pitching Your Project in Five Minutes

In Week 5 each group delivers a five-minute elevator pitch that sells the core concept and innovation of its proposed website project, answers questions from tutors and peers, and submits three milestone artefacts to Canvas.

Read this first: This milestone is not a progress report — it is a pitch. You are being marked on how clearly you communicate the value and goals of your proposed application, especially how your design answers real user needs. Everything below serves that single purpose.

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain exactly what the A1 milestone presentation requires and how long it runs.
  • Structure a five-minute pitch that covers core concept, innovation, merits, and user value.
  • Describe how the milestone is marked and what drives mark deductions.
  • Prepare a script that supports delivery instead of being read aloud.

What the A1 Milestone Actually Is

The A1 milestone is a five-minute elevator pitch of your group's proposed website idea, delivered live and followed by questions.

The milestone sits at the point where your group has chosen a project direction but has not yet built it. You are being asked to convince an audience of tutors and classmates that the idea is worth pursuing. Think of it as the moment a founder stands up and has one shot to make people lean in.

The task has three parts:

  1. The pitch itself — a five-minute spoken presentation introducing the core concept and innovation of your proposed website project.
  2. A brief Q&A — tutors, and possibly other teams, ask questions and offer feedback immediately afterwards.
  3. Canvas submissions — your group contract, your presentation slide, and your MVP list, all due this week.
Elevator pitch — A short, persuasive presentation of an idea that assumes the listener has no prior context and limited patience. It must land the value in seconds, not minutes.
💡
Why it matters: The goal is to impress the audience with the merits and potential of your future web application. You are not demonstrating code; you are selling a vision and the reasoning behind it.
🤔
Pause & predict: Before reading on: if a tutor gave you only thirty seconds, what one sentence would you use to describe your project? Write it down — you will reuse it as the opening of your pitch.

Focus only on the core value of the project — what makes it interesting, unique, and valuable to your target users. Peripheral features, technical scaffolding, and internal team process are not the point here.

The Anatomy of a Five-Minute Pitch

A five-minute pitch is a tight argument with a beginning, a middle, and a close — roughly one minute for the problem, two for the solution, and the rest for proof and ask.

Five minutes is short. Spoken at a comfortable pace it is roughly 600–750 words, so every sentence has to earn its place. A reliable structure is:

  1. Hook and problem (≈45–60s): Name the target user and the concrete pain or unmet need they have today.
  2. Core concept (≈60–90s): Describe what your website is, in plain language, in one or two sentences.
  3. Innovation (≈45–60s): Explain what makes your approach different or better than what exists.
  4. Merits and potential (≈45–60s): Show the value — why users will adopt it, why it can grow, what impact it creates.
  5. Close and ask (≈30s): Restate the value in one line and invite the questions you want.
🧠
Memory hook: Problem → Concept → Difference → Value → Close. If you can say those five words from memory, you can rebuild the pitch anywhere.

What belongs in each beat

BeatAnswers the questionMust include
ProblemWho hurts and why?Target user, concrete need
ConceptWhat is it?One-sentence description
InnovationWhy is it new?Differentiator versus alternatives
ValueWhy does it matter?Benefits, potential, user impact
CloseWhat next?One-line summary, invitation to Q&A
⚠️
Common trap: Groups try to cover every planned feature. Five minutes cannot hold a feature list. Cover the core value and let the Q&A surface the details.

Communicating Value: The Marking Lens

You are assessed on how effectively you communicate the value and goals of your application, particularly how your design addresses the needs of your users.

This is the single most important sentence in the brief, so treat it as your marking rubric. The presentation contributes to your final assignment mark, and it is judged on communication — not on how ambitious or technical the idea is.

Read the emphasis carefully. Two things are being measured:

  • Value and goals — can a listener who has never met you understand what your app is for and why it matters?
  • Design addressing user needs — can you connect specific design decisions back to specific user problems?

Strong pitch behaviour

  • Names a specific user group and their need
  • Links each design choice to that need
  • Uses plain language a non-expert follows instantly
  • States the value in one memorable line
  • Sounds like a conversation, not a recitation

Weak pitch behaviour

  • Describes features with no user behind them
  • Assumes the audience already knows the project
  • Drowns the idea in jargon and acronyms
  • Buries the value at the end (or never states it)
  • Reads word-for-word from a script
✅
Exam tip: Rehearse a single sentence of the form "We help [user] who struggle with [need] by [core concept], which is different because [innovation]." That sentence is the spine of the whole pitch.
🤔
Pause & predict: what evidence or reasoning will you give that your chosen design actually addresses the user need you have named? If you cannot answer in one sentence, refine the idea before the lab.

Presentation Order, Timing, and Readiness

Groups present in ascending group-number order, each get exactly five minutes, and the timer is enforced without exception.

The session runs like a disciplined sequence of pitches:

  • Groups present in ascending group number order — Group 1, then Group 2, then Group 3, and so on.
  • Each group has 5 minutes for the presentation, followed by a short Q&A.
  • There is a strict time limit. Any group will be stopped when time is up; the timer is enforced to ensure fairness across everyone.
⚠️
Common trap: Running over is not a minor slip — you will simply be stopped mid-sentence. A pitch that was never finished cannot communicate its value.

Readiness rules

  • Be ready two groups ahead of your scheduled turn. Prepare while others present, not after your name is called.
  • If your group needs to present earlier due to urgent circumstances, notify the tutors before the lab begins.
  • All group members must attend.
ℹ️
Note: "Two groups ahead" means you should already be set up, slides loaded, and mentally rehearsed by the time the group before yours stands up. Treat it as your cue to move to the front.
🧠
Memory hook: Order is ascending, time is five minutes, and readiness is two groups early — 1 → 2 → 3, 5:00, and minus 2.

Conduct During the Session

The session is a shared event: you owe every other group the same quiet, respectful attention you want for your own pitch.

Expectations during the lab:

  • Be respectful and quiet during other groups' presentations.
  • All group members must attend.
  • Not all members need to speak, but everyone should be ready to answer questions.
  • Absence, or failure to reasonably answer questions, may result in mark penalties.

The last two rules are easy to overlook. You are allowed to divide speaking roles, but you are not allowed to hide behind them. A group member who cannot engage with questions about their own project is a liability to the group's mark.

💡
Why it matters: The Q&A tests whether the whole team understands the project, not just the presenter. Distributing the work is fine; distributing the understanding is not possible.

Practical etiquette

  • Listen actively and take notes on other pitches — they are useful benchmarking.
  • Ask constructive questions of other teams; the brief invites peer feedback.
  • Keep side conversations out of the room while someone is presenting.
  • Have every member ready to field a question, even if they did not speak.

Marks and Penalties: What Costs You

The presentation feeds your final assignment mark, and marks are lost through poor communication, reading from a script, absence, or being unable to answer questions.

Collect the deduction triggers into one place so nothing surprises you:

Delivering Without Reading

A script is allowed, but reading from it aloud significantly reduces your mark — so use it to rehearse, then deliver.

The rule creates a clear two-stage approach: write the script to think, then perform the pitch from understanding.

How to use a script correctly

  1. Write it out fully — this forces you to find the clearest wording for each beat.
  2. Reduce it to keywords — one or two cue words per beat on a card or speaker notes.
  3. Rehearse aloud, repeatedly — at least three full run-throughs timed with a stopwatch.
  4. Practise the transitions — the joins between problem, concept, innovation, and value are where memory fails.
  5. Deliver from the cues — glance at the card, then speak to the audience.
🧠
Memory hook: A script is scaffolding, not the building. Build with it, then remove it.
ℹ️
Note: You are allowed a script and you should prepare one — the penalty is for reading directly from it, not for having it.

Handling the Q&A

After each pitch, tutors and possibly other teams ask questions — this is feedback and an assessed part of your participation.

The Q&A is described in the brief as a chance to receive valuable feedback and ideas for improving your project. Approach it as a conversation rather than an interrogation.

What to expect

  • Questions come from tutors and possibly other teams.
  • The session is brief — answers should be short and direct.
  • Every member should be ready to answer, even non-speakers.
  • Failure to reasonably answer may result in mark penalties.

How to answer well

  • Answer the question asked, in one or two sentences, before adding detail.
  • Pause and think rather than guessing; a considered answer beats a fast wrong one.
  • Share the floor — if a question suits a teammate's area, pass it to them.
  • Take feedback openly — noting a suggestion is a strength, not a weakness.
  • Say "we'll look into that" when you genuinely do not know, then explain your reasoning.
✅
Exam tip: Anticipate three likely questions in advance — usually about the user need, the innovation, and feasibility — and rehearse an answer to each. Most Q&A panics disappear after that.
🤔

Canvas Deliverables Due This Week

Three artefacts must be submitted on Canvas this week for the A1 milestone: the group contract, the presentation slide, and the MVP list.

The presentation is not the only deliverable. The milestone is only complete when the Canvas submission is made.

ArtefactPurpose
Group contractSets out how the team will work together and divide responsibilities
Presentation slideThe visual support for your five-minute pitch
MVP listDefines the minimum viable product — the smallest set of features that delivers the core value
💡
Why it matters: These artefacts are how the milestone is recorded. A brilliant live pitch with a missing Canvas submission leaves the milestone incomplete.
ℹ️
Note: The brief refers to "your presentation slide" in the singular — keep the visual support minimal and high-impact so it reinforces your spoken pitch rather than competing with it.
⚠️
Common trap: The MVP list is not a wish list. It is the minimum that still delivers value — the same discipline your five-minute pitch demands.

A Worked Example: A Model Pitch Outline

Here is what a five-minute pitch actually looks like when it is laid out beat by beat.

Assume a group proposing a community tool for sharing surplus food. The outline below shows the shape — substitute your own project.

Problem (≈50s)

Students near campus throw away food at the end of each week while others nearby skip meals. There is no trusted, local way to connect the two. Name the user: students in shared housing.

Core concept (≈70s)

One sentence: a neighbourhood app where people list surplus food in seconds and neighbours claim it before it spoils. Explain how it works in plain language.

Innovation (≈50s)

Existing options are generic marketplaces or charity drives. Ours is hyper-local, expiry-aware, and trust-verified within a single campus community.

Merits and potential (≈60s)

Less waste, cheaper food, stronger community. Growth path: one campus, then neighbouring suburbs, then other universities.

Close (≈30s)

Restate the value in one line and invite questions about trust, logistics, and scale.

🧠
Memory hook: Five cards, five beats, one value sentence. If you can present your five cards from memory, you are ready.
Hook→Problem→Core concept→Innovation→Value→Close→Q&A

Preparation Workflow

Good pitches are built backwards from the value sentence, then rehearsed forwards until the timing is exact.

Work through these steps in order across the week.

  1. Agree the value sentence. One line: who you help, with what need, and why your approach is different.
  2. Map the five beats. Assign content to problem, concept, innovation, value, and close.
  3. Write the full script. Use it to find the clearest wording; keep the total near 600–750 words.
  4. Build the single slide. Make it reinforce the pitch, not duplicate it.
  5. Finalise the MVP list. Cut features until only the value-delivering minimum remains.
  6. Complete the group contract. Agree roles and how you will work together.
  7. Time a full run-through. If it exceeds five minutes, cut — do not speed up.
  8. Rehearse at least three times aloud. Convert the script into keyword cues.
  9. Plan the Q&A. Prepare answers to the user-need, innovation, and feasibility questions.
  10. Upload all three artefacts to Canvas. Contract, slide, MVP list — before the deadline.
✅
Exam tip: Cut from the middle, never from the close. The value sentence and the invitation to Q&A must always survive a trim.
🤔
Pause & predict: Final check: could a stranger repeat your value sentence after hearing the pitch once? If not, the pitch is not finished.
